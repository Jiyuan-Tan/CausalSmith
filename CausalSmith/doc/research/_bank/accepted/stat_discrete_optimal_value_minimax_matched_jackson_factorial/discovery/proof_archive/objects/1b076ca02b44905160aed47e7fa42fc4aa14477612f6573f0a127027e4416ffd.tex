Write \(L_d=\log(ed)\).  First record a regular submodel used below.  Put all mass on \(X=1\), take \(A\) fair, set \(\mu_{01}=0\), and compare \(\mu_{11}=1/2\) with \(\mu_{11}=1/2+\delta\), where \(\delta=1/(8\sqrt n)\).  Complete both laws as in \(\mathrm{def:dense-moment-matching-construction}\).  They belong to every \(\mathcal P_{d,\epsilon}\), including after padding by null cells, and their target values differ by \(\delta\).  The one-observation chi-square divergence is \(2\delta^2\), so product expansion gives
\[
 \chi^2(P_1^n,P_0^n)=(1+2\delta^2)^n-1\leq e^{1/32}-1.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz implies \(\operatorname{TV}(P_1^n,P_0^n)\leq\tfrac12\sqrt{e^{1/32}-1}<1/4\).  Thresholding any estimator at the midpoint of the two target values and applying Markov's inequality to squared error therefore yields
\[
 \mathfrak R_{n,d,\epsilon}\geq c/n.                                 \tag{30}
\]
This argument covers all measurable estimators and uses neither a positive minimum cell mass nor an interior outcome mean for the fixed arm \(a=0\).

We now splice four exhaustive regimes.  If \(d\geq D_0\) and \(n>d^2\), \(\mathrm{lem:dense-moment-matching-lower}\) gives
\[
 \mathfrak R_{n,d,\epsilon}\geq c\,d/(nL_d).
\]
This ratio is below one in the present regime.

Suppose next that \(d\geq D_0\) and \(d/L_d\leq n\leq d^2\).  Let \(\mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}\) denote the minimax mean-squared risk for estimating \(\|\mathbf P-\mathbf Q\|_1\) from \(n\) independent draws from each of \(\mathbf P,\mathbf Q\in\Delta_d\).  Apply \(\mathrm{lem:jhw-poisson-l1-lower}\) with Poisson mean \(2n\).  Its hypotheses hold with fixed numerical constants: \(2n\geq c d/\log d\) and \(\log(2n)\leq C\log d\).  Given a fixed-\(n\) two-sample estimator, applying it to the first \(n\) observations from each of two independent \(\operatorname{Pois}(2n)\) samples when both totals exceed \(n\), and outputting zero otherwise, shows
\[
 \mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}\geq c\frac d{n\log(2n)}-C e^{-n/4}
 \geq c'\frac d{nL_d},                                                \tag{31}
\]
after enlarging \(D_0\); here \(\log(2n)\leq C L_d\), and the exponential remainder is uniform because \(n\geq d/L_d\).  The exact Markov reduction in \(\mathrm{prop:equal-propensity-l1-reduction}\) transfers (31), with the factor \(1/16\), to the causal risk.

If \(d\geq D_0\) and \(n<d/L_d\), the desired lower scale is one.  For all sufficiently large \(n\), put
\[
 s_n=\lfloor n\log(en)\rfloor.
\]
The inequality \(n<d/L_d\) implies \(d>nL_d>n\log(en)\), since then \(d>n\) and hence \(L_d>\log(en)\).  Thus \(2\leq s_n\leq d\).  Apply \(\mathrm{lem:jhw-poisson-l1-lower}\) at alphabet size \(s_n\) and Poisson mean \(2n\): its two size conditions hold with fixed constants because \(s_n\asymp n\log n\), and its lower bound is a positive numerical constant.  The same fixed/Poisson transfer as in (31), followed by \(\mathrm{prop:equal-propensity-l1-reduction}\) and padding the remaining \(d-s_n\) cells with zeros, yields
\[
 \mathfrak R_{n,d,\epsilon}\geq c.                                  \tag{32}
\]
The finitely many smaller values of \(n\) are also covered by the two-point construction above: choosing the fixed separation \(1/(8\sqrt{n_0})\), where \(n_0\) is the finite cutoff, keeps the same total-variation bound and yields a uniform positive constant.

Finally, if \(2\leq d<D_0\), (30) and boundedness of \(d/L_d\) above and away from zero imply
\[
 \mathfrak R_{n,d,\epsilon}\geq c_\epsilon\min\{1,d/(nL_d)\}.
\]
Equations (30)--(32) and the dense lemma cover every \(n\geq1,d\geq2\).  Decreasing one constant over the finitely many cutoffs proves the theorem.  Every hard law has propensity \(1/2\), so the same construction is valid for each fixed \(0<\epsilon<1/2\); allowing the displayed constant to depend on \(\epsilon\) is therefore harmless.
