For \(r\in\{1,2\}\), let \(\Psi^{(r)}\in\mathbb S_+^p\) be the invariant nuisance in the \(r\)-th fitted BACKSHIFT equation.  The fitting hypotheses give
\[
 D^{(r)}\Sigma_eD^{(r)\mathsf T}
 =\Psi^{(r)}+\operatorname{diag}(s^{(r)}_e),
 \qquad e\in H_r.
 \tag{1}
\]
Because \(H_1,H_2\subseteq E\), \(|E|=m\), and \(|H_r|\ge h=m-c\), inclusion--exclusion gives
\[
 |J|=|H_1\cap H_2|
 =|H_1|+|H_2|-|H_1\cup H_2|
 \ge 2h-m=m-2c=h-c.
 \tag{2}
\]

Set \(Q=D^{(2)}(D^{(1)})^{-1}\).  The noncollinearity hypothesis implies that \(J\) is nonempty, since every set of at most two points in \(\mathbb R^2\) is contained in an affine line.  Fix \(e_0\in J\).  For \(e\in J\), subtract (1) at \(e_0\) from (1) at \(e\) in each explanation and use \(D^{(2)}=QD^{(1)}\).  With
\[
 x_e=s^{(1)}_e-s^{(1)}_{e_0},
 \qquad
 y_e=s^{(2)}_e-s^{(2)}_{e_0},
\]
this gives
\[
 Q\operatorname{diag}(x_e)Q^{\mathsf T}
 =\operatorname{diag}(y_e),
 \qquad e\in J.
 \tag{3}
\]
Let \(W=\operatorname{span}\{x_e:e\in J\}\subseteq\mathbb R^p\).  By linearity of (3),
\[
 Q\operatorname{diag}(w)Q^{\mathsf T}
 \text{ is diagonal for every }w\in W.
 \tag{4}
\]

For each \(k<\ell\), the coordinate projection of \(W\) onto coordinates \(k,\ell\) is all of \(\mathbb R^2\).  Otherwise that projection would have dimension at most one, so there would be a nonzero \((u,v)\in\mathbb R^2\) satisfying
\[
 u(s^{(1)}_{ek}-s^{(1)}_{e_0k})
 +v(s^{(1)}_{e\ell}-s^{(1)}_{e_0\ell})=0
 \quad\text{for every }e\in J.
 \tag{5}
\]
Equation (5) would put the entire \((k,\ell)\)-cloud on the affine line through \((s^{(1)}_{e_0k},s^{(1)}_{e_0\ell})\) with normal \((u,v)\), contradicting the hypothesis.

Choose a basis \(w^1,\ldots,w^d\) of \(W\), where \(d=\dim(W)\), and define the coordinate profiles
\[
 u_k=(w^1_k,\ldots,w^d_k)^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^d,
 \qquad 1\le k\le p.
\]
The projection conclusion says that \(u_k,u_\ell\) are linearly independent whenever \(k\ne\ell\); in particular, each \(u_k\) is nonzero.  Choose \(\beta\in\mathbb R^d\) outside the finitely many proper hyperplanes \(\{\beta:\beta^{\mathsf T}u_k=0\}\).  Having fixed \(\beta\), choose \(\alpha\in\mathbb R^d\) outside the finitely many hyperplanes
\[
 \left\{\alpha:
 \alpha^{\mathsf T}\bigl((\beta^{\mathsf T}u_\ell)u_k
 -(\beta^{\mathsf T}u_k)u_\ell\bigr)=0\right\},
 \qquad k<\ell.
 \tag{6}
\]
Each normal vector in (6) is nonzero: if it vanished, the nonzero scalars \(\beta^{\mathsf T}u_k\) and \(\beta^{\mathsf T}u_\ell\) would make \(u_k,u_\ell\) proportional.  These choices exist because a finite union of proper linear hyperplanes cannot cover \(\mathbb R^d\).  For completeness, the product of their nonzero defining linear forms is a nonzero polynomial; induction on the number of variables, using the one-variable root bound, shows that a nonzero real polynomial cannot vanish everywhere.

Put
\[
 b=\sum_{j=1}^d\beta_jw^j,
 \qquad
 a=\sum_{j=1}^d\alpha_jw^j,
 \qquad
 \rho_k=\frac{a_k}{b_k}.
\]
The choice of \(\beta\) gives \(b_k\ne0\) for every \(k\), and (6) gives \(\rho_k\ne\rho_\ell\) for \(k\ne\ell\).  By (4), both
\[
 G=Q\operatorname{diag}(a)Q^{\mathsf T}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 K=Q\operatorname{diag}(b)Q^{\mathsf T}
\]
are diagonal.  Moreover, \(K\) is invertible because \(Q\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(b)\) are invertible.  Hence
\[
 GK^{-1}
 =Q\operatorname{diag}(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_p)Q^{-1}
 \tag{7}
\]
is diagonal.  Its eigenvalues are the pairwise distinct numbers \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_p\), so every eigenspace of the diagonal matrix on the left of (7) is a coordinate axis.  The columns of \(Q\), which are the corresponding eigenvectors on the right, are therefore supported on coordinate axes.  Invertibility then makes \(Q\) monomial: there are a permutation \(\pi\) and nonzero scalars \(q_i\) such that
\[
 Q_{i,\pi(i)}=q_i,
 \qquad Q_{ij}=0\quad(j\ne\pi(i)).
 \tag{8}
\]

It remains to remove this monomial ambiguity.  By \(\text{def:admissible-matrices}\), both fitted structural matrices have unit diagonal and satisfy the strict cycle-product bound.  From \(D^{(2)}=QD^{(1)}\), (8), and the unit diagonals,
\[
 1=D^{(2)}_{ii}=q_iD^{(1)}_{\pi(i),i}.
 \tag{9}
\]
If \(\pi\) had a nontrivial cycle \(i_1,\ldots,i_r\), where \(r\ge2\) and \(\pi(i_t)=i_{t+1}\) cyclically, (9) would imply
\[
 \prod_{t=1}^r\left|D^{(1)}_{i_{t+1},i_t}\right|
 =\prod_{t=1}^r|q_{i_t}|^{-1}.
 \tag{10}
\]
The entries on the left of (10), up to signs, form a directed simple cycle of \(I_p-D^{(1)}\).  On the other hand,
\[
 D^{(2)}_{i_t,i_{t+1}}
 =q_{i_t}D^{(1)}_{i_{t+1},i_{t+1}}=q_{i_t},
\]
so these entries, again up to signs, form the reverse directed simple cycle of \(I_p-D^{(2)}\).  The two strict cycle-product inequalities in \(\text{def:admissible-matrices}\) would therefore give
\[
 \prod_{t=1}^r|q_{i_t}|^{-1}<1
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \prod_{t=1}^r|q_{i_t}|<1,
\]
whose product is the contradiction \(1<1\).  Thus \(\pi\) is the identity.  Equation (9) and \(D^{(1)}_{ii}=1\) then give \(q_i=1\) for every \(i\).  Hence \(Q=I_p\) and \(D^{(1)}=D^{(2)}\).
