Fix \(0<e<1\) and put \(c=1/e\).  If \(P\ll Q\) and \(r=dP/dQ\), then \(\int r\,dQ=1\).  Consequently, for every \(b\in\mathbb R\), replacing \(f\) by \(f_b(t)=f(t)-b(t-1)\) changes neither \(D_f(P\|Q)\) nor \(B_f(e)\), because
\[
 e(c-1)+(1-e)(0-1)=0. \tag{1}
\]
Convexity also gives the chord inequality
\[
 f(t)\le \left(1-\frac{t}{c}\right)f(0)+\frac{t}{c}f(c),\qquad 0\le t\le c. \tag{2}
\]
If \(Q=eP+(1-e)R\), then \(Q\ge eP\), so \(P\ll Q\) and \(r\le c\), \(Q\)-almost surely.  Integrating (2), using \(\int r\,dQ=1\) and \(1/c=e\), yields
\[
 D_f(P\|Q)\le (1-e)f(0)+ef(c)=B_f(e). \tag{3}
\]
Thus \(\mathfrak L^{\to}_e(P)\subseteq\mathfrak B^{\to}_{f,e}(P)\) for every admissible \(f\).

Suppose \(f\in\mathfrak H_c\), and choose \(b\) so that \(g(t)=f(t)-b(t-1)\) is zero on \([0,c]\) and strictly positive on \((c,\infty)\).  Then \(B_g(e)=0\), and affine invariance gives, for \(Q\in\mathfrak B^{\to}_{f,e}(P)\),
\[
 0\le\int g(r)\,dQ=D_f(P\|Q)\le B_f(e)=0.
\]
The nonnegative integrand therefore vanishes \(Q\)-almost surely, so \(r\le c\).  The measure \(R=(Q-eP)/(1-e)\) has nonnegative \(Q\)-density \((1-er)/(1-e)\) and total mass one.  Thus \(Q\in\mathfrak L^{\to}_e(P)\), and (3) proves universal exactness.

Conversely, assume exactness for every full-support binary \(P\).  Write \(P=\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\), where \(p\in(0,1)\), and \(Q_q=\operatorname{Bernoulli}(q)\).  The boundary law \(Q_{ep}=eP+(1-e)\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0)\) lies in \(\mathfrak L^{\to}_e(P)\), whereas every \(0<q<ep\) violates \(p/q\le c\) and lies outside that class.  Exactness and continuity of
\[
 q\longmapsto qf(p/q)+(1-q)f((1-p)/(1-q))
\]
at \(q=ep\) imply
\[
 epf(c)+(1-ep)f\!\left(\frac{1-p}{1-ep}\right)=B_f(e). \tag{4}
\]
Letting \(p\downarrow0\), continuity and \(f(1)=0\) give \(B_f(e)=0\).

Set \(g(t)=f(t)+f(0)(t-1)\).  Affine invariance, \(B_f(e)=0\), and \(f(1)=0\) give
\[
 g(0)=g(1)=g(c)=0. \tag{5}
\]
Equation (4) then yields \(g((1-p)/(1-ep))=0\).  This argument ranges over every point of \((0,1)\), so continuity gives \(g=0\) on \([0,1]\).  Given \(y\in(1,c)\) and \(x\in(0,1)\), let \(\lambda=(1-x)/(y-x)\).  Define full-support binary \(Q\) with cell masses \(\lambda,1-\lambda\), and define \(P\) by multiplying those masses by \(y,x\), respectively.  Since \(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x=1\), \(P\) is a probability law, and since both ratios are at most \(c\), the cap characterization puts \(Q\) in \(\mathfrak L^{\to}_e(P)\).  Exactness and convexity at \(1=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x\) give
\[
 0=g(1)\le D_g(P\|Q)=\lambda g(y)+(1-\lambda)g(x)=\lambda g(y)\le B_g(e)=0.
\]
Thus \(g(y)=0\), and \(g=0\) on \([0,c]\).

For \(t>c\), write \(c\) as a strict convex combination of \(1\) and \(t\).  Convexity and (5) imply \(g(t)\ge0\).  If \(g(t)=0\), the same two-ratio construction with ratios \(t\) and any \(x\in(0,1)\) has \(D_g=0=B_g(e)\), hence belongs to the divergence ball, but violates the cap because \(t>c\).  This contradicts exactness.  Therefore \(g(t)>0\) for every \(t>c\), which is precisely \(f\in\mathfrak H_c\).  Universal exactness trivially implies binary exactness, so all three conditions are equivalent.  Finally, one-sided bow-mixture completeness identifies the mixture class with the causal SCM class.
