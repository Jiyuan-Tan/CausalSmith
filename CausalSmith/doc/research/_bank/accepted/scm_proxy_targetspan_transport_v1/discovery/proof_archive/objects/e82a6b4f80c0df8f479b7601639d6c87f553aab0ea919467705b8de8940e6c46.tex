We verify the quantitative claim by the same two-point-sequence construction, keeping all support, rank, span, covariance, and limiting conditions explicit.

\paragraph{The fixed regular baseline.}
Let
\[
\mathcal E=\{-1,+1\},\qquad
\mathcal U=\mathcal W=\mathcal Y=\{0,1\},\qquad
\mathcal X=\{x\}.
\]
Vectors over a binary state space are ordered as \((1,0)\); environment columns are ordered as \((+1,-1)\), and the latent columns of the proxy channel are ordered as \((1,0)\). Define \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\) by
\[
\pi=(1/2,1/2),\qquad
S^{(0)}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M^{(0)}=
\begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&3/4\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
a_x(u)=1,\qquad q^{(0)}=(1/2,1/2)^{\top},\qquad
f^{(0)}_{x,1}(u,w)=f^{(0)}_{x,0}(u,w)=1/2.
\]
Every primitive entry is strictly positive, every displayed kernel is stochastic, and
\[
\det M^{(0)}=\frac12\ne0.
\]
Thus the latent-shift factorization, target-mechanism invariance, strict primitive positivity, and proxy-channel injectivity assumptions, together with the definition of \(\mathfrak M_{+}\), give \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\in\mathfrak M_{+}\).

The source factorization gives, after summing over \(u\),
\[
P_{O,0}(e,w,x,y)=\sum_{u}\pi_eS^{(0)}_{ue}M^{(0)}_{wu}a_x(u)f^{(0)}_{x,y}(u,w)=\frac18
\]
for all \(e,w,y\). Target-mechanism invariance similarly gives
\[
b_0=M^{(0)}q^{(0)}=(1/2,1/2)^{\top},
\qquad
\theta_{x,1}(\mathcal M^{(0)})
=\sum_{u,w}q^{(0)}_uM^{(0)}_{wu}f^{(0)}_{x,1}(u,w)=\frac12.
\]
By the definition of the unconditional moments,
\[
H_{x,0}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/4&1/4\\1/4&1/4\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
z_{x,1,0}=(1/4,1/4)^{\top}.
\]
The singular values of \(H_{x,0}\) are \(1/2\) and \(0\); hence it has rank one and \(\sigma_1(H_{x,0})=1/2\ge1/4\). Moreover,
\[
b_0=H_{x,0}(1,1)^{\top}.
\]
Thus the fixed-rank, singular-gap, and target-span conditions in the definition of \(\mathcal R_{1,1/4,1/16}\) hold. Every source observed cell equals \(1/8\), and every target proxy cell equals \(1/2\), so the cell-floor condition holds with \(\eta=1/16\).

It remains to verify the positive-variance condition rather than assume it. Since \(H_{x,0}\) has rank one,
\[
H_{x,0}^{\dagger}=
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D_z\phi_1(H_{x,0},z_{x,1,0},b_0)
=\bigl(H_{x,0}^{\dagger}b_0\bigr)^{\top}=(1,1).
\]
Therefore the contribution of the source sample to the scalar delta-method influence variable is, up to centering, \(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}+\psi(E,W)\) for some deterministic function \(\psi\) coming from the \(H\)-coordinates. Under \(P_{O,0}\), conditional on \((E,W)\), the outcome \(Y\) is fair by the constant baseline response kernel. The elementary conditional-variance decomposition, obtained by expanding the finite sums, yields
\[
\begin{split}
\operatorname{Var}_{P_{O,0}}\!\left(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}+\psi(E,W)\right)
&=\mathbb E_{P_{O,0}}\!\left[\operatorname{Var}_{P_{O,0}}\!\left(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}\mid E,W\right)\right]\\
&\quad+\operatorname{Var}_{P_{O,0}}\!\left(
\mathbb E_{P_{O,0}}[\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}+\psi(E,W)\mid E,W]
\right)\\
&\ge\frac14.
\end{split}
\]
The source allocation factor is \(p^{-1}\), and the independent target contribution is nonnegative. Hence
\[
V_{1,x,1}(\mathcal M^{(0)};p)\ge\frac{1}{4p}>\frac1{16}.
\]
All five defining conditions have now been derived, so
\[
\mathcal M^{(0)}\in\mathcal R_{1,1/4,1/16}.
\]

\paragraph{An admissible weak-rank array with separated causal value.}
For \(n\ge2\), put \(\gamma_n=\delta_n=n^{-2}\). Define \(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n\) using the same \(\pi\) and \(a_x\), and set
\[
S^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+\gamma_n&1/2-\gamma_n\\
1/2-\gamma_n&1/2+\gamma_n
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+\delta_n&1/2-\delta_n\\
1/2-\delta_n&1/2+\delta_n
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
q^{(1)}=(3/4,1/4)^{\top},\qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,1}(1,w)=3/4,qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,1}(0,w)=1/4,qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,0}=1-f^{(1)}_{x,1}.
\]
For \(n\ge2\), all entries of \(S^{(1)}_n\), \(M^{(1)}_n\), \(q^{(1)}\), and \(f^{(1)}\) lie in \([1/4,3/4]\), while \(a_x=1\) and \(\pi_e=1/2\). Moreover,
\[
\det M^{(1)}_n=2\delta_n>0,
\qquad
\det S^{(1)}_n=2\gamma_n>0.
\]
Thus strict primitive positivity and proxy-channel injectivity hold in every row, and the two factorization assumptions and the definition of \(\mathfrak M_{+}\) give \(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n\in\mathfrak M_{+}\).

Because treatment has the single value \(x\), direct summation of the source factorization gives
\[
B^{(1)}_{x,n}=M^{(1)}_nS^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+2\delta_n\gamma_n&1/2-2\delta_n\gamma_n\\
1/2-2\delta_n\gamma_n&1/2+2\delta_n\gamma_n
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
H^{(1)}_{x,n}=\frac12B^{(1)}_{x,n},
\qquad
\det B^{(1)}_{x,n}=4\delta_n\gamma_n>0.
\]
Target-mechanism invariance gives
\[
b^{(1)}_n=M^{(1)}_nq^{(1)}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/2+\delta_n/2\\1/2-\delta_n/2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Since \(B^{(1)}_{x,n}\) is invertible, \(b^{(1)}_n\in\operatorname{col}(B^{(1)}_{x,n})\) for every \(n\). Combining this verified target-span property with the fixed-proportion allocation and the two row-wise independent identically distributed sampling assumptions proves, by the definition of \(\mathcal A_p\), that
\[
\mathbb M^{(1)}=(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n)_{n\ge2}\in\mathcal A_p.
\]
This array genuinely reaches the unrestricted weak-rank portion of the class. Indeed, if \(\lambda_n\) is the unique solution of \(B^{(1)}_{x,n}\lambda_n=b^{(1)}_n\), adding and subtracting its two row equations gives
\[
\lambda_{1,n}+\lambda_{2,n}=1,
\qquad
\lambda_{1,n}-\lambda_{2,n}=\frac{1}{4\gamma_n}.
\]
Thus \(\lVert\lambda_n\rVert_2\to\infty\), and the corresponding unconditional weight norm also diverges because \(P_{O,n}(E=e,X=x)=1/2\).

The target causal value is nevertheless fixed away from the baseline. By target-mechanism invariance and the intervention definition,
\[
\begin{split}
\theta_{x,1,n}(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n)
&=\sum_{u,w}q^{(1)}_uM^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,1}(u,w)\\
&=\frac34\cdot\frac34+\frac14\cdot\frac14
=\frac58
\end{split}
\]
for every \(n\).

\paragraph{Indistinguishability of the two experiments.}
For probability vectors \(P,Q\) on a common finite set, define
\[
\lVert P-Q\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac12\sum_a|P(a)-Q(a)|.
\]
As an auxiliary comparison, retain \(f^{(1)}\), but replace both \(S^{(1)}_n\) and \(M^{(1)}_n\) by the binary all-\(1/2\) kernels \(S^{\star}\) and \(M^{\star}\). Its observed \((E,W,X,Y)\) law is exactly \(P_{O,0}\): \(E\) and \(W\) are independent and fair, and averaging the response probabilities \((3/4,1/4)\) over the fair latent variable makes \(Y\) fair independently of \((E,W)\).

At the full-data level, add and subtract the density with \(S^{\star}\) and \(M^{(1)}_n\):
\[
\begin{split}
&\pi_eS^{(1)}_n(u,e)M^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)
-\pi_eS^{\star}(u,e)M^{\star}(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)\\
&=\pi_e\bigl(S^{(1)}_n(u,e)-S^{\star}(u,e)\bigr)
M^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)\\
&\quad+\pi_eS^{\star}(u,e)
\bigl(M^{(1)}_n(w,u)-M^{\star}(w,u)\bigr)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w).
\end{split}
\]
Summing absolute values, using stochasticity of every unchanged kernel, and dividing by two bounds the full-data total variation by the sum of the two conditional binary-kernel total variations, \(\gamma_n+\delta_n\). Marginalization cannot increase this bound because it only groups summands before taking absolute values. Therefore
\[
\lVert P^{(1)}_{O,n}-P_{O,0}\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}
\le\gamma_n+\delta_n=2n^{-2}.
\]
The displayed target proxy vectors give directly
\[
\lVert b^{(1)}_n-b_0\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}=\delta_n/2.
\]

For arbitrary finite probability vectors, the telescoping identity
\[
\bigotimes_{i=1}^N P_i-
\bigotimes_{i=1}^N Q_i
=\sum_{j=1}^N
\left(\bigotimes_{i<j}P_i\right)\otimes(P_j-Q_j)\otimes
\left(\bigotimes_{i>j}Q_i\right)
\]
and the triangle inequality show that product total variation is at most the sum of coordinatewise total variations, since every probability vector has \(\ell_1\)-norm one. The allocation identity \(n_s(n)+n_t(n)=n\) consequently implies
\[
\begin{split}
&\left\lVert
(P^{(1)}_{O,n})^{\otimes n_s(n)}\otimes(b^{(1)}_n)^{\otimes n_t(n)}
-P_{O,0}^{\otimes n_s(n)}\otimes b_0^{\otimes n_t(n)}
\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\\
&\qquad\le n_s(n)(\gamma_n+\delta_n)+n_t(n)\delta_n/2
\le 2n^{-1}\longrightarrow0.
\end{split}
\]
This is the claimed convergence of the two induced two-sample laws.

\paragraph{Transferred coverage.}
Write
\[
C_n=\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
A_n=\{5/8\in C_n\}.
\]
Measurability of \(C_n\) makes \(A_n\) an event. The assumed uniform-coverage inequality and \(\mathbb M^{(1)}\in\mathcal A_p\) give
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(1)}_n}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(A_n)
\ge1-\alpha.
\]
For every event, the difference of its probabilities under two laws is bounded by their total variation distance, directly from the defining absolute-sum formula. The preceding two-sample total-variation convergence therefore yields
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(A_n)
\ge1-\alpha. \tag{1}
\]

\paragraph{Localization of the fixed-model Wald interval.}
Under \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\), every coordinate of \(\widehat H_{x,n}\), \(\widehat z_{x,1,n}\), and \(\widehat b_n\) is an average of independent binary variables. For an average \(\widehat\mu\) of \(N\) such variables,
\[
\mathbb E[(\widehat\mu-\mu)^2]\le\frac1{4N},
\qquad
\Pr\{|\widehat\mu-\mu|>t\}
\le\frac{1}{4Nt^2},
\]
where the probability inequality follows from
\(\mathbf 1\{|\widehat\mu-\mu|>t\}\le(\widehat\mu-\mu)^2/t^2\).
There are finitely many coordinates, and the allocation assumption implies \(n_s(n)/n\to p\) and \(n_t(n)/n\to1-p\), both positive. A finite union bound thus gives
\[
(\widehat H_{x,n},\widehat z_{x,1,n},\widehat b_n)
-(H_{x,0},z_{x,1,0},b_0)=O_P(n^{-1/2}). \tag{2}
\]

At \(H_{x,0}\), the eigenvalues of \(H_{x,0}H_{x,0}^{\top}\) are \(1/4\) and \(0\). For a real \(2\times2\) matrix \(H\) in a sufficiently small neighborhood, the two eigenvalues are the roots of the quadratic characteristic polynomial and remain separated. On that neighborhood the leading projector is
\[
P_1(H)=\frac{HH^{\top}-\lambda_2(H)I}{\lambda_1(H)-\lambda_2(H)},
\]
so the roots, \(P_1(H)\), the rank-one truncation \(P_1(H)H\), its pseudoinverse, \(\phi_1\), and \(D\phi_1\) are continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives after shrinking the neighborhood if necessary. This verifies rather than assumes the local regularity used below. The plug-in multinomial covariance is a polynomial function of empirical cell frequencies and is bounded on the finite probability simplex. Equation \((2)\), the definitions of the Wald functional, estimator, and totalized plug-in variance, and the mean-value inequality on this neighborhood yield
\[
\widehat\theta^{\,W}_{x,1,n}=\frac12+O_P(n^{-1/2}),
\qquad
\widehat V_n=O_P(1). \tag{3}
\]
Since the standard-normal quantile is finite for \(0<\alpha<1\), the untruncated Wald radius is \(O_P(n^{-1/2})\). Hence the interval \(I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\) is nonempty with probability tending to one. To make the next random variable defined on every sample, set
\[
\Delta_n=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sup_{\zeta\in I^W_{n,1-\alpha}}|\zeta-1/2|,
&I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\ne\varnothing,\\
1,&I^W_{n,1-\alpha}=\varnothing.
\end{cases}
\]
Clipping an interval to \([0,1]\) cannot increase its maximum distance from \(1/2\). Therefore \((3)\) gives
\[
\Delta_n=O_P(n^{-1/2})=o_P(1). \tag{4}
\]

\paragraph{The quantitative adaptation lower bound.}
Fix \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/8)\) and put \(G_n=\{\Delta_n\le\varepsilon\}\). By \((4)\),
\[
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(G_n^c)\longrightarrow0. \tag{5}
\]
On \(A_n\cap G_n\), the definition of \(\Delta_n\) implies that the Wald interval is nonempty. Because \(C_n\) is nonempty by hypothesis and contains \(5/8\) on \(A_n\), the first directed term in the definition of Hausdorff distance gives
\[
\begin{split}
d_H(C_n,I^W_{n,1-\alpha})
&\ge\inf_{\zeta\in I^W_{n,1-\alpha}}|5/8-\zeta|\\
&\ge\frac18-
\sup_{\zeta\in I^W_{n,1-\alpha}}|\zeta-1/2|\\
&=\frac18-\Delta_n
\ge\frac18-\varepsilon. \tag{6}
\end{split}
\]
Consequently, with probabilities evaluated under the fixed baseline two-sample law,
\[
\begin{split}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}
\left\{I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\ne\varnothing,
d_H(C_n,I^W_{n,1-\alpha})\ge\frac18-\varepsilon\right\}
&\ge \mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(A_n\cap G_n)\\
&\ge \mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(A_n)
-\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}(G_n^c).
\end{split}
\]
Taking lower limits and using \((1)\) and \((5)\) proves
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}
\left\{I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\ne\varnothing,
d_H(C_n,I^W_{n,1-\alpha})\ge\frac18-\varepsilon\right\}
\ge1-\alpha. \tag{7}
\]
Equation \((7)\) is stronger than the requested unguarded display and also shows that its lower limit is unchanged by any totalization of \(d_H\) on the event that the Wald interval is empty. Since \(1/8-\varepsilon>0\) and \(1-\alpha>0\), equation \((7)\) implies that \(d_H(C_n,I^W_{n,1-\alpha})\) does not converge to zero in probability at \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\). Therefore \(\sqrt n\,d_H(C_n,I^W_{n,1-\alpha})\xrightarrow{P}0\) is impossible a fortiori.

Finally, uniform coverage alone remains available. For each row of every \(\mathbb M\in\mathcal A_p\), the array target-span assumption gives \(b_n\in\operatorname{col}(B_{x,n})\). Applying the already-established finite-sample projection coverage theorem to \(\mathcal M_n\), \(n_s(n)\), and \(n_t(n)\) gives coverage at least \(1-\alpha\) for the row-indexed concentration projection set. The inequality holds for every row and every admissible array, so taking the infimum over \(\mathcal A_p\) and then the lower limit preserves \(1-\alpha\). Thus unrestricted uniform coverage is compatible with changing rank, but the proved lower bound shows that it cannot be combined with even unscaled Wald localization unless the array class is restricted.
