We give an explicit two-point-sequence obstruction.  This also verifies all support, rank, span, and limiting conditions used below.

Let
\[
\mathcal E=\{-1,+1\},\qquad
\mathcal U=\mathcal W=\mathcal Y=\{0,1\},\qquad
\mathcal X=\{x\}.
\]
Vectors over a binary state space use the order \((1,0)\); the columns of \(S\), \(B_x\), and \(H_x\) use the environment order \((+1,-1)\), while the columns of \(M\) use the latent order \((1,0)\).  Define a baseline model \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\) by
\[
\pi=(1/2,1/2),\qquad
S^{(0)}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M^{(0)}=
\begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&3/4\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
a_x(u)=1,\qquad q^{(0)}=(1/2,1/2)^{\top},\qquad
f^{(0)}_{x,1}(u,w)=f^{(0)}_{x,0}(u,w)=1/2.
\]
Each primitive probability is strictly positive, each displayed kernel is stochastic, and
\(\det M^{(0)}=1/2\ne0\).  Thus the source factorization and target-mechanism invariance assumptions make \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\) a member of \(\mathfrak M_{+}\) on the stated DAG.  Direct marginalization gives, for every \(e,w,y\),
\[
P_{O,0}(e,w,x,y)=1/8,\qquad
b_0=(1/2,1/2)^{\top},\qquad
\theta_{x,1,0}=1/2.
\]
Its unconditional moments are
\[
H_{x,0}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/4&1/4\\1/4&1/4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
z_{x,1,0}=(1/4,1/4)^{\top}.
\]
Therefore \(\operatorname{rank}(H_{x,0})=1\), its singular values are \(1/2\) and \(0\), and \(b_0=H_{x,0}(1,1)^{\top}\).  In particular the fixed-rank, singular-gap, and target-span conditions hold with \(r_0=1\) and \(c=1/4\).  The cell-floor condition holds with \(\eta=1/16\).

For completeness, the positive-variance condition also holds with this value of \(\eta\).  At the baseline point,
\[
H_{x,0}^{\dagger}=
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D_z\phi_1(H_{x,0},z_{x,1,0},b_0)=
(H_{x,0}^{\dagger}b_0)^{\top}=(1,1).
\]
The source part of the scalar delta-method influence function is consequently
\(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}+\psi(E,W)\), up to an irrelevant centering constant, for some function \(\psi\) contributed by the \(H\)-coordinates.  Under \(P_{O,0}\), \(Y\) is an independent fair binary variable conditional on \((E,W)\).  The conditional-variance identity therefore yields
\[
\operatorname{Var}_{P_{O,0}}
\bigl(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}+\psi(E,W)\bigr)
\ge
\mathbb E_{P_{O,0}}\!\left[
\operatorname{Var}_{P_{O,0}}(\mathbf 1\{Y=1\}\mid E,W)
\right]=1/4.
\]
The source allocation multiplies this contribution by \(p^{-1}\), and the target covariance contribution is nonnegative.  Hence
\[
V_{1,x,1}(\mathcal M^{(0)};p)\ge(4p)^{-1}>1/16.
\]
All five defining conditions of \(\mathcal R_{1,1/4,1/16}\) have now been checked, so \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\in\mathcal R_{1,1/4,1/16}\).

We next construct the weak-rank array.  Put \(\delta_n=\epsilon_n=n^{-2}\) and define \(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n\) using the same \(\pi\) and \(a_x\), but with
\[
S^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+\epsilon_n&1/2-\epsilon_n\\
1/2-\epsilon_n&1/2+\epsilon_n
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+\delta_n&1/2-\delta_n\\
1/2-\delta_n&1/2+\delta_n
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
q^{(1)}=(3/4,1/4)^{\top},\qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,1}(1,w)=3/4,\qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,1}(0,w)=1/4,\qquad
f^{(1)}_{x,0}=1-f^{(1)}_{x,1}.
\]
For \(n\ge2\), all entries lie in \([1/4,3/4]\), and
\[
\det M^{(1)}_n=2\delta_n>0,\qquad
\det S^{(1)}_n=2\epsilon_n>0.
\]
Thus each \(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n\) is in \(\mathfrak M_{+}\).  Multiplication gives
\[
B^{(1)}_{x,n}=M^{(1)}_nS^{(1)}_n=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2+2\delta_n\epsilon_n&1/2-2\delta_n\epsilon_n\\
1/2-2\delta_n\epsilon_n&1/2+2\delta_n\epsilon_n
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
H^{(1)}_{x,n}=\tfrac12B^{(1)}_{x,n},\qquad
\det B^{(1)}_{x,n}=4\delta_n\epsilon_n>0,\qquad
b^{(1)}_n=M^{(1)}_nq^{(1)}=
\begin{pmatrix}1/2+\delta_n/2\\1/2-\delta_n/2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because \(B^{(1)}_{x,n}\) is invertible, \(b^{(1)}_n\in\operatorname{col}(B^{(1)}_{x,n})\) in every row.  With samples drawn according to the two row-wise i.i.d. assumptions and the fixed allocation, \(\mathbb M^{(1)}=(\mathcal M^{(1)}_n)_{n\ge2}\) is therefore in \(\mathcal A_p\).  The unique conditional balancing vector obeys
\[
\lambda_{1,n}+\lambda_{2,n}=1,\qquad
\lambda_{1,n}-\lambda_{2,n}=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_n},
\]
so its norm, and also the norm of the corresponding unconditional weight, diverges.  Thus this array uses precisely the unrestricted weak-rank portion of \(\mathcal A_p\), rather than violating target span.

The target causal probability in every alternative row is separated from the baseline by a constant.  Indeed, target-mechanism invariance and intervention on the singleton treatment give
\[
\theta_{x,1,n}
=\sum_{u,w}q^{(1)}_uM^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,1}(u,w)
=\frac34\frac34+\frac14\frac14
=\frac58.
\]

It remains to show that the samples cannot distinguish this separation.  For probability vectors \(P\) and \(Q\) on the same finite set, write
\(\lVert P-Q\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac12\sum_a|P(a)-Q(a)|\).
As an auxiliary comparison law, retain \(f^{(1)}\), but replace both \(S^{(1)}_n\) and \(M^{(1)}_n\) by the all-\(1/2\) binary kernel.  Its observed \((E,W,X,Y)\) marginal is exactly \(P_{O,0}\): averaging \((3/4,1/4)\) against a fair latent variable makes \(Y\) fair, while \(E\) and \(W\) are independent and fair.  Denote the all-\(1/2\) kernels by \(S^\star\) and \(M^\star\).  At the full-data level, adding and subtracting the density that uses \(S^\star\) and \(M^{(1)}_n\) gives
\[
\begin{split}
&\pi_eS^{(1)}_n(u,e)M^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)
-\pi_eS^\star(u,e)M^\star(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)\\
&=\pi_e\bigl(S^{(1)}_n(u,e)-S^\star(u,e)\bigr)
M^{(1)}_n(w,u)f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w)\\
&\quad+\pi_eS^\star(u,e)\bigl(M^{(1)}_n(w,u)-M^\star(w,u)\bigr)
f^{(1)}_{x,y}(u,w).
\end{split}
\]
Summing absolute values, using stochasticity of the unchanged kernels, and dividing by two bounds the full-data total variation by \(\epsilon_n+\delta_n\), because the two binary-kernel distances are respectively \(\epsilon_n\) and \(\delta_n\).  Marginalization cannot increase this absolute-sum bound.  Hence
\[
\left\lVert P^{(1)}_{O,n}-P_{O,0}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}
\le \epsilon_n+\delta_n=2n^{-2}.
\]
Likewise, direct calculation from the displayed target proxy vectors gives
\[
\left\lVert b^{(1)}_n-b_0\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}=\delta_n/2.
\]
For probability vectors, inserting one tensor factor at a time gives the identity
\[
\bigotimes_{i=1}^N P_i-\bigotimes_{i=1}^N Q_i
=\sum_{j=1}^N
\left(\bigotimes_{i<j}P_i\right)\otimes(P_j-Q_j)\otimes
\left(\bigotimes_{i>j}Q_i\right).
\]
Taking \(\ell_1\)-norms and using that every probability vector has \(\ell_1\)-norm one proves that product total variation is at most the sum of the coordinatewise total variations.  Applying this inequality now gives
\[
\begin{split}
&\left\lVert
(P^{(1)}_{O,n})^{\otimes n_s(n)}\otimes(b^{(1)}_n)^{\otimes n_t(n)}
-P_{O,0}^{\otimes n_s(n)}\otimes b_0^{\otimes n_t(n)}
\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\\
&\qquad\le n_s(n)(\epsilon_n+\delta_n)+n_t(n)\delta_n/2
\le 2n^{-1}\longrightarrow0,
\end{split}
\]
where the last inequality uses \(n_s(n)+n_t(n)=n\).  This is the promised asymptotic indistinguishability.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that the requested confidence sets exist.  Apply their uniform coverage property to the single array \(\mathbb M^{(1)}\).  Since the infimum over \(\mathcal A_p\) is no larger than the coverage probability of this array,
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(1)}_n}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}
\{5/8\in\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha}\}
\ge1-\alpha.
\]
Total-variation convergence of the two sample laws then implies
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}
\{5/8\in\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha}\}
\ge1-\alpha>0. \tag{1}
\]

On the other hand, consider the Wald interval under the fixed baseline model.  Every coordinate of \(\widehat H_{x,n}\), \(\widehat z_{x,1,n}\), and \(\widehat b_n\) is an average of binary variables.  If such a coordinate has sample size \(N\), then its centered second moment is at most \(1/(4N)\), and
\[
\Pr\{|\widehat\mu-\mu|>t\}
\le t^{-2}\mathbb E[(\widehat\mu-\mu)^2]
\le(4Nt^2)^{-1},
\]
because \((\widehat\mu-\mu)^2\ge
t^2\mathbf 1\{|\widehat\mu-\mu|>t\}\).  There are finitely many coordinates, and the allocation assumption makes both sample sizes proportional to \(n\).  A finite union bound therefore gives joint \(O_P(n^{-1/2})\) convergence to \((H_{x,0},z_{x,1,0},b_0)\).  At that point the retained singular value is \(1/2\) and the next singular value is zero.  Hence a fixed neighborhood has a strict cutoff gap, and on this neighborhood \(\phi_1\), its derivative, and the plug-in covariance map are continuous and bounded.  The definitions of the Wald estimator and variance consequently yield
\[
\widehat\theta^{\,W}_{x,1,n}=1/2+O_P(n^{-1/2}),\qquad
\widehat V_n=O_P(1).
\]
After intersection with \([0,1]\), it follows that
\[
\Delta_n:=\sup_{\zeta\in I^W_{n,1-\alpha}}|\zeta-1/2|
=O_P(n^{-1/2}). \tag{2}
\]
The asserted regular-branch equivalence applies because \(\mathcal M^{(0)}\in\mathcal R_{1,1/4,1/16}\), and therefore
\[
d_H\!\left(\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha},I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\right)=o_P(1). \tag{3}
\]
The Hausdorff requirement is meaningful only when both random sets are nonempty; in particular it entails this with probability tending to one on the fixed regular branch.  On the event \(5/8\in\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha}\), the definition of Hausdorff distance and the triangle inequality imply
\[
\frac18
\le d_H\!\left(\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha},I^W_{n,1-\alpha}\right)+\Delta_n.
\]
Equations \((2)\) and \((3)\) make the right-hand side converge to zero in probability, whence
\[
\mathbb P_{\mathcal M^{(0)}}^{\,n_s(n),n_t(n)}
\{5/8\in\widehat C^{\mathrm{SAR}}_{x,1,n,1-\alpha}\}\longrightarrow0,
\]
contradicting \((1)\).  Thus no confidence-set construction, studentized or otherwise, can meet the two requested requirements on \(\mathcal A_p\).  The root-\(n\) diameter requirement cannot rescue the construction because the contradiction already follows from the stronger demanded Hausdorff localization.

Finally, the positive part recorded in the statement follows without any asymptotic calibration.  For each row of any \(\mathbb M\in\mathcal A_p\), target span holds by definition.  Applying the finite-sample concentration projection theorem to \(\mathcal M_n\), \(n_s(n)\), and \(n_t(n)\) gives coverage at least \(1-\alpha\) in every row.  Taking the infimum over \(\mathcal A_p\) and then the lower limit proves its uniform-coverage assertion.  The counterexample above explains why that honest projection must be permitted to remain wide near the weak sequence unless the array class is restricted by a separation, bounded-weight, or comparable regularity condition.
