%%% FIELD minimax-lower-bound-proof
We prove the assertion on a finite subclass of \(\mathcal P_4\).  Because the supremum in the theorem is over all of \(\mathcal P_4\), a lower bound on this subclass is also a lower bound on the original experiment.

Fix a nonnegative function \(\varphi\in C_c^\infty((1/4,3/4)^2)\) such that
\[
 \lVert\varphi\rVert_\infty=\varphi(1/2,1/2)=1.
\]
Such a function is obtained, for example, by normalizing the product of two copies of the function
\[
 s\longmapsto
 \begin{cases}
 \exp\!\left\{-[(s-1/4)(3/4-s)]^{-1}\right\},&s\in(1/4,3/4),\\
 0,&s\notin(1/4,3/4).
 \end{cases}
\]
Let \(K_\varphi<\infty\) be large enough to bound the absolute values of all partial derivatives of \(\varphi\) through order four and, via the mean-value theorem, the Euclidean Lipschitz seminorms of its third derivatives.  Choose a fixed \(\rho>0\) satisfying
\[
 \rho\le\frac12,
 \qquad
 \rho K_\varphi\le1.                                      \tag{1}
\]
The choices of \(\varphi\) and \(\rho\) are universal and do not depend on \(M\) or on an unknown law.

Fix a constant \(\gamma>0\), whose value will be reduced below, and, for every sufficiently large \(M\), define
\[
 b:=\left(\frac{\gamma\log M}{M}\right)^{1/12},
 \qquad
 N_M:=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2b}\right\rfloor .             \tag{2}
\]
Then \(b<1/8\), \(N_M\ge2\), and \(N_M\to\infty\).  Since \(\lfloor y\rfloor\ge y/2\) for \(y\ge2\), eventually
\[
 \log N_M
 \ge \log\!\left(\frac{1}{4b}\right)
 =\frac1{12}\{\log M-\log\log M-\log\gamma\}-\log4
 \ge\frac1{13}\log M.                                    \tag{3}
\]

We now construct the finite subclass.  Under \(P_0\), let \(X\) be uniform on \([0,1]\), let \(A\) be independent of \(X\) with \(P_0(A=1)=1/2\), set \(g_0=g_1=1\), and set \(\lambda_0=\lambda_1=1\) on \([0,1]^2\).  Given \(X\), generate \(T(0)\), \(T(1)\), and \(C\) mutually independently and independently of \(A\), give each \(T(a)\) hazard one, and give \(C\), conditionally on either arm and \(X=x\), survival
\[
 P_0(C\ge t\mid A=a,X=x)=(1-t)^2,
 \qquad 0\le t<1.
\]
Extend the event hazards as one after time one and put \(T=T(A)\).  This last equality verifies \(\textnormal{ass:consistency}\); the conditional construction verifies \(\textnormal{ass:treatment-exchangeability}\) and \(\textnormal{ass:independent-censoring}\).  The identities \(f=1\), \(\pi_1=1/2\), \(\lambda_a=1\), and \(g_a=1\) verify, respectively, \(\textnormal{ass:covariate-density-band}\), \(\textnormal{ass:treatment-overlap}\), \(\textnormal{ass:event-hazard-band}\), \(\textnormal{ass:quadratic-censoring-endpoint}\), and \(\textnormal{ass:censoring-residual-envelope}\).  These primitive functions are constant, so they also verify \(\textnormal{ass:propensity-holder}\), \(\textnormal{ass:hazard-holder}\), and \(\textnormal{ass:residual-holder}\).  The function \((1-t)^2\) is a nonincreasing survival tending to zero at one.  Its density is \(2(1-t)\), and the event distributions are continuous, so there is no event or censoring atom before one.  Consequently \(P_0\in\mathcal P_4\) by \(\textnormal{def:quadratic-holder-class}\).

For \(j=1,\ldots,N_M\), put \(x_j:=2(j-1)b\) and define, with \(\varphi\) understood to be zero outside its displayed support,
\[
 B_j(t,x):=\rho b^4
 \varphi\!\left(\frac{1-t}{b},\frac{x-x_j}{b}\right),
 \qquad
 \lambda_{1,j}(t,x):=1+B_j(t,x),
 \qquad
 \lambda_{0,j}(t,x):=1.                                  \tag{4}
\]
Let \(P_j\) be generated exactly as \(P_0\), except that the conditional hazard of \(T(1)\) is \(\lambda_{1,j}\).  The perturbation is supported in
\[
 \mathcal R_j:=
 (1-3b/4,1-b/4)\times(x_j+b/4,x_j+3b/4).                  \tag{5}
\]
The covariate projections of these rectangles are pairwise disjoint because consecutive \(x_j\)'s differ by \(2b\).  Moreover, \(N_M\le(2b)^{-1}\) implies
\[
 x_{N_M}+3b/4=2(N_M-1)b+3b/4\le1-5b/4<1,
\]
so every \(\mathcal R_j\) lies in \([0,1)^2\).

For every multi-index \(\nu\) with \(|\nu|\le3\), the chain rule gives
\[
 \lVert\partial^\nu B_j\rVert_\infty
 \le \rho K_\varphi b^{4-|\nu|}
 \le \rho K_\varphi,                                    \tag{6}
\]
and the mean-value theorem, applied to each third derivative and using the fourth-derivative bound included in \(K_\varphi\), gives
\[
 [\partial^\nu B_j]_{\mathrm{Lip}}
 \le \rho K_\varphi,
 \qquad |\nu|=3.                                         \tag{7}
\]
Because \(\varphi\) has compact support in the open square, its zero extension is smooth; hence (6)--(7) also hold across the edge of \(\mathcal R_j\).  Equations (1), (6), and (7), together with \(1\le1+B_j\le1+\rho\le3/2\), show that \(\lambda_{1,j}\) obeys \(\textnormal{ass:event-hazard-band}\) and \(\textnormal{ass:hazard-holder}\) (indeed its displayed Hölder norm is at most \(2<20\)).  All other primitives retain their \(P_0\) values.  Since \(B_j\) vanishes on a neighborhood of every boundary, extending the event hazard as one after time one introduces neither a discontinuity nor an atom.  Therefore every defining condition remains valid, and
\[
 P_j\in\mathcal P_4,
 \qquad j=1,\ldots,N_M.                                  \tag{8}
\]
At \((t,x)=(1-b/2,x_j+b/2)\), the function \(B_j\) equals \(\rho b^4\), whereas \(B_k\) vanishes there for every \(k\ne j\).  Thus, if
\[
 d(\lambda_j,\lambda_k)
 :=\max_{a\in\{0,1\}}\lVert\lambda_{a,j}-\lambda_{a,k}\rVert_\infty,
\]
then
\[
 d(\lambda_j,\lambda_k)\ge\rho b^4,
 \qquad j\ne k.                                          \tag{9}
\]

It remains to quantify the information in the observed data.  The laws \(P_j\) and \(P_0\) differ only conditionally on \(A=1\).  Given \((A=1,X=x)\), the event part of the observed-data density at \(u\) is \(S_{1,j}(u\mid x)G_1(u\mid x)\lambda_{1,j}(u,x)\), while the censoring part is \(S_{1,j}(u\mid x)\{-dG_1(u\mid x)\}\).  Dividing each part by its baseline counterpart therefore gives, for \(P_j\)-almost every observed unit,
\[
 \log\frac{dP_j}{dP_0}(U,\Delta\mid A=1,X=x)
 =\Delta\log\{1+B_j(U,x)\}-\int_0^U B_j(t,x)\,dt.       \tag{10}
\]
Conditional independent censoring and the hazard construction give
\[
 P_j(U\ge t\mid A=1,X=x)=S_{1,j}(t\mid x)G_1(t\mid x),  \tag{11}
\]
and the event subdistribution has Lebesgue density
\[
 t\longmapsto S_{1,j}(t\mid x)G_1(t\mid x)\{1+B_j(t,x)\}.
\]
Taking the \(P_j\)-expectation of (10), integrating the first term against this event density, and writing the nonnegative second term as
\[
 E_{P_j}\!\left[\int_0^U B_j(t,X)\,dt\,\middle|\,A=1,X=x\right]
 =\int_0^1 S_{1,j}(t\mid x)G_1(t\mid x)B_j(t,x)\,dt
\]
by (11), yields the exact identity
\[
 \begin{split}
 \operatorname{KL}(P_j,P_0)
 ={}&\frac12\int_0^1\!\int_0^1
 S_{1,j}(t\mid x)G_1(t\mid x)\\
 &\quad\times\bigl[\{1+B_j(t,x)\}\log\{1+B_j(t,x)\}-B_j(t,x)\bigr],dt,dx.
 \end{split}                                               \tag{12}
\]
Here the factor \(1/2\) is the probability of treatment, and the integration in \(x\) uses \(f=1\).

For \(0\le y\le1/2\), set \(r(y)=(1+y)\log(1+y)-y\).  Since \(r(0)=r'(0)=0\) and \(r''(y)=(1+y)^{-1}\le1\), two integrations of \(r''\) give
\[
 0\le r(y)\le\frac{y^2}{2}.                              \tag{13}
\]
On \(\mathcal R_j\), (5) gives \(G_1(t\mid x)=(1-t)^2\le b^2\); also \(S_{1,j}\le1\), \(B_j^2\le\rho^2b^8\), and \(\operatorname{Leb}(\mathcal R_j)\le b^2\).  Outside \(\mathcal R_j\), the integrand in (12) is zero.  Hence (12)--(13) imply, for the universal constant \(C_0:=\rho^2/4\),
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(P_j,P_0)\le C_0b^{12}.                 \tag{14}
\]
By the definition of the product experiment \(P_j^{\otimes M}\), its log likelihood ratio is the sum of the \(M\) one-observation log likelihood ratios.  Taking expectation under that product law therefore gives
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(P_j^{\otimes M},P_0^{\otimes M})
 =M\operatorname{KL}(P_j,P_0)
 \le C_0Mb^{12}=C_0\gamma\log M.                          \tag{15}
\]

For completeness, we derive the needed finite-hypothesis testing inequality.  Let \(J\) be uniform on \(\{1,\ldots,N_M\}\), and, conditionally on \(J=j\), let the sample \(Z\) have law \(Q_j:=P_j^{\otimes M}\).  Define \(\overline Q:=N_M^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_M}Q_j\) and \(Q_0:=P_0^{\otimes M}\).  Expansion of the logarithms under the common dominating measure gives
\[
 \frac1{N_M}\sum_{j=1}^{N_M}\operatorname{KL}(Q_j,Q_0)
 =\frac1{N_M}\sum_{j=1}^{N_M}\operatorname{KL}(Q_j,\overline Q)
  +\operatorname{KL}(\overline Q,Q_0).                    \tag{16}
\]
The first term on the right is the mutual information \(I(J;Z)\), and the second is nonnegative.  Equations (15)--(16) imply
\[
 I(J;Z)\le C_0\gamma\log M.                              \tag{17}
\]
For an arbitrary decoder \(\widehat J=\widehat J(Z)\), let \(E=\mathbf 1\{\widehat J\ne J\}\) and \(p_e=P(E=1)\).  Because \(E\) is determined by \((J,Z)\), the entropy chain rule gives
\[
 H(J\mid Z)=H(E\mid Z)+H(J\mid E,Z).
\]
Moreover, \(H(E\mid Z)\le\log2\); on \(\{E=0\}\), \(J=\widehat J(Z)\) is determined by \(Z\); and on \(\{E=1\}\), its conditional entropy is at most \(\log N_M\).  Therefore
\[
 H(J\mid Z)\le\log2+p_e\log N_M.                         \tag{18}
\]
Since \(H(J)=\log N_M=I(J;Z)+H(J\mid Z)\), (18) entails
\[
 p_e\ge1-\frac{I(J;Z)+\log2}{\log N_M}.                  \tag{19}
\]
Choose \(\gamma>0\) small enough that \(13C_0\gamma\le1/8\).  By (3) and (17), \(I(J;Z)\le(1/8)\log N_M\), and, because \(N_M\to\infty\), also \(\log2\le(1/8)\log N_M\) for all sufficiently large \(M\).  Taking the infimum over decoders in (19) thus yields
\[
 \inf_{\widehat J}\frac1{N_M}\sum_{j=1}^{N_M}
 Q_j(\widehat J\ne j)\ge\frac34.                         \tag{20}
\]

Finally, fix any measurable estimator \(\widetilde\lambda=(\widetilde\lambda_0,\widetilde\lambda_1)\).  Decode by choosing the smallest index minimizing \(d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_j)\).  This decoder is measurable because it compares finitely many measurable real-valued losses.  If, under \(Q_j\),
\[
 d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_j)<\frac{\rho b^4}{2},      \tag{21}
\]
then for every \(k\ne j\), the triangle inequality and (9) give
\[
 d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_k)
 \ge d(\lambda_j,\lambda_k)-d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_j)
 >\frac{\rho b^4}{2};
\]
hence \(j\) is the unique minimizer and the decoder is correct.  Taking contrapositives, averaging over \(j\), and invoking (20) gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 \sup_{P\in\mathcal P_4}P^{\otimes M}\!\left\{
 \max_a\lVert\widetilde\lambda_a-\lambda_a\rVert_\infty
 \ge\frac{\rho b^4}{2}\right\}
 &\ge \max_{1\le j\le N_M}Q_j\!\left\{
 d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_j)\ge\frac{\rho b^4}{2}\right\}\\
 &\ge\frac1{N_M}\sum_{j=1}^{N_M}Q_j\!\left\{
 d(\widetilde\lambda,\lambda_j)\ge\frac{\rho b^4}{2}\right\}\\
 &\ge\frac34.                                             \tag{22}
 \end{split}
\]
By (2), \(b^4=\gamma^{1/3}(\log M/M)^{1/3}\).  Taking the infimum over all measurable \(\widetilde\lambda\) in (22) proves the claimed lower bound with
\[
 c_0:=\frac{\rho\gamma^{1/3}}{2},
 \qquad
 c_1:=\frac34.
\]
These constants depend only on universal choices compatible with the displayed primitive-class constants.  The subclass in (8) fixes \(f=1\), \(\pi_1=1/2\), and \(g_a=1\), so every lower-bound instance is a member of the same class \(\mathcal P_4\), notwithstanding that the class permits arbitrary measurable \(f\) within its band.  Finally, \(\textnormal{thm:primitive-endpoint-concentration}\) supplies the matching uniform upper order \((\log M/M)^{1/3}\).  Thus the event-hazard sup-norm rate is minimax sharp over \(\mathcal P_4\); no lower bound for the RMST risk is claimed.
