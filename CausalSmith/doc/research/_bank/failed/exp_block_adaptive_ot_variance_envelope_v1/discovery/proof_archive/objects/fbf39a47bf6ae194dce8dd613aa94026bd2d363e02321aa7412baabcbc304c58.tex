Put \(r=4+\delta_0\), and condition on an arbitrary \(\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\)-history. The counts, fitting values, potential outcomes, and residual lists used below are then fixed. For an arm \(a\), write
\[
X_i=R_{nbi}(a)-\bar R_{nba},\qquad N=n_{nb},\qquad m=m_{nba}.
\]
Jensen's inequality and \(\mathrm{ass:residual\text{-}moments}\) imply
\[
\frac1N\sum_i|X_i|^r
\le2^{r-1}\left\{\frac1N\sum_i|R_{nbi}(a)|^r+|\bar R_{nba}|^r\right\}
\le2^rC_R.
\tag{1}
\]
By \(\mathrm{ass:consistency\text{-}no\text{-}interference}\), the residual observed on a label assigned to arm \(a\) is \(R_{nbi}(a)\). By \(\mathrm{ass:complete\text{-}block\text{-}randomization}\), those labels form a simple random sample without replacement of size \(m\) from the \(N\) labels.

We first derive a finite-population transport bound. Define the population-centered sample and population measures
\[
\nu=\frac1m\sum_{i:Z_{nbi}=a}\delta_{X_i},
\qquad
\mu=\frac1N\sum_i\delta_{X_i}.
\]
For \(T>0\), clip both measures to \([-T,T]\), partition that interval into \(H\) cells of length at most \(2T/H\), and project every cell to one representative. If \(P_h\) and \(\widehat P_h\) are the population and sample masses of cell \(h\), the first- and second-order inclusion probabilities of simple random sampling give
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat P_h\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1})
=\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}P_h(1-P_h)\le\frac1{2m}.
\]
Therefore Cauchy--Schwarz yields
\[
E\left(\sum_{h=1}^H|\widehat P_h-P_h|\middle|\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\right)
\le\frac{H}{\sqrt{2m}}.
\tag{2}
\]
Match projected mass within each cell and move unmatched clipped mass by distance at most \(2T\). The within-cell contribution is at most \(4T^2/H^2\), and the unmatched contribution is at most a constant times \(T^2\sum_h|\widehat P_h-P_h|\). The population clipping cost is bounded using (1) and
\(X_i^2\mathbf1\{|X_i|>T\}\le |X_i|^rT^{-(r-2)}\). The conditional expected sample clipping cost equals the population clipping cost by the first-order inclusion probability. Combining these observations with (1)--(2) and the triangle inequality for \(W_2\) gives a constant \(C\), depending only on \(r\) and \(C_R\), such that
\[
E\{W_2^2(\nu,\mu)\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\}
\le C\left(\frac{T^2}{H^2}+\frac{T^2H}{\sqrt m}+T^{-(r-2)}\right).
\tag{3}
\]
Choose \(H=\lceil m^{1/6}\rceil\) and \(T=m^{1/(3r)}\). Enlarging \(C\) to absorb the ceiling, every term in (3) is at most \(Cm^{-\beta}\), where
\[
\beta=\frac{r-2}{3r}.
\tag{4}
\]

The observable measure \(\widehat\mu_{nba}\) is obtained from \(\nu\) by translation through minus the sample mean \(\bar X_S=m^{-1}\sum_{i:Z_{nbi}=a}X_i\). Because \(N^{-1}\sum_iX_i=0\), the exact simple-random-sampling covariance identity gives
\[
E(\bar X_S^2\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1})
=\left(\frac1m-\frac1N\right)S_{nba}^2\le\frac{C}{m},
\tag{5}
\]
where the last inequality follows from (1). Translation by a scalar has squared quadratic-transport cost equal to the squared scalar. The triangle inequality for \(W_2\), (3)--(5), and \(\beta<1\) therefore imply
\[
E\{W_2^2(\widehat\mu_{nba},\mu_{nba})\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\}
\le Cm_{nba}^{-\beta}.
\tag{6}
\]

For the variance term, write
\[
\overline{X^2}_S=\frac1m\sum_{i:Z_{nbi}=a}X_i^2,
\qquad
\overline{X^2}=\frac1N\sum_iX_i^2.
\]
The same sampling covariance identity applied to the list \((X_i^2)_i\), followed by (1), gives
\[
E\{|\overline{X^2}_S-\overline{X^2}|\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\}
\le\left\{\left(\frac1m-\frac1N\right)
\frac1{N-1}\sum_i(X_i^2-\overline{X^2})^2\right\}^{1/2}
\le Cm^{-1/2}.
\tag{7}
\]
Equations (5), (7), and
\[
\widehat S_{nba}^2=\frac{m}{m-1}(\overline{X^2}_S-\bar X_S^2),
\qquad
S_{nba}^2=\frac{N}{N-1}\overline{X^2}
\]
show, for \(m,N\ge2\), that
\[
E\{|\widehat S_{nba}^2-S_{nba}^2|\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\}
\le Cm_{nba}^{-1/2}.
\tag{8}
\]
Here we also used \(|m/(m-1)-1|\le2/m\), \(|N/(N-1)-1|\le2/N\), and the uniform second-moment consequence of (1). All constants in (1)--(8) are history-uniform.

We next prove continuity of both multi-marginal endpoints. For every arm, take an optimal quadratic coupling of \(\mu_{nba}\) and \(\widehat\mu_{nba}\). Because the measures have finite support, these pairwise couplings admit finite conditional kernels. Starting from any \(\gamma\in\Gamma_{nb}\), draw the empirical-margin coordinates conditionally independently given the corresponding population coordinates. If \(X\) has law \(\gamma\) and \(Y\) is the resulting vector, then the margins of \(Y\) are the empirical margins and
\(E(X_a-Y_a)^2=W_2^2(\mu_{nba},\widehat\mu_{nba})\). By \(\mathrm{ass:contrast\text{-}normalization}\) and Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|E(c^\top X)^2-E(c^\top Y)^2|
&\le\{E(c^\top(X-Y))^2\}^{1/2}
\{E(c^\top(X+Y))^2\}^{1/2}\\
&\le\left\{\sum_aW_2^2(\mu_{nba},\widehat\mu_{nba})\right\}^{1/2}
\left\{2\sum_a(EX_a^2+EY_a^2)\right\}^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\]
Apply (9) first to a population optimizer and then, with the roles reversed, to an empirical optimizer. Existence follows from compactness of the finite coupling polytopes. Conditional Cauchy--Schwarz, (1), (6), and the corresponding uniform expected second-moment bound for the empirical margins yield, for either sign,
\[
E\{|\widehat J_{nb}^{\pm}-J_{nb}^{\pm}|\mid\mathcal F_{n,b-1}\}
\le C\left(\min_a m_{nba}\right)^{-\beta/2}.
\tag{10}
\]
No uniqueness of an optimizer is required.

Let \(t_{nb}=w_{nb}^2/n_{nb}\), so \(\sum_bt_{nb}=s_n^2\). Executed overlap gives \(m_{nba}^{-1}\le(\varepsilon_0n_{nb})^{-1}\), and \((n_{nb}-1)^{-1}\le2n_{nb}^{-1}\) whenever \(n_{nb}\ge2\). Combining (8), (10), and the definitions of the oracle and empirical endpoints, summing absolute block errors, and applying the tower property along the original adaptive filtration gives, whenever all arm counts are at least two,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sup_{\mathbb D\in\mathfrak D}
E_{\mathbb D}\frac{|\widehat L_n-L_n|+|\widehat U_n-U_n|}{s_n^2}
&\le\frac{C}{s_n^2}\sum_bt_{nb}
\left(q_n^{-1/2}+q_n^{-\beta/2}\right)\\
&\le Cq_n^{-\kappa},
\qquad
\kappa=\frac\beta2=\frac{2+\delta_0}{6(4+\delta_0)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]
There is no union bound over blocks or histories in (11). At block \(b\), the predictable counts and fitted values are fixed only at its pre-block history; the conditional bounds above hold uniformly at that history, and the tower property integrates it out.

If some arm count is less than two, \(\min_{b,a}m_{nba}\ge q_n\) implies \(q_n<2\). The truncated loss is at most one, whereas
\(1\le2^\kappa q_n^{-\kappa}\). Thus, after increasing \(C\) by the universal factor \(2^\kappa\), the truncated version of (11) holds for every \(n\), including the total-convention cases. We have therefore proved
\[
\sup_{\mathbb D\in\mathfrak D}E_{\mathbb D}\left[1\wedge
\frac{|\widehat L_n-L_n|+|\widehat U_n-U_n|}{s_n^2}\right]
\le C_{\mathrm{ep}}q_n^{-\kappa}=C_{\mathrm{ep}}r_n,
\tag{12}
\]
where \(C_{\mathrm{ep}}\) depends only on \(K,\varepsilon_0,\delta_0,C_R\).

For a nonnegative random variable \(X\),
\(\mathbf1\{X>\eta\}\le(1\wedge X)/(1\wedge\eta)\). Apply this inequality to the normalized summed endpoint error in (12) to obtain the stated probability bound. The Hausdorff distance between two real intervals is
\(\max\{|\widehat L_n-L_n|,|\widehat U_n-U_n|\}\), which is no larger than the summed endpoint error, proving the last bound with the same constant. Finally, \(q_n\to\infty\) and \(\kappa>0\) imply \(r_n\to0\). Every probability and expectation above is under the original sequential randomization law; only pre-block conditional expectations were used, never conditioning on \(\mathcal F_{n,B_n}\) or freezing final adaptive counts.
