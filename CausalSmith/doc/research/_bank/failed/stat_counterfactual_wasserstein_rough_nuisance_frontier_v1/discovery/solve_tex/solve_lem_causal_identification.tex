Fix \(P\in\mathcal M(d,m,\alpha,\beta,s,L)\), and let \(P_{\mathrm{obs}}\) denote its marginal law on \(\mathcal O\).  By \(\texttt{def:model-class}\), this law satisfies each assumption cited below.  Write \(P_X\) for the marginal law of \(X\).  Because \(\mathcal X\), \(\mathcal Y\), and \(\{0,1\}\) are standard Borel spaces, take regular conditional probabilities in what follows; all conditional equalities need hold only for \(P_X\)-almost every \(x\), which is sufficient for every displayed integral.

Let \(B\subseteq\mathcal Y\) be Borel.  Conditional exchangeability, namely \(Y(1)\perp A\mid X\) from \(\texttt{ass:ignorability}\), gives
\[
 P\{Y(1)\in B,A=1\mid X=x\}
 =P\{Y(1)\in B\mid X=x\}\,P(A=1\mid X=x).
\]
By the definition \(e(x)=P(A=1\mid X=x)\), the left-hand side is also
\[
 e(x)P\{Y(1)\in B\mid A=1,X=x\}.
\]
The lower-overlap inequality in \(\texttt{ass:overlap}\) states that \(e(x)\ge 1/4>0\).  Division by \(e(x)\) is therefore valid and yields
\[
 P\{Y(1)\in B\mid X=x\}
 =P\{Y(1)\in B\mid A=1,X=x\}.
\tag{1}
\]

Consistency in \(\texttt{ass:consistency}\) says \(Y=Y(A)\) almost surely.  Hence \(Y=Y(1)\) almost surely on \(\{A=1\}\).  More explicitly,
\[
 0=P\{Y\ne Y(1),A=1\mid X=x\}
   =e(x)P\{Y\ne Y(1)\mid A=1,X=x\},
\]
and another use of \(e(x)>0\) gives
\[
 P\{Y(1)\in B\mid A=1,X=x\}
 =P(Y\in B\mid A=1,X=x).
\tag{2}
\]
By the definition of \(\eta\) as the conditional density of \(Y\) given \(A=1,X=x\),
\[
 P(Y\in B\mid A=1,X=x)=\int_B\eta(y\mid x)\,dy.
\tag{3}
\]
Combining \((1)\)--\((3)\), applying the law of total probability conditional on \(X\), and then using the known uniform design in \(\texttt{ass:known-uniform-design}\), we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P\{Y(1)\in B\}
 &=\int_{\mathcal X}P\{Y(1)\in B\mid X=x\}\,dP_X(x)\\
 &=\int_{[0,1]^d}\int_B\eta(y\mid x)\,dy\,dx.
 \end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

The conditional density \(\eta\) is nonnegative and jointly measurable.  Thus the nonnegative-integral interchange in \((4)\) gives
\[
 \int_{[0,1]^d}\int_B\eta(y\mid x)\,dy\,dx
 =\int_B\left\{\int_{[0,1]^d}\eta(y\mid x)\,dx\right\}dy
 =\int_B q_1(y)\,dy,
\tag{5}
\]
where the last equality is exactly \(\texttt{def:target-density}\).  The same interchange, \(\texttt{ass:treated-normalization}\), and the fact that \([0,1]^d\) has Lebesgue volume one show
\[
 \int_{\mathcal Y}q_1(y)\,dy
 =\int_{[0,1]^d}\left\{\int_{\mathcal Y}\eta(y\mid x)\,dy\right\}dx
 =\int_{[0,1]^d}1\,dx=1.
\]
Consequently \(q_1\) is a probability density, and \(\texttt{def:target-law}\), \((4)\), and \((5)\) imply, for every Borel \(B\),
\[
 Q_1(B)=\int_Bq_1(y)\,dy=P\{Y(1)\in B\}.
\]
Therefore \(Q_1=\operatorname{Law}_P\{Y(1)\}\).

Finally, this equality is identification rather than a definition of the causal target.  Indeed, define the observed-law functional
\[
 \Psi(P_{\mathrm{obs}})(B)
 :=\int_{[0,1]^d}P_{\mathrm{obs}}(Y\in B\mid A=1,X=x)\,dx.
\]
Uniform overlap ensures that the treated conditional law is determined \(P_X\)-almost everywhere by \(P_{\mathrm{obs}}\); different conditional versions therefore give the same integral.  Equations \((3)\)--\((5)\) show that \(\Psi(P_{\mathrm{obs}})=Q_1\), while equations \((1)\)--\((4)\) show that this observed-law functional equals the causal law \(\operatorname{Law}_P\{Y(1)\}\).  Hence any two members of \(\mathcal M(d,m,\alpha,\beta,s,L)\) with the same observed law have the same marginal law of \(Y(1)\), which proves point identification.
