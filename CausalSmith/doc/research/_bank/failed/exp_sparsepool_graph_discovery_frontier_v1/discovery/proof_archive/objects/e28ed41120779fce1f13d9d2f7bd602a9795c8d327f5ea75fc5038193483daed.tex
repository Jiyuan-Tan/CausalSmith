We prove the noisy and noiseless assertions separately.

First suppose \(\sigma>0\); when \(\sigma=0\), the first displayed lower bound has zero right-hand side and is immediate from \(m\ge0\).  Work in the subclass in which the innovations are independent \(N(0,\sigma^2)\) random variables over rounds and outcome coordinates.  This is a subclass of \(\mathcal M_s\): Gaussian innovations satisfy the conditional moment-generating-function inequality in the noise assumption, and every response surface constructed below satisfies additive interference, has incoming degree at most one, and has every nonzero spillover equal in magnitude to \(\delta\).

Define, for \(j\in\mathcal U\) and \(z\in\mathcal Z_B\),
\[
 a_j(z)=\begin{cases}z_j,&p\le1/2,\\1-z_j,&p>1/2.\end{cases}
\]
By the exact-budget condition and the definitions of \(p\) and \(q\),
\[
 \sum_{j\in\mathcal U}a_j(z)=nq. \tag{1}
\]
Let \(P_0\) have \(\alpha=\tau=0\), \(\Gamma=0\), and the Gaussian innovations just specified.  Thus \(A(P_0)\) is empty and all conditional response means are zero.  For each \(j\), choose a fixed \(i(j)\ne j\).  Define \(P_j\) to agree with \(P_0\) except that
\[
 \mu_{i(j)}(z)=\delta a_j(z). \tag{2}
\]
If \(p\le1/2\), equation (2) is realized by \(\Gamma_{i(j),j}=\delta\) and zero baseline.  If \(p>1/2\), it is realized by \(\alpha_{i(j)}=\delta\) and \(\Gamma_{i(j),j}=-\delta\).  Hence \(P_j\in\mathcal M_s\) and its graph contains exactly the edge \((i(j),j)\).

Write \(Q_j=\Pr_{P_j,d}\).  Under either environment, the joint density of a finite history factors into the same predictable assignment factors \(\pi_t(z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})\) and environment-specific Gaussian outcome factors.  In the likelihood ratio \(dQ_0/dQ_j\), every assignment factor therefore cancels pointwise.  For two Gaussian vectors with common covariance \(\sigma^2I_n\) and mean difference \(u\), direct integration of the log likelihood ratio gives divergence \(\lVert u\rVert_2^2/(2\sigma^2)\).  Iterated expectation over the sequential factorization consequently gives
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(Q_0,Q_j)
 =\frac{\delta^2}{2\sigma^2}
   \mathbb E_{P_0,d}\sum_{t=1}^m a_j(Z_{t\cdot}). \tag{3}
\]
This calculation remains valid for adaptive kernels because the conditional assignment rule, as a function of the realized history, is part of \(d\) and is the same under the two environments.

Summing (3) over \(j\) and using (1) shows that some \(j_*\) satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(Q_0,Q_{j_*})\le \frac{mq\delta^2}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{4}
\]
Let \(E_0=\{g(\mathscr H_m)=A(P_0)\}\).  Uniform graph error at most \(\eta\) implies
\[
 Q_0(E_0)\ge1-\eta,
 \qquad Q_{j_*}(E_0)\le\eta. \tag{5}
\]
Applying the elementary log-sum inequality separately on \(E_0\) and \(E_0^c\) gives
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(Q_0,Q_{j_*})
 \ge \operatorname{kl}(Q_0(E_0),Q_{j_*}(E_0))
 \ge (1-2\eta)\log\!\left(\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\right). \tag{6}
\]
For completeness, the first inequality follows by writing each restricted density as its mass times a conditional density and discarding the two nonnegative conditional divergences.  The second follows because binary divergence is increasing in its first argument and decreasing in its second argument on the region in (5).  Since \(\eta\le1/4\), \(1-2\eta\ge1/2\) and
\(\log((1-\eta)/\eta)\ge\log(3/(4\eta))\ge\frac12\log(1/\eta)\).  Equations (4)--(6) therefore prove
\[
 m\ge c_1\frac{\sigma^2\log(1/\eta)}{q\delta^2} \tag{7}
\]
for a universal \(c_1>0\).

We next obtain the \(\log(n-1)\) term without assuming a nonadaptive design.  Put \(a=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), and choose disjoint \(I,J\subseteq\mathcal U\) with \(|I|=|J|=a\).  For every bijection \(h:I\to J\), define \(P_h\) by
\[
 \mu_i(z)=\delta a_{h(i)}(z)\quad(i\in I),
 \qquad \mu_i(z)=0\quad(i\notin I). \tag{8}
\]
The same baseline-and-spillover construction used after (2) shows that \(P_h\in\mathcal M_s\), and its graph is the directed matching \(\{(i,h(i)):i\in I\}\).  The graphs are distinct as \(h\) varies.  For every legal action,
\[
 \lVert\mu^{(h)}(z)-\mu^{(0)}(z)\rVert_2^2
 =\delta^2\sum_{j\in J}a_j(z)
 \le \delta^2nq, \tag{9}
\]
because a subset contains at most all \(B=nq\) treated units when \(p\le1/2\), and at most all \(n-B=nq\) untreated units when \(p>1/2\).  The sequential likelihood calculation above, now with expectation under \(P_h\), gives
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(\Pr_{P_h,d},Q_0)
 \le\frac{mnq\delta^2}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{10}
\]

Let \(H\) be uniform on the \(M=a!\) bijections, conditionally generate the history from \(P_H\), and let \(\overline Q=M^{-1}\sum_h\Pr_{P_h,d}\).  Expanding the logarithms in the definitions of divergence and mutual information yields the exact identity
\[
 \frac1M\sum_h\operatorname{KL}(\Pr_{P_h,d},Q_0)
 =I(H;\mathscr H_m)+\operatorname{KL}(\overline Q,Q_0). \tag{11}
\]
Thus (10) upper-bounds \(I(H;\mathscr H_m)\).  Conversely, decode \(H\) from the output graph whenever that graph is one of the matching graphs, using an arbitrary value otherwise.  Its error probability is at most \(\eta\).  If \(E\) is its error indicator, then conditioning first on \(E\) gives
\[
 H(H\mid\mathscr H_m)
 \le H(E)+\Pr(E=1)\log(M-1)
 \le\log2+\eta\log M. \tag{12}
\]
The first inequality follows because \(H\) is determined on \(E=0\) and has at most \(M-1\) possible values on \(E=1\).  Hence
\[
 I(H;\mathscr H_m)\ge(1-\eta)\log M-\log2\ge\frac12\log M, \tag{13}
\]
where \(a\ge4\), so \(M=a!\ge24\), and \(\eta\le1/4\).  At least \(\lfloor a/2\rfloor\) factors in \(a!\) are at least \(a/2\), whence
\[
 \log(a!)\ge\lfloor a/2\rfloor\log(a/2)\ge c_2n\log(n-1) \tag{14}
\]
for a universal \(c_2>0\).  To verify the final uniform constant, for \(n\ge16\) use \(a\ge n/3\), \(\lfloor a/2\rfloor\ge a/3\), and compare \(\log(n/6)\) with \(\log(n-1)\); the finitely many integers \(8\le n<16\) have \(a!\ge24\), so decreasing \(c_2\) covers them.  Combining (10), (11), (13), and (14) proves
\[
 m\ge c_3\frac{\sigma^2\log(n-1)}{q\delta^2}. \tag{15}
\]
The maximum of the right-hand sides of (7) and (15) is at least half their sum.  After decreasing a universal constant, this proves the Gaussian-noise assertion.

It remains to prove the noiseless combinatorial assertion.  Here ``identifies uniformly'' means probability-one correctness for every environment in the degree-one noiseless subclass.  Realize every predictable kernel on the finite action set \(\mathcal Z_B\) by inverse transform from a common sequence of independent uniform seeds, and include those seeds in the recorded internal randomization.  Consider the finite family consisting of the empty environment and all one-edge alternatives used in the next paragraph.  Each environment has a probability-one correctness event in the common seed space; their finite intersection has probability one.  Fix a seed in this intersection.  Along the empty environment's all-zero outcome history, the adaptive rule then generates a deterministic legal matrix \(Z\).

Every column of \(Z\) is nonconstant.  Indeed, if column \(j\) is identically zero, choose \(i\ne j\) and set \(\Gamma_{ij}=\delta\); this one-edge alternative has zero response along the empty path.  If column \(j\) is identically one, set \(\alpha_i=-\delta\) and \(\Gamma_{ij}=\delta\); this alternative also has zero response along that path.  Distinct columns must also be distinct: if columns \(j\) and \(k\) agree, take recipient \(k\), set \(\Gamma_{kj}=\delta\) and \(\tau_k=-\delta\), and obtain
\[
 \mu_k(Z_{t\cdot})=\delta Z_{tj}-\delta Z_{tk}=0
 \quad(t=1,\ldots,m). \tag{16}
\]
Every alternative in this finite family has one spillover edge of magnitude \(\delta\).  Because its outcomes agree recursively with the empty environment, the common seed produces the same assignments, complete history, and terminal output.  Correctness for both distinct graphs is impossible, proving the claimed nonconstancy and pairwise distinctness.

The \(n\) column words and the all-zero and all-one words are therefore pairwise distinct.  Thus
\[
 2^m\ge n+2,
 \qquad m\ge\lceil\log_2(n+2)\rceil. \tag{17}
\]
Nonconstancy also makes every column contain at least one one and at least one zero.  Counting entries first by columns and then by rows gives
\[
 mB\ge n,
 \qquad m(n-B)\ge n. \tag{18}
\]
Since \(q=\min\{B/n,(n-B)/n\}\) and \(m\) is an integer, (18) implies \(m\ge\lceil1/q\rceil\).  Combining this with (17) proves the noiseless degree-one assertion.
