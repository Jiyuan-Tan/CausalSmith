\section*{stat\_\allowbreak{}corrupted\_\allowbreak{}ate\_\allowbreak{}overlap\_\allowbreak{}nuisance\_\allowbreak{}frontier}

\noindent\textit{Specialization:} v1\par

\paragraph{TL;DR.} On the exact one-dimensional boundary class with Lipschitz outcome regressions and propensity calibration smoothness at least one, one total finite dyadic cell estimator attains the same-experiment minimax risk \(r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\) for every \(\kappa>0\). The clean term is parametric below \(\kappa=1\), equals \(\{\log(en)/n\}^{1/2}\) at the elbow, and is \(n^{-2/(\kappa+3)}\) above it. The theorem proves that clean minimax functional rate and the contamination modulus are simultaneously attainable on this subfamily; the higher-dimensional or non-Lipschitz smoothness interactions remain open.

\section{Project justification}

\paragraph{Gap.} The clean weak-overlap literature does not provide a same-experiment minimax theorem when an arbitrary Huber contaminant may replace \(X\), \(A\), and \(Y\). In particular, the Ma--Sant'Anna--Sasaki--Ura clean conditions and this exact known-design polynomial-boundary contamination experiment are incomparable, so their asymptotic results and converses do not transfer automatically. No located result gives the all-\(\kappa\) clean elbow together with the full-record contamination radius on this declared class.

\paragraph{Niche.} The missing benchmark is a boundary reconstruction whose variance changes correctly across the integrable, logarithmic, and nonintegrable inverse-overlap regimes while remaining stable when all contaminating mass is placed in its lowest-occupancy slab.

\paragraph{Fill.} The variable-width dyadic estimator has error envelope \(h^2+n^{-1/2}V_\kappa(h)^{1/2}+\epsilon h^{-\kappa}+\exp\{-cnh^{\kappa+3/2}\}\). Optimizing its explicit cutoff yields the complete one-dimensional phase diagram, and exact clean-law pairs plus Huber-neighborhood collisions give matching same-experiment lower bounds.

\section{Related work}

MaSantAnnaSasakiUra2026 is the nearest clean-record comparator: its Section 3 estimator and Appendix B, Proposition B.1 operate under clean i.i.d. sampling, whereas the present benchmark permits arbitrary joint replacement of \(X\), \(A\), and \(Y\). Its weak-overlap conditions and this exact uniform-design polynomial-boundary experiment are incomparable, so no published converse transfers automatically. KhanTamer2010 explains support-tail irregularity but not this controlled boundary class or its contamination phase. ChenGaoRen2016 supplies the Huber-neighborhood testing template, not the scalar ATE estimator that preserves the clean functional rate. RobinsEtAl2017HOEE, LiuMukherjeeNeweyRobins2017EmpiricalHOIF, and BonviniKennedyDukesBalakrishnan2024 motivate the still-open higher-dimensional projected route under clean sampling. HaradaFujisawa2024 treats outcome outliers under stronger overlap, AgarwalSingh2022 assumes structured corruption with bounded propensity, and neither covers arbitrary full-record contamination at a propensity zero. SasakiUra2022ET and SasakiUra2022Robustate motivate trimming and boundary correction; the right-heart-catheterization analysis is a consumer of the theoretical benchmark, not evidence that its records are corrupted or that its parametric propensity belongs to this class.

\section{Assumptions}

\begin{assumption}[ass:uniform-design]\label{ass:uniform-design}
Under \(P\), the covariate \(X\) is exactly uniform on \(\mathcal X=[0,1]^d\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Known uniform random design, \cite{Stone1982}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:polynomial-overlap]\label{ass:polynomial-overlap}
The clean propensity satisfies \(e(x)=x_1^\kappa u(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathcal X\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Novel.} This controlled boundary-selection benchmark represents treatment access that turns on polynomially with a scalar distance-to-access coordinate while allowing smooth heterogeneity through \(u\).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:calibration-envelope]\label{ass:calibration-envelope}
The calibration obeys \(1/4\leq u(x)\leq1/2\) for every \(x\in\mathcal X\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Novel.} This scale normalization keeps interior treatment intensity nondegenerate and contains constant calibrations \(u=1/4\) and \(u=1/2\) together with smooth bounded perturbations.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:calibration-smoothness]\label{ass:calibration-smoothness}
The calibration satisfies \(u\in\mathcal H^\alpha(L)\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Hölder smoothness, \cite{Stone1982}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:outcome-regression-law]\label{ass:outcome-regression-law}
For each \(a\in\{0,1\}\) and every \(x\in\mathcal X\) with \(P(A=a\mid X=x)>0\), \(\mu_a(x)=\mathbb E_P[Y\mid X=x,A=a]\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Conditional-mean version on the positive-probability region, \cite{Stone1982}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:outcome-smoothness]\label{ass:outcome-smoothness}
For each \(a\in\{0,1\}\), the regression satisfies \(\mu_a\in\mathcal H^\beta(L)\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Hölder outcome regression, \cite{Stone1982}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:outcome-envelope]\label{ass:outcome-envelope}
For each \(a\in\{0,1\}\), the regression obeys \(1/4\leq\mu_a(x)\leq3/4\) for every \(x\in\mathcal X\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Interior bounded-regression envelope, \cite{RobinsEtAl2017HOEE}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:consistency]\label{ass:consistency}
The observed outcome satisfies \(Y=AY(1)+(1-A)Y(0)\) under \(P^{\mathrm F}\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Potential-outcome consistency, \cite{RosenbaumRubin1983}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:exchangeability]\label{ass:exchangeability}
The potential-outcome pair satisfies \((Y(0),Y(1))\perp A\mid X\) under \(P^{\mathrm F}\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Conditional exchangeability, \cite{RosenbaumRubin1983}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:huber-mixture]\label{ass:huber-mixture}
The observed law satisfies \(R=(1-\epsilon)P+\epsilon Q\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Huber gross-error contamination, \cite{ChenGaoRen2016}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:iid-sampling]\label{ass:iid-sampling}
The records \(O_1,\ldots,O_n\) are independent with common law \(R\).
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Independent mixture sampling, \cite{ChenGaoRen2016}).
\end{assumption}

T_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\operatorname{clip}_{[-1/2,1/2]}
\left[
\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n}|D|\{\widehat m_1(D)-\widehat m_0(D)\}
+h_n\{\widehat m_1(B_n)-\widehat m_0(B_n)\}
\right].
\]
A single pass assigns records to slabs and cells and accumulates the two arm counts and outcome sums.
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Inputs.} \texttt{O\_\allowbreak{}i}; \texttt{n}; \texttt{epsilon}; \texttt{kappa}.

\section{Main results}

\begin{proposition}[prop:causal-bridge]\label{prop:causal-bridge}
Under the known uniform-design, polynomial-overlap, calibration-envelope, consistency, conditional-exchangeability, outcome-regression-law, and outcome-smoothness assumptions, the causal ATE equals the clean observed-law functional: \(\theta^{\mathrm F}(P^{\mathrm F})=\theta(P)=\int_{[0,1]^d}\{\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)\}\,\mathrm dx\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let \(\lambda_d\) denote Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]^d\).  For
\(a\in\{0,1\}\), put
\[
p_a(x)=P(A=a\mid X=x),
\qquad
m_a(x)=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[Y(a)\mid X=x],
\]
where \(m_a\) is any measurable version.  Such conditional expectations are
finite because \(0\leq Y(a)\leq1\).  Since \(P\) is the
\((X,A,Y)\)-margin of \(P^{\mathrm F}\), the conditional law of the observed
variables used below is the same under these two laws.

First we verify the positivity needed for conditioning.  The polynomial-overlap
and calibration-envelope assumptions, together with \(\kappa>0\) and
\(0\leq x_1\leq1\), give
\[
p_1(x)=e(x)=x_1^\kappa u(x),
\qquad
0\leq e(x)\leq\frac12,
\qquad
e(x)>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x_1>0.
\]
Consequently,
\[
p_0(x)=1-e(x)\geq\frac12>0
\quad\text{for every }x,
\]
whereas \(p_1(x)>0\) away from
\(F=\{x\in[0,1]^d:x_1=0\}\).  The uniform-design assumption gives
\[
P^{\mathrm F}(X\in F)=P(X\in F)=\lambda_d(F)=0.
\]
It follows that \(p_a(X)>0\) almost surely for both treatment levels.

We next connect the observed and potential conditional means.  Conditional
exchangeability implies, for each \(a\),
\[
\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[Y(a)\mid X,A]
=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[Y(a)\mid X]
=m_a(X)
\quad P^{\mathrm F}\text{-almost surely}.
\]
Let \(g_a(x)=\mathbb E_P[Y\mid X=x,A=a]\) on the set where
\(p_a(x)>0\).  By the defining property of conditional expectation,
consistency, iterated conditioning, and conditional exchangeability, in that
order,
\[
\begin{aligned}
p_a(X)g_a(X)
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[\mathbf 1\{A=a\}Y\mid X]\\
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[\mathbf 1\{A=a\}Y(a)\mid X]\\
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}\!\left[
  \mathbf 1\{A=a\}
  \mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[Y(a)\mid X,A]
  \mathrel{\big|}X\right]\\
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[\mathbf 1\{A=a\}m_a(X)\mid X]\\
&=p_a(X)m_a(X)
\quad P^{\mathrm F}\text{-almost surely}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because \(p_a(X)>0\) almost surely, division is valid off a null set and yields
\(g_a(X)=m_a(X)\) almost surely.  The outcome-regression-law assumption says
that \(g_a(x)=\mu_a(x)\) whenever \(p_a(x)>0\).  Hence
\[
m_a(X)=\mu_a(X)
\quad P^{\mathrm F}\text{-almost surely},
\qquad a\in\{0,1\}.
\]
For \(a=1\), no equality at the nonidentified face \(F\) was used.  Moreover,
the outcome-smoothness assumption with \(\beta>0\) makes \(\mu_1\) continuous,
and the set \(\{x:x_1>0\}\) is dense in \([0,1]^d\); thus its boundary value,
if mentioned, is uniquely fixed by continuous extension.  In any event,
\(\lambda_d(F)=0\), so that value cannot affect the integral below.  The same
smoothness assumption also makes each \(\mu_a\) bounded and Borel measurable on
the compact cube, so all displayed integrals are finite.

Finally, boundedness of the potential outcomes permits linearity and the tower
property.  Using successively the definition of the causal ATE, the tower
property, the almost-sure equalities just proved, and the uniform-design
assumption, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\theta^{\mathrm F}(P^{\mathrm F})
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[Y(1)-Y(0)]\\
&=\mathbb E_{P^{\mathrm F}}[m_1(X)-m_0(X)]\\
&=\mathbb E_P[\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)]\\
&=\int_{[0,1]^d}\{\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)\}\,\mathrm dx\\
&=\theta(P),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the final equality is the definition of the clean observed-law
functional.  This proves the claimed bridge without asserting that the clean
law, or \(\theta(P)\), is point identified from the contaminated law \(R\).
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This standard supporting result separates causal identification under the clean law from the contaminated-sampling decision problem. \textit{Gap.} RosenbaumRubin1983 supplies the standard identification bridge, and the known uniform design converts the covariate expectation to the displayed Lebesgue integral; no novelty is claimed for this proposition. \textit{Consumer.} The proposition lets applied ATE users interpret the statistical radius without asserting point identification of the clean law from \(R\).

\begin{theorem}[thm:bernoulli-reduction]\label{thm:bernoulli-reduction}
For every \(n\geq2\) and \(0\leq\epsilon\leq1/10\), applying \(\mathsf{Ber}\) to the bounded-outcome Huber experiment preserves \(\theta(P)\), maps every admissible \(Q\) to an admissible binary-response contaminant, and leaves the minimax absolute risk \(\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)\) unchanged after finite derandomization.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(\mathcal E_{[0,1]}\) denote the experiment in the statement and let \(\mathcal E_{\{0,1\}}\) be its subexperiment in which the response is binary.  We first check that the kernel in \(\texttt{def:bernoulli-reduction}\) maps the former experiment into the latter.  For a clean law \(P\), conditional iterated expectation and \(\texttt{ass:outcome-regression-law}\) give, at every point at which the conditional mean is defined,
\[
 \mathbb P_P(Z=1\mid X=x,A=a)
 =\mathbb E_P\{\mathbb P(Z=1\mid O)\mid X=x,A=a\}
 =\mathbb E_P(Y\mid X=x,A=a)=\mu_a(x).
\]
Thus the transformed clean law has the same uniform covariate law, propensity \(e\), and regressions \(\mu_a\).  In particular, it is in the same clean class by \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\), and its identifying functional is the same \(\theta(P)\).  The value at \(x_1=0\) remains the prescribed continuous extension and is not inferred from a null conditional event.  If \(Q\) is any contaminating law, its image \(Q\mathsf{Ber}\) is a probability law on the binary-response subset of \(\mathcal O\), hence is admissible in \(\texttt{def:contaminated-class}\).  Linearity of a Markov kernel and \(\texttt{ass:huber-mixture}\) yield
\[
 R\mathsf{Ber}=(1-\epsilon)P\mathsf{Ber}+\epsilon Q\mathsf{Ber}.
\]
Applying the kernel independently to the independent records preserves the product sampling structure in \(\texttt{ass:iid-sampling}\).

It remains to prove equality, rather than merely monotonicity, of the two minimax risks.  Write \(\mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}\) for the minimax value over \(\mathcal E_{\{0,1\}}\).  Binary-response clean and contaminating laws are allowed in \(\mathcal E_{[0,1]}\).  Consequently, restriction of any estimator to binary samples gives
\[
 \mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)
 \geq \mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L).
\]
Conversely, let \(T_{\mathrm{bin}}\) be any Borel estimator on binary samples with values in \([-1/2,1/2]\).  For a bounded-response sample \(o_i=(x_i,a_i,y_i)\), define the finite conditional expectation
\[
 \overline T(o_1,\ldots,o_n)
 =\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^n}T_{\mathrm{bin}}(x_1,a_1,z_1,\ldots,x_n,a_n,z_n)
   \prod_{i=1}^n\bigl\{z_i y_i+(1-z_i)(1-y_i)\bigr\}.
\]
This is a finite Borel function, its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one, and therefore \(\overline T\in[-1/2,1/2]\).  Conditional Jensen's inequality, followed by the preceding kernel calculation, gives for every admissible \((P,Q,R)\)
\[
 \mathbb E_{R^n}|\overline T-\theta(P)|
 \leq
 \mathbb E_{(R\mathsf{Ber})^n}|T_{\mathrm{bin}}-\theta(P\mathsf{Ber})|.
\]
Taking the supremum and then the infimum over \(T_{\mathrm{bin}}\) proves the reverse inequality \(\mathfrak R_n\leq\mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}\).  Hence the minimax risks are equal, and the displayed finite sum supplies the claimed derandomization.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} The reduction removes unrestricted conditional outcome distributions without changing the decision problem. \textit{Gap.} ChenGaoRen2016 treats generic clean laws but does not give this causal bounded-to-binary exact experiment reduction. \textit{Consumer.} A finite categorical representation makes same-experiment lower witnesses and computable sieves possible.

\begin{theorem}[thm:ate-tv-modulus]\label{thm:ate-tv-modulus}
There exist \(c_0,C_0>0\) and \(\delta_0>0\), depending only on \(d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L\), such that for every \(0<\delta\leq\delta_0\), \[c_0\delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\leq\omega_\theta(\delta)\leq C_0\delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}},\qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta+\kappa+1}.\] The exponent is uniform in the propensity calibration \(u\), is unchanged when \(P\) and \(P'\) have different propensities, and does not depend on \(\alpha\) or \(d\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix \(P,P'\in\mathcal P_{d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L}\) and put \(\Delta=\operatorname{TV}(P,P')\).  Both laws have Lebesgue-uniform \(X\) by \(\texttt{ass:uniform-design}\).  Define
\[
 r(x)=e(x)\mu_1(x),\quad r'(x)=e'(x)\mu'_1(x),\qquad
 s(x)=\{1-e(x)\}\mu_0(x),\quad s'(x)=\{1-e'(x)\}\mu'_0(x).
\]
For every measurable \(g:[0,1]^d\to[-1,1]\), the definition of total variation implies
\[
 |\mathbb E_P\{g(X)AY\}-\mathbb E_{P'}\{g(X)AY\}|\leq2\Delta,
 \qquad
 |\mathbb E_P\{g(X)A\}-\mathbb E_{P'}\{g(X)A\}|\leq2\Delta.
\]
The conditional-mean identity in \(\texttt{ass:outcome-regression-law}\), followed by the dual characterization of the total variation of a finite signed measure, therefore gives
\[
 \int|r-r'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta,\qquad
 \int|e-e'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta.
\]
The same argument with \((1-A)Y\) gives \(\int|s-s'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta\).  Since \(0\leq\mu'_1,\mu'_0\leq1\),
\[
 e|\mu_1-\mu'_1|\leq|r-r'|+|e-e'|,\qquad
 (1-e)|\mu_0-\mu'_0|\leq|s-s'|+|e-e'|.
\]
By \(\texttt{ass:polynomial-overlap}\) and \(\texttt{ass:calibration-envelope}\), \(e(x)\geq x_1^\kappa/4\) and \(1-e(x)\geq1/2\).  Hence
\[
 \int x_1^\kappa|\mu_1-\mu'_1|\,\mathrm dx\leq16\Delta,
 \qquad
 \int|\mu_0-\mu'_0|\,\mathrm dx\leq8\Delta. \tag{1}
\]
This proves in particular that propensity changes cannot invalidate the weighted comparison.

We next prove the boundary interpolation inequality used below.  Let \(m=\lceil\beta\rceil-1\) and \(q=\beta-m\in(0,1]\).  The Gram matrix
\[
 G_{jk}=\int_1^2 t^{j+k}\,\mathrm dt,\qquad 0\leq j,k\leq m,
\]
is positive definite, because \(c^\top Gc=\int_1^2(\sum_{j=0}^m c_jt^j)^2\,\mathrm dt\) vanishes only for \(c=0\).  Thus there is a unique polynomial \(w_m(t)=\sum_{k=0}^m c_kt^k\) satisfying
\[
 \int_1^2w_m(t)t^j\,\mathrm dt=\frac1{j+1},\qquad 0\leq j\leq m. \tag{2}
\]
If \(f\in\mathcal H^\beta(2L)\), restriction to the first coordinate and the Hölder condition on its order-\(m\) derivative give, uniformly in \(z=(x_2,\ldots,x_d)\),
\[
 f(t,z)=\sum_{j=0}^m\frac{\partial_1^jf(0,z)}{j!}t^j+\mathcal R(t,z),
 \qquad |\mathcal R(t,z)|\leq C_{\beta,L}t^\beta. \tag{3}
\]
For \(m=0\), (3) is exactly the declared Hölder condition.  For \(m\geq1\), it follows by applying the fundamental theorem of calculus successively to the lower derivatives and using the \(q\)-Hölder remainder for \(\partial_1^mf\); this also covers the integer case \(q=1\).  Substitution of (3) into (2), with the change of variable \(t=hs\), cancels every polynomial term and proves, for \(0<h\leq1/2\),
\[
 \left|\int_0^h f(t,z)\,\mathrm dt-
 \int_h^{2h}w_m(t/h)f(t,z)\,\mathrm dt\right|
 \leq C_{\beta,L}h^{\beta+1}. \tag{4}
\]

Apply (4) to \(f=\mu_1-\mu'_1\), whose Hölder radius is at most \(2L\), and integrate over \(z\in[0,1]^{d-1}\).  Writing \(W_m=\sup_{1\leq t\leq2}|w_m(t)|\), equations (1) and (4) imply
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \left|\int f(x)\,\mathrm dx\right|
 &\leq C h^{\beta+1}+\int_{x_1\geq h}|f(x)|\,\mathrm dx
      +W_m\int_{h\leq x_1\leq2h}|f(x)|\,\mathrm dx\\
 &\leq C\{h^{\beta+1}+\Delta h^{-\kappa}\}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{5}
\]
Choose \(h=\Delta^{1/(\beta+\kappa+1)}\), and choose \(\delta_0>0\) so that this \(h\) is at most \(1/2\) whenever \(\Delta\leq\delta_0\).  With
\[
 \gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta+\kappa+1}<1,
\]
(5) is at most \(C\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  The control-arm contribution in (1) is at most \(8\Delta\leq8\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  The triangle inequality proves
\[
 |\theta(P)-\theta(P')|\leq C_0\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}. \tag{6}
\]

For the reverse inequality, set \(r_\beta=\lceil\beta\rceil+1\) and \(\phi(t)=(1-t)_+^{r_\beta}\).  Let
\[
 I_0=\int_0^1\phi(t)\,\mathrm dt>0,\qquad
 I_\kappa=\int_0^1t^\kappa\phi(t)\,\mathrm dt>0.
\]
Choose a constant \(a_\beta>0\), depending only on \(\beta,L\), sufficiently small that every function \(a_\beta h^\beta\phi(x_1/h)\), \(0<h\leq1/4\), has \(\mathcal H^\beta\)-radius at most \(L/2\) and supremum at most \(1/4\).  This choice is possible directly from scaling: for \(0\leq j\leq m\), its order-\(j\) derivative is bounded by a constant times \(a_\beta h^{\beta-j}\), while the \(q\)-Hölder seminorm of its order-\(m\) derivative is bounded by a constant times \(a_\beta h^{\beta-m-q}=a_\beta\).  Mixed and tangential derivatives vanish.

Define two binary-outcome clean laws \(\widetilde P_{0,h}\) and \(\widetilde P_{1,h}\) with uniform \(X\), common \(u=1/2\), common \(\mu_0=1/2\), and
\[
 \mu_{1,0}(x)=\frac12,\qquad
 \mu_{1,1}(x)=\frac12+a_\beta h^\beta\phi(x_1/h).
\]
The preceding scaling calculation, the envelopes, and the constant calibration show from \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\) that both laws belong to \(\mathcal P_{d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L}\) for every \(d\) and \(\alpha\).  Since the laws differ only in the treated binary response and \(e(x)=x_1^\kappa/2\), direct integration gives
\[
 |\theta(\widetilde P_{1,h})-\theta(\widetilde P_{0,h})|=a_\beta I_0h^{\beta+1},\qquad
 \operatorname{TV}(\widetilde P_{0,h},\widetilde P_{1,h})=\frac{a_\beta I_\kappa}{2}h^{\beta+\kappa+1}. \tag{7}
\]
For all sufficiently small \(\delta\), take
\[
 h=\left(\frac{2\delta}{a_\beta I_\kappa}\right)^{1/(\beta+\kappa+1)}\leq\frac14.
\]
Then (7) gives total variation exactly \(\delta\) and target separation \(c_0\delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  Combining this with (6), and reducing \(\delta_0\) if necessary, proves both bounds.  The upper argument allowed \(e\neq e'\), whereas the lower witness used a common constant calibration; consequently the exponent is uniform over \(u\), unaffected by allowing different propensities, and independent of \(\alpha\) and \(d\).
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This theorem derives the irreducible population ambiguity caused jointly by weak overlap and full-record contamination. \textit{Gap.} ChenGaoRen2016 identifies the total-variation modulus as the robust lower-bound object, but neither evaluates it for this ATE class nor handles simultaneous propensity changes. \textit{Consumer.} The modulus gives the mandatory corruption-radius floor for any error bar supplied alongside SasakiUra2022Robustate.

\begin{lemma}[lem:lipschitz-lower-pair-all-kappa]\label{lem:lipschitz-lower-pair-all-kappa}
Fix \(d=1\), \(\beta=1\), \(\alpha\geq1\), \(\kappa>0\), and \(L\geq4\). For every \(0<h\leq1/4\), the laws in \(P_h^{\mathrm{pair}}=(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})\) belong to \(\mathcal P_{1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L}\), and
\[
|\theta(P_{1,h})-\theta(P_{0,h})|=\frac{h^2}{8},
\qquad
\operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
=\frac{h^{\kappa+2}}{8(\kappa+1)(\kappa+2)},
\]
\[
\operatorname{KL}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
\leq\frac{h^{\kappa+3}}
{4(\kappa+1)(\kappa+2)(\kappa+3)}.
\]
There is a constant \(c_0>0\), depending only on \(\alpha,\kappa,L\), such that jointly for every \(n\geq2\) and \(0\leq\epsilon\leq1/10\),
\[
\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L)
\geq c_0\left\{r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\right\}.
\]
For the contamination component the lower bound is realized by the explicit common-observed-law collision in \(\texttt{def:lipschitz-lower-pair}\). For the clean component it is realized by a constant regression perturbation when \(0<\kappa<1\), a multiscale Lipschitz perturbation when \(\kappa=1\), and the displayed boundary pair when \(\kappa>1\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix the parameters in the statement. We first verify the displayed boundary pair. Under either law, \(X\) is uniform, \(u=1/2\) belongs to \(\mathcal H^\alpha(L)\) for every \(\alpha\geq1\), satisfies the calibration envelope, and gives \(e(x)=x^\kappa/2\). The control regression and the baseline treated regression equal \(1/2\). The alternative treated regression
\[
\mu_{1,1}(x)=\frac12+\frac{(h-x)_+}{4}
\]
is \(1/4\)-Lipschitz and takes values in \([1/2,9/16]\). Thus \(\texttt{ass:uniform-design}\), \(\texttt{ass:polynomial-overlap}\), \(\texttt{ass:calibration-envelope}\), \(\texttt{ass:calibration-smoothness}\), \(\texttt{ass:outcome-regression-law}\), \(\texttt{ass:outcome-smoothness}\), and \(\texttt{ass:outcome-envelope}\) hold, so both laws belong to \(\mathcal P_{1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L}\) by \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\).

Put \(v_h(x)=(h-x)_+/4\). Only the treated conditional response law changes. Total variation of two Bernoulli laws is the absolute difference of their success probabilities, so Tonelli's theorem and direct integration give
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\theta(P_{1,h})-\theta(P_{0,h})|
&=\int_0^h\frac{h-x}{4}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{h^2}{8},\\
\operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
&=\int_0^h\frac{x^\kappa}{2}\frac{h-x}{4}\,\mathrm dx\\
&=\frac18h^{\kappa+2}\int_0^1t^\kappa(1-t)\,\mathrm dt
=\frac{h^{\kappa+2}}{8(\kappa+1)(\kappa+2)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here the common \((X,A)\)-margin and the equality of the control laws justify reducing total variation to the displayed treated integral. Because \(h\leq1/4\), \(0\leq v_h\leq1/16\), and
\[
\operatorname{KL}\{\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2),
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2+v)\}
=-\frac12\log(1-4v^2)\leq4v^2.
\]
The inequality follows from \(-\log(1-z)\leq z/(1-z)\) and \(4v^2\leq1/64\). Conditioning on \((X,A)\) and using the common treatment law therefore yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{KL}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
&\leq\int_0^h\frac{x^\kappa}{2}\,4
\left(\frac{h-x}{4}\right)^2\,\mathrm dx\\
&=\frac18h^{\kappa+3}\int_0^1t^\kappa(1-t)^2\,\mathrm dt\\
&=\frac{h^{\kappa+3}}
{4(\kappa+1)(\kappa+2)(\kappa+3)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

We will repeatedly use the following elementary two-point inequality. If probability laws \(G_0,G_1\) have targets \(\vartheta_0,\vartheta_1\), integration of the pointwise triangle inequality against their common part gives, for every estimator \(T\),
\[
\max_{j=0,1}\mathbb E_{G_j}|T-\vartheta_j|
\geq\frac{|\vartheta_1-\vartheta_0|}{2}
\{1-\operatorname{TV}(G_0,G_1)\}.
\tag{1}
\]
Moreover, Jensen's inequality applied under \(G_0\) to the square root of the likelihood ratio, followed by Cauchy--Schwarz, gives
\[
\operatorname{TV}(G_0,G_1)
\leq\sqrt{1-\exp\{-\operatorname{KL}(G_0,G_1)\}}
\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(G_0,G_1)}.
\tag{2}
\]
For product laws, the log likelihood ratios add, hence the Kullback--Leibler divergence is multiplied by \(n\).

We next prove the clean lower rate, treating the three overlap regimes separately. If \(\kappa>1\), take
\[
h=\frac14n^{-1/(\kappa+3)}.
\]
The preceding Kullback--Leibler bound makes \(\operatorname{KL}(P_{0,h}^n,P_{1,h}^n)\) smaller than a constant strictly below \(1/4\), whereas the target separation is a constant multiple of \(n^{-2/(\kappa+3)}=r_{n,\kappa}\). Equations (1)--(2) prove the desired clean lower bound in this regime.

Suppose \(0<\kappa<1\). Keep the same uniform design, calibration, propensity, control regression, and binary conditional outcomes, but replace the treated regression pair by
\[
\mu_{1,0}(x)=\frac12,
\qquad
\mu_{1,1}(x)=\frac12+v_n,
\qquad
v_n=\eta n^{-1/2},
\]
where \(0<\eta\leq1/32\) is fixed. These regressions satisfy the stated Lipschitz and envelope restrictions. Their target separation is \(v_n\), and the Bernoulli calculation above gives
\[
\operatorname{KL}(P_{0,n},P_{1,n})
\leq4v_n^2\int_0^1\frac{x^\kappa}{2}\,\mathrm dx
=\frac{2v_n^2}{\kappa+1}.
\]
Thus the product divergence is at most \(2\eta^2/(\kappa+1)<1/4\). Equations (1)--(2) give a constant multiple of \(n^{-1/2}=r_{n,\kappa}\).

It remains to obtain the equality-case logarithm. Let \(\kappa=1\), put \(\ell_n=\log(en)\), choose a fixed \(0<\eta\leq2^{-10}\), and define
\[
c_n=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{n\ell_n}},
\qquad s_n=\sqrt{c_n},
\qquad
f_n(x)=\frac{c_n}{x\vee s_n}.
\]
Use treated regressions \(1/2\) and \(1/2+f_n\), leaving all other ingredients unchanged. Since \(f_n(0)=s_n\leq1/16\) and the derivative on \((s_n,1]\) has magnitude at most \(c_n/s_n^2=1\), \(f_n\) is one-Lipschitz and the two laws remain in \(\mathcal P_{1,\alpha,1,1,L}\). Direct calculation gives
\[
\int_0^1f_n(x)\,\mathrm dx
=c_n\{1+\log(1/s_n)\}
\asymp \sqrt{\frac{\log(en)}{n}},
\tag{3}
\]
because \(\log(1/s_n)=\tfrac14\log(n\ell_n)+\tfrac12\log(1/\eta)\asymp\ell_n\) uniformly for \(n\geq2\). On the other hand,
\[
\int_0^1x f_n(x)^2\,\mathrm dx
=c_n^2\left\{\frac12+\log(1/s_n)\right\},
\]
and hence
\[
n\operatorname{KL}(P_{0,n},P_{1,n})
\leq2n\int_0^1x f_n(x)^2\,\mathrm dx
\leq C\eta^2.
\tag{4}
\]
Reducing \(\eta\), if necessary, makes (4) smaller than \(1/4\). Equations (1)--(4) give the clean lower rate \(\{\log(en)/n\}^{1/2}=r_{n,1}\). The three cases prove
\[
\mathfrak R_n(0;1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L)\geq c_0r_{n,\kappa}.
\tag{5}
\]

For any \(\epsilon\), taking \(Q=P\) makes \(R=P\), so the contaminated experiment contains the clean experiment and (5) remains a lower bound. For \(0<\epsilon\leq1/10\), set
\[
h=\frac14\left(\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{1/(\kappa+2)}.
\]
Then \(h\leq1/4\), and the exact total-variation formula shows that
\[
t=\operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}.
\]
Consequently \(c=(1-\epsilon)t/\epsilon\in[0,1]\), so \(Q_{0,h}\) and \(Q_{1,h}\) in \(\texttt{def:lipschitz-lower-pair}\) are probability laws. The Jordan identity \(P_{1,h}-P_{0,h}=t(S_+-S_-)\) gives
\[
(1-\epsilon)(P_{0,h}-P_{1,h})
+\epsilon(Q_{0,h}-Q_{1,h})=0.
\]
Thus the two admissible contaminated triples have exactly the same observed law \(R_h\), while their targets differ by
\[
\frac{h^2}{8}=\frac1{128}
\left(\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{2/(\kappa+2)}.
\]
Applying the pointwise triangle inequality under this common law gives a risk of at least half this separation. Since \(1\leq(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\leq10/9\), this is bounded below by a constant multiple of \(\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\). At \(\epsilon=0\) this component is zero. Finally, the maximum of the clean and contamination lower bounds is at least half their sum, proving the lemma.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} The explicit all-kappa witnesses certify the three clean regimes and the contamination component inside the same declared experiment. \textit{Gap.} Generic Huber theory does not construct pairs satisfying the exact uniform design, polynomial propensity, Lipschitz regression, and bounded-response restrictions, nor the logarithmic clean elbow. \textit{Consumer.} The lemma supplies every lower component of the one-dimensional all-kappa frontier and the collision template retained by the general open question.

\begin{theorem}[thm:lipschitz-all-kappa]\label{thm:lipschitz-all-kappa}
Fix \(d=1\), \(\beta=1\), \(\alpha\geq1\), \(\kappa>0\), and \(L\geq4\), and define
\[
r_{n,\kappa}=
\begin{cases}
n^{-1/2},&0<\kappa<1,\\
\{\log(en)/n\}^{1/2},&\kappa=1,\\
n^{-2/(\kappa+3)},&\kappa>1.
\end{cases}
\]
There are constants \(c_0,C_0>0\), depending only on \(\alpha,\kappa,L\), such that jointly for every \(n\geq2\) and \(0\leq\epsilon\leq1/10\),
\[
c_0\{r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\}
\leq\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L)
\leq\sup_{(P,Q,R)\in\mathcal C_{1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L}(\epsilon)}
\mathbb E_{R^n}|T_{\mathrm{Lip}}-\theta(P)|
\leq C_0\{r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\}.
\]
When \(b_n\leq1/4\), the pre-optimization bound is
\[
\sup_{(P,Q,R)\in\mathcal C_{1,\alpha,1,\kappa,L}(\epsilon)}
\mathbb E_{R^n}|T_{\mathrm{Lip}}-\theta(P)|
\leq C_0\left[
h_n^2+n^{-1/2}V_\kappa(h_n)^{1/2}
+\epsilon h_n^{-\kappa}
+\exp\{-c_0nh_n^{\kappa+3/2}\}
\right],
\]
where
\[
V_\kappa(h)=
\begin{cases}
1,&0<\kappa<1,\\
1+\log(1/h),&\kappa=1,\\
h^{1-\kappa},&\kappa>1.
\end{cases}
\]
For every body cell \(D\subseteq[r_j,2r_j]\), writing \(q_D=Q\{X\in D\}\), its weighted contamination shift is at most \(C_0\epsilon q_D/r_j^\kappa\), and \(\sum_Dq_D\leq1\); the reused cell \(B_n\) contributes at most \(C_0\epsilon q_{B_n}/h_n^\kappa\). Thus the bound covers both point and diffuse within-cell contamination. The estimator uses \(O(n\log(1/h_n)+h_n^{-1})\) arithmetic operations and \(O(h_n^{-1})\) memory. In particular, the clean functional rate and the ATE contamination modulus are simultaneously attained throughout this subfamily, including the logarithmic elbow at \(\kappa=1\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix an admissible triple \((P,Q,R)\). Because \(\theta(P)\in[-1/2,1/2]\), projection onto this interval cannot increase absolute error. It therefore suffices to study the expression inside the clipping map. Assume first that \(b_n\leq1/4\). The dyadic ceiling satisfies
\[
b_n\leq h_n<2b_n,
\qquad h_n\leq\frac12.
\tag{6}
\]

For a cell \(D\) and arm \(a\), set
\[
p_a(x)=
\begin{cases}e(x),&a=1,\\1-e(x),&a=0,\end{cases}
\qquad
s_a^P(D)=\int_Dp_a(x)\,\mathrm dx,
\qquad
m_a^P(D)=\frac{\int_Dp_a(x)\mu_a(x)\,\mathrm dx}{s_a^P(D)}.
\]
All denominators are positive: on a body or reconstruction cell, \(x\geq h_n>0\), so \(e(x)\geq x^\kappa/4>0\), while \(1-e(x)\geq1/2\) by \(u\leq1/2\). If \(\overline\mu_a(D)=|D|^{-1}\int_D\mu_a\), the exact covariance identity under the uniform distribution on \(D\) is
\[
m_a^P(D)-\overline\mu_a(D)
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{\operatorname{Unif}(D)}(p_a,\mu_a)}
{\mathbb E_{\operatorname{Unif}(D)}p_a}.
\tag{7}
\]

Let \(D\subseteq[r,2r]\) have length \(\ell\). The assumption \(\alpha\geq1\) and \(\texttt{ass:calibration-smoothness}\) imply that \(u\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz: for \(\alpha=1\) this is the defining Hölder condition, and for \(\alpha>1\) it follows from the bound on its first derivative. By the mean-value theorem, for \(x,y\in[r,2r]\),
\[
|x^\kappa-y^\kappa|\leq C_\kappa r^{\kappa-1}|x-y|.
\]
Together with \(1/4\leq u\leq1/2\), this gives
\[
\inf_De\geq\frac{r^\kappa}{4},
\qquad
\operatorname{osc}_D(e)\leq C_{\alpha,\kappa,L}r^{\kappa-1}\ell,
\qquad
\operatorname{osc}_D(\mu_a)\leq L\ell,
\qquad
\inf_D(1-e)\geq\frac12.
\tag{8}
\]
The elementary inequality \(|\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)|\leq\operatorname{osc}(U)\operatorname{osc}(V)\), (7), and (8) imply, for both arms,
\[
|m_a^P(D)-\overline\mu_a(D)|
\leq C_{\alpha,\kappa,L}\frac{\ell^2}{r}.
\tag{9}
\]
For the control arm, the direct bound is \(C r^{\kappa-1}\ell^2\), which is no larger than the right side of (9) because \(0<r\leq1\).

Within slab \([r_j,2r_j]\), all cells have length \(\ell_j=r_j/M_j\), apart from the harmless endpoint convention, and
\[
\frac{h_n\sqrt{r_j}}2\leq\ell_j\leq h_n\sqrt{r_j}.
\tag{10}
\]
Indeed, the upper bound follows from the ceiling in \(M_j\), and the lower bound follows from \(M_j\leq2\sqrt{r_j}/h_n\), since \(r_j\geq h_n\). Because the cell lengths in one slab sum to \(r_j\), (9)--(10) give
\[
\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n}|D|
|m_a^P(D)-\overline\mu_a(D)|
\leq C\sum_j\ell_j^2
\leq Ch_n^2\sum_jr_j
\leq Ch_n^2.
\tag{11}
\]
For \(t\in[0,h_n]\) and \(x\in B_n=[h_n,2h_n]\), outcome Lipschitzness gives \(|\mu_a(t)-\mu_a(x)|\leq2Lh_n\). Since \(m_a^P(B_n)\) is a convex average of the values \(\mu_a(x)\),
\[
\left|\int_0^{h_n}\mu_a(t)\,\mathrm dt-h_nm_a^P(B_n)\right|
\leq2Lh_n^2.
\tag{12}
\]
Equations (11)--(12), summed over the two arms, prove a clean population approximation bias bounded by \(Ch_n^2\).

We next compare clean and contaminated population cell means. Write
\[
q_{D,a}=Q\{X\in D,A=a\},
\qquad q_D=Q\{X\in D\},
\]
and denote the corresponding conditional response mean under \(R\) by \(m_a^R(D)\). Expanding the mixture numerator and denominator and using \(0\leq Y\leq1\) gives
\[
|m_a^R(D)-m_a^P(D)|
\leq\frac{\epsilon q_{D,a}}
{(1-\epsilon)s_a^P(D)+\epsilon q_{D,a}}
\leq\frac{\epsilon q_{D,a}}{(1-\epsilon)s_a^P(D)}.
\tag{13}
\]
For a body cell in \([r_j,2r_j]\), (8) gives
\[
s_1^P(D)\geq\frac14r_j^\kappa|D|,
\qquad
s_0^P(D)\geq\frac12|D|.
\]
Hence its weighted shift is at most \(C\epsilon q_D/r_j^\kappa\) for either arm. The body cells are disjoint, so \(\sum_Dq_D\leq1\). Likewise,
\[
s_1^P(B_n)\geq c_\kappa h_n^{\kappa+1},
\qquad
s_0^P(B_n)\geq\frac12h_n,
\]
and the boundary weight \(h_n\) turns (13) into a bound \(C\epsilon q_{B_n}/h_n^\kappa\). Although \(B_n\) is reused, it is charged only one additional time and \(q_{B_n}\leq1\). Since \(r_j\geq h_n\), the total population contamination shift is therefore at most
\[
C\epsilon h_n^{-\kappa}.
\tag{14}
\]
This uses only the global \(Q\)-mass budget, so neither an atom nor diffuse contamination within one cell creates an additional term.

It remains to control sampling fluctuations and empty cells. If a measurable arm-cell has \(R\)-probability \(p>0\), its count \(N\) is binomial by \(\texttt{ass:iid-sampling}\). Integrating \((1-p+pt)^n\) over \([0,1]\) gives
\[
\mathbb E\frac1{N+1}=\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1}}{(n+1)p},
\qquad
\mathbb E\left\{\frac{\mathbf1\{N>0\}}N\right\}
\leq\frac2{(n+1)p}.
\tag{15}
\]
Conditional on the disjoint body arm-cell counts, every nonempty cell mean is centered at \(m_a^R(D)\), and conditional cross-covariances between distinct categories vanish. Since a response in \([0,1]\) has variance at most \(1/4\), (15), Jensen's inequality, and \(p_a^R(D)\geq(1-\epsilon)s_a^P(D)\) show that the expected absolute centered treated-body fluctuation is at most
\[
\begin{aligned}
C\left\{\frac1n\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n}
\frac{|D|^2}{r_j^\kappa|D|}\right\}^{1/2}
&=C\left\{\frac1n\sum_jr_j^{1-\kappa}\right\}^{1/2}\\
&\leq Cn^{-1/2}V_\kappa(h_n)^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
\]
The final inequality is the elementary geometric-sum calculation
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}(2^jh_n)^{1-\kappa}+h_n^{1-\kappa}
\leq C_\kappa
\begin{cases}
1,&0<\kappa<1,\\
1+\log(1/h_n),&\kappa=1,\\
h_n^{1-\kappa},&\kappa>1.
\end{cases}
\tag{17}
\]
The extra term in (17) is the treated reconstruction-cell variance, because its weight squared divided by its clean mass is of order \(h_n^{1-\kappa}\). For the control arm, the analogous body sum and reconstruction term are bounded by a constant, hence are absorbed by \(V_\kappa(h_n)\geq1\). The body and reconstruction fluctuations were combined by the triangle inequality, so the reuse of \(B_n\) requires no independence assertion.

For a treated body cell, (10) and the clean mixture weight \(1-\epsilon\geq9/10\) imply
\[
p_1^R(D)\geq c r_j^\kappa\ell_j
\geq c h_nr_j^{\kappa+1/2}
\geq c h_n^{\kappa+3/2}.
\tag{18}
\]
Control cells have mass at least \(c h_n^{3/2}\), the treated reconstruction cell has mass at least \(c_\kappa h_n^{\kappa+1}\), and the control reconstruction cell has mass at least \(ch_n\); all are at least a constant multiple of \(h_n^{\kappa+3/2}\). Thus each relevant empty-cell probability is at most \(\exp\{-cnh_n^{\kappa+3/2}\}\). The nonnegative body weights sum to \(1-h_n\) for each arm and the reconstruction weight is \(h_n\). Summing weighted empty-cell errors, without a union bound over cells, gives
\[
C\exp\{-cnh_n^{\kappa+3/2}\}.
\tag{19}
\]
Combining (11)--(19) proves the pre-optimization bound.

Let \(q_{n,\kappa}=r_{n,\kappa}^{1/2}\). By definition, \(b_n=\max\{\epsilon^{1/(\kappa+2)},q_{n,\kappa}\}\). Equations (6) and \(\epsilon\leq h_n^{\kappa+2}\) imply
\[
h_n^2\leq4\{r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\},
\qquad
\epsilon h_n^{-\kappa}\leq h_n^2.
\tag{20}
\]
For the stochastic term, (17) and \(h_n\geq q_{n,\kappa}\) give, respectively,
\[
n^{-1/2}V_\kappa(h_n)^{1/2}
\leq C_\kappa
\begin{cases}
n^{-1/2},&0<\kappa<1,\\
\{\log(en)/n\}^{1/2},&\kappa=1,\\
n^{-1/2}q_{n,\kappa}^{(1-\kappa)/2},&\kappa>1,
\end{cases}
\leq C_\kappa r_{n,\kappa}.
\tag{21}
\]
In the last line for \(\kappa>1\), the identity
\(n^{-1/2}q_{n,\kappa}^{(1-\kappa)/2}=n^{-2/(\kappa+3)}\) was used. At the minimal clean bandwidth,
\[
nq_{n,\kappa}^{\kappa+3/2}\geq
\begin{cases}
n^{(5/2-\kappa)/4},&0<\kappa<1,\\
n^{3/8},&\kappa=1,\\
n^{3/\{2(\kappa+3)\}},&\kappa>1.
\end{cases}
\]
Each exponent is positive. Since increasing \(h_n\) only decreases the exponential and increases \(h_n^2\), the elementary bound \(\sup_{t\geq1}t^a e^{-ct^b}<\infty\), for \(a,b,c>0\), yields
\[
\exp\{-cnh_n^{\kappa+3/2}\}\leq C_\kappa h_n^2.
\tag{22}
\]
Equations (20)--(22) prove the rate upper bound when \(b_n\leq1/4\).

If \(b_n>1/4\), the estimator returns zero and its absolute error is at most \(1/2\). In this branch either \(r_{n,\kappa}>1/16\) or \(\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}>1/16\), so the same upper rate follows after enlarging \(C_0\). The lower bound is exactly \(\texttt{lem:lipschitz-lower-pair-all-kappa}\). Since \(T_{\mathrm{Lip}}\) is one member of \(\mathfrak T_n\), \(\texttt{def:minimax-risk}\) supplies the middle inequality.

Finally,
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}M_j
\leq h_n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\sqrt{r_j}+J
=O(h_n^{-1}).
\]
Constructing cell endpoints therefore uses \(O(h_n^{-1})\) arithmetic and memory. Binary search assigns each record using \(O(\log(1/h_n))\) comparisons, and one pass accumulates all counts and sums. Zero counts use the declared value \(1/2\), and every remaining operation is a finite Borel comparison, sum, product, quotient with a checked positive denominator, or clipping. Hence the estimator is total, finite, data-only, and belongs to \(\mathfrak T_n\), with the stated computational bounds. Finally, \(\texttt{thm:ate-tv-modulus}\) with \(\beta=1\) gives \(\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}=2/(\kappa+2)\). Therefore the attained contamination term is exactly the order of \(\omega_\theta(\epsilon/(1-\epsilon))\), because \(1\leq(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\leq10/9\). This proves the simultaneous-attainment assertion and completes the proof.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This is the headline matched minimax phase diagram, including a computable estimator, the equality-case logarithm, and arbitrary full-record corruption. \textit{Gap.} The clean Ma--Sant'Anna--Sasaki--Ura experiment is incomparable with this controlled polynomial-boundary Huber experiment, and no published clean converse or robust whole-law theorem yields this scalar-functional equality. \textit{Consumer.} The result gives the robustate workflow a theoretical trimming-scale and error-radius benchmark across every polynomial boundary exponent, without asserting that its data or parametric propensity satisfy the benchmark.

\begin{theorem}[thm:lipschitz-calibration]\label{thm:lipschitz-calibration}
On the full declared class with \(d=1\), \(\alpha=\beta=1\), and \(\kappa=2\), there exist \(c_0,C_0>0\), depending only on \(L\), such that jointly for every \(n\geq2\) and \(0\leq\epsilon\leq1/10\), \[c_0\{n^{-2/5}+\epsilon^{1/2}\}\leq\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;1,1,1,2,L)\leq\sup_{(P,Q,R)\in\mathcal C_{1,1,1,2,L}(\epsilon)}\mathbb E_{R^n}|T_{\mathrm{Lip}}-\theta(P)|\leq C_0\{n^{-2/5}+\epsilon^{1/2}\}.\] When \(b_n\leq1/4\), the sharper pre-optimization bound is \[\sup_{(P,Q,R)\in\mathcal C_{1,1,1,2,L}(\epsilon)}\mathbb E_{R^n}|T_{\mathrm{Lip}}-\theta(P)|\leq C_0\left[h_n^2+(nh_n)^{-1/2}+\epsilon h_n^{-2}+\exp\{-c_0nh_n^{7/2}\}\right].\] For each body cell \(D\subseteq[r_j,2r_j]\), writing \(q_D=Q\{X\in D\}\), its weighted contamination shift is at most \(C_0\epsilon q_D/r_j^2\), and \(\sum_Dq_D\leq1\); the reused boundary slab \(B_n\) contributes at most \(C_0\epsilon q_{B_n}/h_n^2\). These bounds depend only on total contaminating mass and therefore cover both point masses and diffuse within-cell contamination. The estimator uses \(O(n\log(1/h_n)+h_n^{-1})\) arithmetic operations and \(O(h_n^{-1})\) memory.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Specialize \(\texttt{thm:lipschitz-all-kappa}\) to \(\alpha=1\) and \(\kappa=2\). Then
\[
r_{n,2}=n^{-2/5},
\qquad
\epsilon^{2/(2+2)}=\epsilon^{1/2},
\qquad
V_2(h)=h^{-1}.
\]
Consequently its stochastic term is \(n^{-1/2}h_n^{-1/2}=(nh_n)^{-1/2}\), its contamination term is \(\epsilon h_n^{-2}\), and its empty-cell exponent is \(nh_n^{7/2}\). Its cellwise contamination and computational bounds specialize verbatim to those in the present statement. This proves the original quadratic-overlap theorem as a corollary and supplies the requested consistency check.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} The original quadratic-overlap result is now a direct corollary of the stronger all-kappa theorem. \textit{Gap.} It records the previously solved rate and pre-optimization envelope without competing with the generalized headline. \textit{Consumer.} It keeps the quadratic benchmark available for direct comparison with the earlier presolve and applied discussion.

c_0\rho_{\mathrm{eval}}

\section{Interpretation}

The overlap exponent controls the clean price through the summability of inverse treated-cell mass. When \(\kappa<1\), that sum is finite and root-\(n\) risk remains possible; at \(\kappa=1\), contributions from all dyadic scales accumulate a square-root logarithm; above one, the lowest retained slab dominates and produces \(n^{-2/(\kappa+3)}\). Corruption has a different balance: a boundary slab of width \(h\) amplifies adversarial mass by \(h^{-\kappa}\), so balancing it with Lipschitz boundary bias yields \(\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\). One bandwidth attains both terms, so there is no additional nuisance--contamination interaction penalty on this subfamily.

\section*{Technical internal limitation (diagnostic only)}

The direct cell argument relies on one-dimensional Lipschitz covariance bias and boundary reconstruction. For a boundary cell \(B=[h,2h]\), clean treated mass is of order \(h^{\kappa+1}\), so either point or diffuse contaminating mass \(q_B\) shifts its weighted contribution by at most order \(\epsilon q_Bh^{-\kappa}\); at \(h=\epsilon^{1/(\kappa+2)}\) this equals the squared boundary bias. In higher dimension or under other smoothness orders, preserving the clean projected functional gain requires robust high-resolution nuisance and Gram estimation that is not proved here.

\section{Honest scope}

The headline is the exact one-dimensional result for \(d=1\), \(\beta=1\), every \(\alpha\geq1\), and every \(\kappa>0\): \(\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon)\asymp r_{n,\kappa}+\epsilon^{2/(\kappa+2)}\), with a total finite estimator, the \(\kappa=1\) logarithm, and matching same-experiment converses. The former quadratic theorem is retained as a corollary. The cases \(d>1\), \(\beta\neq1\), or \(\alpha<1\), their interactions, and their remaining equality-case logarithms are open. Fixed nonzero contamination leaves irreducible ambiguity; no claim identifies \(\theta(P)\) from \(R\), and no claim says the Sasaki--Ura application is contaminated or satisfies this exact benchmark class.
