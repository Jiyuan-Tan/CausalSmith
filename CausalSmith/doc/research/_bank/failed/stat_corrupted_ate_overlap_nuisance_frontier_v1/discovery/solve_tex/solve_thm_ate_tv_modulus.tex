%%% FIELD proof-bernoulli-reduction
Let \(\mathcal E_{[0,1]}\) denote the experiment in the statement and let \(\mathcal E_{\{0,1\}}\) be its subexperiment in which the response is binary.  We first check that the kernel in \(\texttt{def:bernoulli-reduction}\) maps the former experiment into the latter.  For a clean law \(P\), conditional iterated expectation and \(\texttt{ass:outcome-regression-law}\) give, at every point at which the conditional mean is defined,
\[
 \mathbb P_P(Z=1\mid X=x,A=a)
 =\mathbb E_P\{\mathbb P(Z=1\mid O)\mid X=x,A=a\}
 =\mathbb E_P(Y\mid X=x,A=a)=\mu_a(x).
\]
Thus the transformed clean law has the same uniform covariate law, propensity \(e\), and regressions \(\mu_a\).  In particular, it is in the same clean class by \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\), and its identifying functional is the same \(\theta(P)\).  The value at \(x_1=0\) remains the prescribed continuous extension and is not inferred from a null conditional event.  If \(Q\) is any contaminating law, its image \(Q\mathsf{Ber}\) is a probability law on the binary-response subset of \(\mathcal O\), hence is admissible in \(\texttt{def:contaminated-class}\).  Linearity of a Markov kernel and \(\texttt{ass:huber-mixture}\) yield
\[
 R\mathsf{Ber}=(1-\epsilon)P\mathsf{Ber}+\epsilon Q\mathsf{Ber}.
\]
Applying the kernel independently to the independent records preserves the product sampling structure in \(\texttt{ass:iid-sampling}\).

It remains to prove equality, rather than merely monotonicity, of the two minimax risks.  Write \(\mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}\) for the minimax value over \(\mathcal E_{\{0,1\}}\).  Binary-response clean and contaminating laws are allowed in \(\mathcal E_{[0,1]}\).  Consequently, restriction of any estimator to binary samples gives
\[
 \mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)
 \geq \mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L).
\]
Conversely, let \(T_{\mathrm{bin}}\) be any Borel estimator on binary samples with values in \([-1/2,1/2]\).  For a bounded-response sample \(o_i=(x_i,a_i,y_i)\), define the finite conditional expectation
\[
 \overline T(o_1,\ldots,o_n)
 =\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^n}T_{\mathrm{bin}}(x_1,a_1,z_1,\ldots,x_n,a_n,z_n)
   \prod_{i=1}^n\bigl\{z_i y_i+(1-z_i)(1-y_i)\bigr\}.
\]
This is a finite Borel function, its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one, and therefore \(\overline T\in[-1/2,1/2]\).  Conditional Jensen's inequality, followed by the preceding kernel calculation, gives for every admissible \((P,Q,R)\)
\[
 \mathbb E_{R^n}|\overline T-\theta(P)|
 \leq
 \mathbb E_{(R\mathsf{Ber})^n}|T_{\mathrm{bin}}-\theta(P\mathsf{Ber})|.
\]
Taking the supremum and then the infimum over \(T_{\mathrm{bin}}\) proves the reverse inequality \(\mathfrak R_n\leq\mathfrak R_n^{\mathrm{bin}}\).  Hence the minimax risks are equal, and the displayed finite sum supplies the claimed derandomization.

%%% FIELD proof-ate-tv-modulus
Fix \(P,P'\in\mathcal P_{d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L}\) and put \(\Delta=\operatorname{TV}(P,P')\).  Both laws have Lebesgue-uniform \(X\) by \(\texttt{ass:uniform-design}\).  Define
\[
 r(x)=e(x)\mu_1(x),\quad r'(x)=e'(x)\mu'_1(x),\qquad
 s(x)=\{1-e(x)\}\mu_0(x),\quad s'(x)=\{1-e'(x)\}\mu'_0(x).
\]
For every measurable \(g:[0,1]^d\to[-1,1]\), the definition of total variation implies
\[
 |\mathbb E_P\{g(X)AY\}-\mathbb E_{P'}\{g(X)AY\}|\leq2\Delta,
 \qquad
 |\mathbb E_P\{g(X)A\}-\mathbb E_{P'}\{g(X)A\}|\leq2\Delta.
\]
The conditional-mean identity in \(\texttt{ass:outcome-regression-law}\), followed by the dual characterization of the total variation of a finite signed measure, therefore gives
\[
 \int|r-r'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta,\qquad
 \int|e-e'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta.
\]
The same argument with \((1-A)Y\) gives \(\int|s-s'|\,\mathrm dx\leq2\Delta\).  Since \(0\leq\mu'_1,\mu'_0\leq1\),
\[
 e|\mu_1-\mu'_1|\leq|r-r'|+|e-e'|,\qquad
 (1-e)|\mu_0-\mu'_0|\leq|s-s'|+|e-e'|.
\]
By \(\texttt{ass:polynomial-overlap}\) and \(\texttt{ass:calibration-envelope}\), \(e(x)\geq x_1^\kappa/4\) and \(1-e(x)\geq1/2\).  Hence
\[
 \int x_1^\kappa|\mu_1-\mu'_1|\,\mathrm dx\leq16\Delta,
 \qquad
 \int|\mu_0-\mu'_0|\,\mathrm dx\leq8\Delta. \tag{1}
\]
This proves in particular that propensity changes cannot invalidate the weighted comparison.

We next prove the boundary interpolation inequality used below.  Let \(m=\lceil\beta\rceil-1\) and \(q=\beta-m\in(0,1]\).  The Gram matrix
\[
 G_{jk}=\int_1^2 t^{j+k}\,\mathrm dt,\qquad 0\leq j,k\leq m,
\]
is positive definite, because \(c^\top Gc=\int_1^2(\sum_{j=0}^m c_jt^j)^2\,\mathrm dt\) vanishes only for \(c=0\).  Thus there is a unique polynomial \(w_m(t)=\sum_{k=0}^m c_kt^k\) satisfying
\[
 \int_1^2w_m(t)t^j\,\mathrm dt=\frac1{j+1},\qquad 0\leq j\leq m. \tag{2}
\]
If \(f\in\mathcal H^\beta(2L)\), restriction to the first coordinate and the Hölder condition on its order-\(m\) derivative give, uniformly in \(z=(x_2,\ldots,x_d)\),
\[
 f(t,z)=\sum_{j=0}^m\frac{\partial_1^jf(0,z)}{j!}t^j+\mathcal R(t,z),
 \qquad |\mathcal R(t,z)|\leq C_{\beta,L}t^\beta. \tag{3}
\]
For \(m=0\), (3) is exactly the declared Hölder condition.  For \(m\geq1\), it follows by applying the fundamental theorem of calculus successively to the lower derivatives and using the \(q\)-Hölder remainder for \(\partial_1^mf\); this also covers the integer case \(q=1\).  Substitution of (3) into (2), with the change of variable \(t=hs\), cancels every polynomial term and proves, for \(0<h\leq1/2\),
\[
 \left|\int_0^h f(t,z)\,\mathrm dt-
 \int_h^{2h}w_m(t/h)f(t,z)\,\mathrm dt\right|
 \leq C_{\beta,L}h^{\beta+1}. \tag{4}
\]

Apply (4) to \(f=\mu_1-\mu'_1\), whose Hölder radius is at most \(2L\), and integrate over \(z\in[0,1]^{d-1}\).  Writing \(W_m=\sup_{1\leq t\leq2}|w_m(t)|\), equations (1) and (4) imply
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \left|\int f(x)\,\mathrm dx\right|
 &\leq C h^{\beta+1}+\int_{x_1\geq h}|f(x)|\,\mathrm dx
      +W_m\int_{h\leq x_1\leq2h}|f(x)|\,\mathrm dx\\
 &\leq C\{h^{\beta+1}+\Delta h^{-\kappa}\}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{5}
\]
Choose \(h=\Delta^{1/(\beta+\kappa+1)}\), and choose \(\delta_0>0\) so that this \(h\) is at most \(1/2\) whenever \(\Delta\leq\delta_0\).  With
\[
 \gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta+\kappa+1}<1,
\]
(5) is at most \(C\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  The control-arm contribution in (1) is at most \(8\Delta\leq8\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  The triangle inequality proves
\[
 |\theta(P)-\theta(P')|\leq C_0\Delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}. \tag{6}
\]

For the reverse inequality, set \(r_\beta=\lceil\beta\rceil+1\) and \(\phi(t)=(1-t)_+^{r_\beta}\).  Let
\[
 I_0=\int_0^1\phi(t)\,\mathrm dt>0,\qquad
 I_\kappa=\int_0^1t^\kappa\phi(t)\,\mathrm dt>0.
\]
Choose a constant \(a_\beta>0\), depending only on \(\beta,L\), sufficiently small that every function \(a_\beta h^\beta\phi(x_1/h)\), \(0<h\leq1/4\), has \(\mathcal H^\beta\)-radius at most \(L/2\) and supremum at most \(1/4\).  This choice is possible directly from scaling: for \(0\leq j\leq m\), its order-\(j\) derivative is bounded by a constant times \(a_\beta h^{\beta-j}\), while the \(q\)-Hölder seminorm of its order-\(m\) derivative is bounded by a constant times \(a_\beta h^{\beta-m-q}=a_\beta\).  Mixed and tangential derivatives vanish.

Define two binary-outcome clean laws \(\widetilde P_{0,h}\) and \(\widetilde P_{1,h}\) with uniform \(X\), common \(u=1/2\), common \(\mu_0=1/2\), and
\[
 \mu_{1,0}(x)=\frac12,\qquad
 \mu_{1,1}(x)=\frac12+a_\beta h^\beta\phi(x_1/h).
\]
The preceding scaling calculation, the envelopes, and the constant calibration show from \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\) that both laws belong to \(\mathcal P_{d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L}\) for every \(d\) and \(\alpha\).  Since the laws differ only in the treated binary response and \(e(x)=x_1^\kappa/2\), direct integration gives
\[
 |\theta(\widetilde P_{1,h})-\theta(\widetilde P_{0,h})|=a_\beta I_0h^{\beta+1},\qquad
 \operatorname{TV}(\widetilde P_{0,h},\widetilde P_{1,h})=\frac{a_\beta I_\kappa}{2}h^{\beta+\kappa+1}. \tag{7}
\]
For all sufficiently small \(\delta\), take
\[
 h=\left(\frac{2\delta}{a_\beta I_\kappa}\right)^{1/(\beta+\kappa+1)}\leq\frac14.
\]
Then (7) gives total variation exactly \(\delta\) and target separation \(c_0\delta^{\gamma_{\mathrm{TV}}}\).  Combining this with (6), and reducing \(\delta_0\) if necessary, proves both bounds.  The upper argument allowed \(e\neq e'\), whereas the lower witness used a common constant calibration; consequently the exponent is uniform over \(u\), unaffected by allowing different propensities, and independent of \(\alpha\) and \(d\).

%%% FIELD proof-lipschitz-lower-pair
Fix \(0<h\leq1/4\).  In \(\texttt{def:lipschitz-lower-pair}\), \(X\) is uniform, \(u=1/2\) is in \(\mathcal H^1(L)\) and lies in the calibration envelope, and \(e(x)=x^2/2\).  The control regression and the baseline treated regression equal \(1/2\).  The alternative treated regression
\[
 \mu_{1,1}(x)=\frac12+\frac{(h-x)_+}{4}
\]
is \(1/4\)-Lipschitz and takes values in \([1/2,9/16]\).  Thus all smoothness and envelope requirements in \(\texttt{def:clean-law-class}\) hold for \(L\geq4\), and both laws are members of \(\mathcal P_{1,1,1,2,L}\).

Only the treated conditional binary law differs.  Put \(v(x)=(h-x)_+/4\).  Direct integration gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 |\theta(P_{1,h})-\theta(P_{0,h})|
 &=\int_0^h\frac{h-x}{4}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{h^2}{8},\\
 \operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
 &=\int_0^h\frac{x^2}{2}\frac{h-x}{4}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{h^4}{96}.
 \end{aligned}\tag{8}
\]
For \(0\leq v\leq1/16\),
\[
 \operatorname{KL}\{\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2),
                    \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2+v)\}
 =-\frac12\log(1-4v^2)\leq4v^2,
\]
where the last inequality follows from \(-\log(1-z)\leq z/(1-z)\) for \(0\leq z<1\).  Conditioning on \(X\), and noting that the treatment and control probabilities are common under the two laws, therefore yields
\[
 \operatorname{KL}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})
 \leq\int_0^h\frac{x^2}{2}\,4\left(\frac{h-x}{4}\right)^2\,\mathrm dx
 =\frac18\int_0^h x^2(h-x)^2\,\mathrm dx
 =\frac{h^5}{240}. \tag{9}
\]

For completeness, we convert (9) into the claimed clean risk without invoking an unrecorded testing result.  If \(\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1\) have densities \(p_0,p_1\), their Hellinger affinity \(B=\int\sqrt{p_0p_1}\) satisfies
\[
 \log B\geq-\frac12\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1)
\]
by Jensen's inequality under \(\mathbb P_0\).  Cauchy--Schwarz then gives
\[
 \operatorname{TV}(\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1)
 \leq\sqrt{1-B^2}
 \leq\sqrt{1-\exp\{-\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1)\}}
 \leq\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1)}. \tag{10}
\]
For product laws the log likelihood ratios add, so their Kullback--Leibler divergence is \(n\) times (9).  If \(n\geq4^5\), choose \(h=n^{-1/5}\); if \(2\leq n<4^5\), choose \(h=1/4\).  In both cases the product divergence is at most \(1/240\), and hence the product total variation is less than \(1/2\) by (10).  For any estimator \(T\), pointwise triangle inequality integrated against the common part of the two product laws gives
\[
 \max_{j\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb E_{P_{j,h}^n}|T-\theta(P_{j,h})|
 \geq\frac{|\theta(P_{1,h})-\theta(P_{0,h})|}{2}
       \{1-\operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h}^n,P_{1,h}^n)\}.
\]
Equations (8)--(10) yield a constant multiple of \(n^{-2/5}\); in the finite range \(2\leq n<4^5\), use \(n^{-2/5}\leq1\) and the fixed separation \(1/128\).

Finally suppose \(0<\epsilon\leq1/10\) and put \(h=\{\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)\}^{1/4}/4\).  If \(t=\operatorname{TV}(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})\), (8) gives
\[
 t=\frac{1}{96\,4^4}\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
 \leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}.
\]
Writing the Jordan decomposition as \(P_{1,h}-P_{0,h}=t(S_+-S_-)\), set \(c=(1-\epsilon)t/\epsilon\leq1\) and use the contaminants in \(\texttt{def:lipschitz-lower-pair}\).  They are probability laws, and direct subtraction gives
\[
 (1-\epsilon)(P_{0,h}-P_{1,h})+\epsilon(Q_{0,h}-Q_{1,h})
 =-(1-\epsilon)t(S_+-S_-)+\epsilon c(S_+-S_-)=0.
\]
Thus both contaminated experiments have the same observed law \(R_h\), while (8) gives target separation
\[
 \frac{h^2}{8}=\frac1{128}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
At \(\epsilon=0\) the contamination separation is zero and the asserted contamination lower component is vacuous; the clean construction above remains valid.

%%% FIELD proof-lipschitz-calibration
We prove the upper bound for the explicit estimator in \(\texttt{def:lipschitz-estimator}\), then the same-experiment lower bound.  Clipping cannot increase absolute error because \(\theta(P)\in[-1/2,1/2]\), so it suffices to analyze the expression inside the clipping map.  Assume first that \(b_n\leq1/4\).  The dyadic choice implies
\[
 b_n\leq h_n<2b_n,\qquad h_n\leq\frac12. \tag{11}
\]

For a cell \(D\) and arm \(a\), write
\[
 p_a(x)=\begin{cases}e(x),&a=1,\\1-e(x),&a=0,\end{cases}
 \quad p_a^P(D)=\int_Dp_a(x)\,\mathrm dx,
 \quad m_a^P(D)=\frac{\int_Dp_a(x)\mu_a(x)\,\mathrm dx}{p_a^P(D)}.
\]
If \(\overline\mu_a(D)=|D|^{-1}\int_D\mu_a\), the exact covariance identity under the uniform distribution on \(D\) is
\[
 m_a^P(D)-\overline\mu_a(D)
 =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{\operatorname{Unif}(D)}(p_a,\mu_a)}
        {\mathbb E_{\operatorname{Unif}(D)}p_a}. \tag{12}
\]
Consider \(D\subseteq[r,2r]\) of length \(\ell\).  From \(e(x)=x^2u(x)\), the calibration envelope, and the \(L\)-Lipschitz property of \(u\),
\[
 \inf_D e\geq\frac{r^2}{4},\qquad
 \operatorname{osc}_D(e)\leq C_Lr\ell,\qquad
 \operatorname{osc}_D(\mu_a)\leq L\ell,\qquad
 \inf_D(1-e)\geq\frac12. \tag{13}
\]
Indeed, \(|x^2u(x)-y^2u(y)|\leq|x^2-y^2|/2+y^2L|x-y|\), and \(x,y\in[r,2r]\).  Equations (12)--(13) imply for both arms
\[
 |m_a^P(D)-\overline\mu_a(D)|\leq C_L\frac{\ell^2}{r}. \tag{14}
\]
Here the sharper control bound is no larger than the displayed common bound because \(r\leq1\).

For a slab of length \(r_j\), its cell length \(\ell_j=r_j/M_j\) obeys
\[
 \frac{h_n\sqrt{r_j}}2\leq\ell_j\leq h_n\sqrt{r_j}. \tag{15}
\]
The upper inequality follows from the ceiling in \(M_j\); the lower one follows from \(M_j\leq2\sqrt{r_j}/h_n\), valid because \(r_j\geq h_n\) and \(h_n\leq1/2\).  Summing (14) within a slab and then over slabs gives
\[
 \sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n}|D|\frac{\ell_D^2}{r_j}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\ell_j^2
 \leq h_n^2\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}r_j
 \leq h_n^2. \tag{16}
\]
For \(t\in[0,h_n]\) and \(x\in B_n=[h_n,2h_n]\), outcome Lipschitzness gives \(|\mu_a(t)-\mu_a(x)|\leq2Lh_n\).  Since \(m_a^P(B_n)\) is a convex average of \(\mu_a(x)\) over \(B_n\),
\[
 \left|\int_0^{h_n}\mu_a(t)\,\mathrm dt-h_nm_a^P(B_n)\right|
 \leq2Lh_n^2. \tag{17}
\]
Equations (16)--(17), for the two arms, prove a clean population approximation bias at most \(C_Lh_n^2\).

We next compare clean and contaminated cell means.  Put
\[
 q_{D,a}=Q\{X\in D,A=a\},\qquad q_D=Q\{X\in D\},
\]
and let \(m_a^R(D)\) be the conditional mean under \(R\).  Expanding the mixture ratio from \(\texttt{ass:huber-mixture}\), using \(0\leq Y\leq1\), gives
\[
 |m_a^R(D)-m_a^P(D)|
 \leq\frac{\epsilon q_{D,a}}
 {(1-\epsilon)p_a^P(D)+\epsilon q_{D,a}}
 \leq\frac{\epsilon q_{D,a}}{(1-\epsilon)p_a^P(D)}. \tag{18}
\]
For a body cell in \([r_j,2r_j]\), (13) gives
\[
 p_1^P(D)\geq r_j^2|D|/4,\qquad p_0^P(D)\geq|D|/2.
\]
Thus its weighted shift is at most \(C\epsilon q_D/r_j^2\) for either arm.  The body cells are disjoint and \(\sum_Dq_D\leq1\).  Moreover,
\[
 p_1^P(B_n)\geq h_n^3/4,\qquad p_0^P(B_n)\geq h_n/2,
\]
so the boundary term has weighted shift at most \(C\epsilon q_{B_n}/h_n^2\).  Although \(B_n\) is reused, it is charged only once more and \(q_{B_n}\leq1\).  Summing (18) therefore proves a total population contamination shift at most
\[
 C\epsilon h_n^{-2}. \tag{19}
\]
This argument uses only the single global \(Q\)-mass budget and hence applies equally to point masses and to diffuse contamination within a cell, including corruption of \(X\), \(A\), and \(Y\).

It remains to control random denominators.  By \(\texttt{ass:iid-sampling}\), a count in a measurable cell of \(R\)-probability \(p\) has distribution \(N\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)\).  Binomial expansion and integration of \((1-p+pt)^n\) over \(t\in[0,1]\) give
\[
 \mathbb E\frac1{N+1}=\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1}}{(n+1)p},\qquad
 \mathbb E\left\{\frac{\mathbf1\{N>0\}}N\right\}
 \leq2\mathbb E\frac1{N+1}\leq\frac2{(n+1)p}. \tag{20}
\]
Conditional on all categorical cell counts, each nonempty sample mean is centered at its \(R\)-conditional cell mean; different disjoint cells have zero conditional cross-covariances.  Since a \([0,1]\)-valued response has variance at most \(1/4\), (20), Jensen's inequality, and \(p_a^R(D)\geq(1-\epsilon)p_a^P(D)\) imply that the expected absolute centered body fluctuation for the treated arm is at most
\[
 \left\{\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n}
 \frac{|D|^2}{2(n+1)p_1^R(D)}\right\}^{1/2}
 \leq C\left\{\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\frac1{r_j}\right\}^{1/2}
 \leq C(nh_n)^{-1/2}. \tag{21}
\]
The control-arm body fluctuation is no larger.  The boundary treated mean contributes at most \(Ch_n(nh_n^3)^{-1/2}=C(nh_n)^{-1/2}\), and the control boundary contribution is smaller.  We used the triangle inequality between the body and boundary terms, so reuse of \(B_n\) requires no independence assertion.

The zero-count fallback creates only an exponentially small remainder.  From (15), every treated body cell has clean mass at least
\[
 \frac{r_j^2\ell_j}{4}\geq\frac{h_nr_j^{5/2}}8\geq\frac{h_n^{7/2}}8.
\]
Control cells have larger mass, and the treated boundary cell has mass at least \(h_n^3/4\).  Since \(\epsilon\leq1/10\), every corresponding observed cell mass is at least a fixed multiple of \(h_n^{7/2}\).  The probability of an empty cell is at most \(\exp(-cnh_n^{7/2})\).  The nonnegative body weights sum to \(1-h_n\) for each arm and the boundary weight is \(h_n\); hence summing weighted empty-cell errors, without a union bound over the number of cells, gives
\[
 C\exp(-cnh_n^{7/2}). \tag{22}
\]
Combining (16)--(22) establishes the stated pre-optimization inequality.

By definition of \(b_n\) and (11),
\[
 nh_n^5\geq1,\qquad \epsilon\leq h_n^4,\qquad
 h_n^2\leq4\max\{n^{-2/5},\epsilon^{1/2}\}. \tag{23}
\]
Thus \((nh_n)^{-1/2}\leq h_n^2\) and \(\epsilon h_n^{-2}\leq h_n^2\).  Also
\[
 nh_n^{7/2}=(nh_n^5)h_n^{-3/2}\geq h_n^{-3/2},
\]
and \(\sup_{0<h\leq1/2}h^{-2}\exp(-ch^{-3/2})<\infty\), so (22) is at most \(C h_n^2\).  This proves the upper rate in the branch \(b_n\leq1/4\).  If \(b_n>1/4\), the estimator returns zero and has error at most \(1/2\).  In that branch either \(n^{-2/5}>1/16\) or \(\epsilon^{1/2}>1/16\), so the same upper rate holds after increasing \(C_0\).

For the lower bound, taking \(Q=P\) makes \(R=P\), so the contaminated supremum contains the clean experiment.  The clean part of \(\texttt{lem:lipschitz-lower-pair}\) therefore gives \(\mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;1,1,1,2,L)\geq c n^{-2/5}\).  For \(\epsilon>0\), its collision construction supplies two admissible triples having the same observed law and target separation \(128^{-1}\{\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)\}^{1/2}\).  For every estimator, pointwise triangle inequality under that common law makes the larger of the two risks at least half the separation.  Hence \(\mathfrak R_n\geq c\epsilon^{1/2}\); at \(\epsilon=0\) this is vacuous.  The maximum of these two lower bounds is at least half their sum, proving the first displayed inequality of the theorem.

Finally, the number of cells is
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{J-1}M_j
 \leq h_n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\sqrt{r_j}+J=O(h_n^{-1}).
\]
Constructing their endpoints costs \(O(h_n^{-1})\) operations and memory.  Binary search assigns each of the \(n\) records in \(O(\log(1/h_n))\) operations, after which all arm counts and sums are accumulated in one pass.  Every operation is a finite Borel comparison, sum, product, or zero-safe quotient, followed by clipping; thus \(T_{\mathrm{Lip}}\in\mathfrak T_n\).  The definition of the infimum in \(\texttt{def:minimax-risk}\) then gives \(\mathfrak R_n\leq\sup_{(P,Q,R)}\mathbb E_{R^n}|T_{\mathrm{Lip}}-\theta(P)|\).  This proves the stated arithmetic and memory bounds and completes the theorem.

%%% FIELD oeq-full-frontier-partial
The proved same-experiment facts imply, uniformly for \(0<\epsilon\leq1/10\),
\[
 \mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)
 \geq \mathfrak R_n(0;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \mathfrak R_n(\epsilon;d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa,L)
 \geq c\left(\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{(\beta+1)/(\beta+\kappa+1)}.
\]
The first inequality follows by taking \(Q=P\).  For the second, use the bump pair in the proof of \(\texttt{thm:ate-tv-modulus}\), choose its total variation at most \(\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)\), and apply the explicit Jordan-decomposition collision calculation from \(\texttt{lem:lipschitz-lower-pair}\); the two observed laws then coincide and pointwise triangle inequality gives half the target separation.  For \(\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)\leq\delta_0\), choose equality in the total-variation constraint.  Above that threshold, retain the fixed pair at radius \(\delta_0\) and decrease the constant, which is legitimate because \(\epsilon\leq1/10\).  Moreover, when \((d,\alpha,\beta,\kappa)=(1,1,1,2)\), \(\texttt{thm:lipschitz-calibration}\) proves the complete matched rate \(n^{-2/5}+\epsilon^{1/2}\), so simultaneous attainment is established in that regime.  No corresponding general upper bound or matching interaction lower bound has been derived.

%%% FIELD statement-lipschitz-lower-pair
For every \(0<h\leq1/4\), both members of \(P_h^{\mathrm{pair}}=(P_{0,h},P_{1,h})\) belong to \(\mathcal P_{1,1,1,2,L}\), and
\[
