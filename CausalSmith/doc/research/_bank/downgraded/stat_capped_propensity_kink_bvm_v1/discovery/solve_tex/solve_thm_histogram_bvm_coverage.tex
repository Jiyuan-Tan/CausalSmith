%%% FIELD proof-regularity-frontier
Put \(f(x)=\mu _0(x)+\tau(x)q_\delta(1\mid x)\).  First note that \(K_\delta(P)=0\) is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 \tau(X)=0\quad P\text{-almost surely on }\{e(X)=c\},                                      \tag{1}
\end{equation}
because the integrand in the definition of \(K_\delta(P)\) is nonnegative.

We prove sufficiency without an overlap assumption.  Let \(\{P_t:|t|<t_0\}\) be an arbitrary two-sided dominated quadratic-mean differentiable submodel through \(P=P_0\), with score \(S\in L_2^0(P)\).  If \(p_t=dP_t/d\nu\) for a common dominating measure, quadratic-mean differentiability says
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt{p_t}=\sqrt p+\frac t2S\sqrt p+r_t,\qquad \|r_t\|_{L_2(\nu)}=o(|t|).
\end{equation}
Squaring and applying Cauchy--Schwarz to every term containing \(r_t\) gives
\begin{equation}
 \|p_t-p-tpS\|_{L_1(\nu)}=o(|t|).                                                        \tag{2}
\end{equation}
Thus every bounded marginal or first-moment measure obtained from \(P_t\) has an \(L_1\) first-order expansion.

To handle propensity endpoints explicitly, dominate the covariate marginal measures and write, at a given \(x\),
\begin{equation}
 p=dP_X/d\nu_X,\quad a=dP(X\in dx,A=1)/d\nu_X,\quad
 b_a=d\,E_P[Y\mathbf 1\{A=a,X\in dx\}]/d\nu_X.
\end{equation}
The vector \(z=(p,a,b_1,b_0)\) lies in the moment cone
\begin{equation}
 p\ge0,\qquad 0\le a\le p,\qquad |b_1|\le Ma,\qquad |b_0|\le M(p-a).                    \tag{3}
\end{equation}
The contribution of \(x\) to \(\Psi_\delta\) is the following continuous, positively homogeneous function on this cone:
\begin{equation}
 F(z)=
 \begin{cases}
 \displaystyle \delta b_1+\frac{(p-\delta a)b_0}{p-a},&a<cp,\\[6pt]
 \displaystyle \frac{pb_1}{a},&a\ge cp, p>0,\\[4pt]
 0,&p=0.
 \end{cases}                                                                            \tag{4}
\end{equation}
Indeed, substituting \(e=a/p\), \(\mu _1=b_1/a\), and \(\mu _0=b_0/(p-a)\) into the defining functional yields (4).  On the lower cone, \(p-a\ge(1-c)p\); on the upper cone, \(a\ge cp\).  Direct differentiation, using (3), shows that every partial derivative of either branch is bounded by a constant depending only on \(M\) and \(\delta\).  For example,
\begin{equation}
 \partial_aF_-=-\frac{(\delta-1)p\mu _0}{p-a},\qquad
 \partial_pF_-=\frac{(\delta-1)a\mu _0}{p-a},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 \partial_aF_+=-\frac{\mu _1}{e},\qquad \partial_pF_+=\mu _1,qquad
 \partial_{b_1}F_+=\frac1e.
\end{equation}
The remaining derivatives are \(\partial_{b_1}F_-=\delta\) and
\(\partial_{b_0}F_-=(1-\delta e)/(1-e)\).  A line segment in (3) crosses \(a=cp\) at most once, and the two formulas in (4) agree there.  Integrating the branchwise derivative bounds along the segment therefore proves
\begin{equation}
 |F(z)-F(z')|\le C_{M,\delta}\|z-z'\|_1                                                 \tag{5}
\end{equation}
for all two moment vectors in the cone, including the origin by homogeneity.

At a boundary point \(a=cp>0\), the two gradients, in the coordinate order \((p,a,b_1,b_0)\), are
\begin{equation}
 (\mu _0,-\delta\mu _0,\delta,0)\quad\hbox{and}\quad
 (\mu _1,-\delta\mu _1,\delta,0).                                                       \tag{6}
\end{equation}
They agree precisely when \(\tau=0\).  At a point with \(p=0\), every two-sided feasible derivative in the cone is zero: nonnegativity for positive and negative \(t\) first forces \(\dot p=0\), and (3) then forces \(\dot a=\dot b_0=\dot b_1=0\).  Equations (1), (5), and (6), together with the \(L_1\) expansion (2), permit dominated convergence of the difference quotient of \(\int F(z_t)\,d\nu_X\).  Hence every such submodel has a linear derivative.

For completeness, write \(S_X=E_P[S\mid X]\).  Conditional differentiation gives
\(\dot e=E_P[(A-e)S\mid X]\), and, on a supported arm,
\(\dot\mu_a=E_P[(Y-\mu_a)S\mid A=a,X]\).  Substitution into the derivative just obtained yields, term by term,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \dot\Psi_\delta(P)[S]
 &=E_P[(f(X)-\Psi_\delta(P))S]
   +E_P\!\left[Aw_1(e(X))(Y-\mu _1(X))S\right]\\
 &\quad+E_P\!\left[(1-A)w_0(e(X))(Y-\mu _0(X))S\right]
   +\delta E_P\!\left[\mathbf1\{e(X)<c\}\tau(X)(A-e(X))S\right]\\
 &=E_P[D_P S].
\end{aligned}                                                                             \tag{7}
\end{equation}
At \(e=0\) the treated residual has zero probability, and at \(e=1\) the control residual has zero probability; thus (7) never divides by the probability of an unsupported observed arm.  At \(e=c\), (1) makes the choice of one-sided derivative immaterial.

The unrestricted dominated tangent space has closure \(L_2^0(P)\): for any bounded mean-zero \(S\), the laws with density \(1+tS\) are legal for sufficiently small \(|t|\), and truncation and centering approximate every member of \(L_2^0(P)\).  Consequently (7) identifies \(D_P\) as the canonical gradient.  Moreover \(0\le w_1\le\delta\), \(0\le w_0\le1\), \(|Y-\mu_a|\le2M\), and \(|\tau|\le2M\).  Hence
\begin{equation}
 |D_P|\le (4+4\delta)M                                                                    \tag{8}
\end{equation}
is one valid uniform bound, and the efficiency bound is \(E_P[D_P^2]=V_P\).

For necessity, suppose \(K_\delta(P)>0\).  At least one of
\(B_+=\{e=c,\tau>0\}\) and \(B_-=\{e=c,\tau<0\}\) has
\(\kappa_B=E_P[\tau(X)\mathbf1_B(X)]\ne0\).  Choose that \(B\), keep \(P_X\) and both conditional outcome laws fixed, and put \(e_t=e+t\mathbf1_B\) for
\(|t|<\frac12\min(c,1-c)\).  Its likelihood ratio is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dP_t}{dP}=1+tS_B,\qquad
 S_B=\mathbf1_B(X)\frac{A-c}{c(1-c)},                                                     \tag{9}
\end{equation}
so it is a legal two-sided quadratic-mean differentiable submodel with bounded score.  Only \(B\) changes the target, and therefore
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_\delta(P_t)-\Psi_\delta(P)
 =\kappa_B\{\min(1,1+\delta t)-1\}
 =\begin{cases}\delta t\kappa_B,&t<0,\\0,&t>0.\end{cases}                              \tag{10}
\end{equation}
The two derivatives disagree.  Thus pathwise differentiability fails.

Finally, a regular asymptotically linear estimator with influence function \(\phi\) would, under the local version \(t=h/\sqrt n\) of (9), have a local centering shift linear in \(h\), namely \(hE_P[\phi S_B]\).  This follows directly by expanding
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^n\log(1+hS_B(O_i)/\sqrt n)
 =h\mathbb G_nS_B-\tfrac12h^2E_P[S_B^2]+o_P(1)
\end{equation}
and exponentially tilting the joint central limit theorem for
\(n^{-1/2}\sum_i(\phi(O_i),S_B(O_i))\).  Regular centering at
\(\Psi_\delta(P_{h/\sqrt n})\) would therefore require the local target shift to be the same linear function of \(h\).  Equation (10) is not linear because \(\kappa_B\ne0\), which proves the final assertion.

%%% FIELD proof-legal-finite-partition-tangent
Since \(K_\delta(P)>0\), the nonnegative random variable
\(\tau(X)^2\mathbf1\{e(X)=c\}\) is positive with positive probability.  Decompose its support into
\(B_+=\{e=c,\tau>0\}\) and \(B_-=\{e=c,\tau<0\}\).  If both
\(E_P[\tau\mathbf1_{B_+}]\) and \(E_P[\tau\mathbf1_{B_-}]\) were zero, positivity and negativity on the respective sets would imply that \(\tau=0\) almost surely on \(\{e=c\}\), contradicting \(K_\delta(P)>0\).  Choose a set \(B\) for which
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_B:=E_P[\tau(X)\mathbf1_B(X)]\ne0.                                                 \tag{11}
\end{equation}

For \(|t|<\frac12\min(c,1-c)\), define \(P_t\) by leaving \(P_X\) and the conditional law of \(Y\) given \((A,X)\) unchanged and using propensity
\(e_t=e+t\mathbf1_B\).  On \(B\), both Bernoulli probabilities remain strictly between zero and one; off \(B\), the law is unchanged.  Consequently \(P_t\) is dominated by \(P\), and a direct calculation for \(A=0,1\) gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dP_t}{dP}=1+t\mathbf1_B(X)\frac{A-c}{c(1-c)}=1+tS_B.                              \tag{12}
\end{equation}
The score is bounded and is measurable with respect to the finite partition generated by \(B\) and the two treatment values.  Since
\(E_P[S_B]=0\), expansion of \(\sqrt{1+tS_B}\) proves quadratic-mean differentiability with score \(S_B\).

The outcome regressions and \(P_X\) do not vary, so the definition of the target yields
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_\delta(P_t)-\Psi_\delta(P)
 =E_P\!\left[\tau(X)\mathbf1_B(X)
 \{\min(1,\delta(c+t))-1\}\right].                                                       \tag{13}
\end{equation}
Because \(\delta c=1\), (13) equals \(0\) for \(t>0\) and
\(\delta t\kappa_B\) for \(t<0\).  The right derivative is therefore zero and the left derivative is \(\delta\kappa_B\ne0\) by (11).  The construction localizes to one sign of \(\tau\), so cancellation between positive and negative cap cells is irrelevant.

%%% FIELD statement-exact-branch-remainder-narrow
For every bounded nuisance candidate \(\eta=(s,u_0,u_1)\), including ties \(s=c\) assigned to the saturated branch, the exact identity is
\begin{equation}
R(P,\eta)=\delta\mathbb E_P\!\left[\tau(X)|e(X)-c|\mathbf 1\!\left\{\mathbf 1\{s(X)<c\}\ne\mathbf 1\{e(X)<c\}\right\}\right]+\mathbb E_P\!\left[(s(X)-e(X))\left\{\mathbf 1\{s(X)<c\}\frac{(\delta-1)(u_0(X)-\mu_0(X))}{1-s(X)}+\mathbf 1\{s(X)\ge c\}\frac{u_1(X)-\mu_1(X)}{s(X)}\right\}\right].
\end{equation}
Every displayed denominator is bounded away from zero on its active branch. Consequently, if \(0<\rho\le c\wedge(1-c)\), \(\|s-e\|_\infty\le\rho\), the branch-relevant outcome error is at most \(\varepsilon\), and \(P\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm m}(\alpha,C_{\mathrm m})\), then
\begin{equation}
 |R(P,\eta)|\le C_{\delta,M,C_{\mathrm m}}\{\rho\varepsilon+\rho^{1+\alpha}\}.
\end{equation}

%%% FIELD proof-exact-branch-remainder
Write \(q(r)=\min(1,\delta r)\), \(I_s=\mathbf1\{s<c\}\),
\(I_e=\mathbf1\{e<c\}\), \(v_a=u_a-\mu_a\), and \(q'(s)=\delta I_s\).  Conditional expectation of the candidate signal, using
\(E[A\mid X]=e\) and \(E[Y- u_a\mid A=a,X]=-v_a\), gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 E_P[m_\eta\mid X]-\{\mu_0+\tau q(e)\}
 &=\tau\{q(s)+q'(s)(e-s)-q(e)\}\\
 &\quad +(e-s)\{(q'(s)-w_1(s))v_1+(w_0(s)-q'(s))v_0\}.
\end{aligned}                                                                             \tag{14}
\end{equation}
Here we used the identities \(q(s)=s w_1(s)\) and
\(1-q(s)=(1-s)w_0(s)\), which follow separately on the two branches from the definition of the weights.

The concave hinge has the exact tangent-gap identity
\begin{equation}
 q(s)+q'(s)(e-s)-q(e)
 =\delta |e-c|\mathbf1\{I_s\ne I_e\}.                                                    \tag{15}
\end{equation}
Indeed it is zero when \(e,s\) share a branch; if \(s<c\le e\) it is
\(\delta(e-c)\), and if \(e<c\le s\) it is \(\delta(c-e)\).  These cases also cover either tie because \(I_s\) and \(I_e\) use strict inequalities.

If \(s<c\), then \(q'(s)=w_1(s)=\delta\) and
\begin{equation}
 w_0(s)-q'(s)=\frac{1-\delta s}{1-s}-\delta
 =-\frac{\delta-1}{1-s}.
\end{equation}
If \(s\ge c\), then \(q'(s)=w_0(s)=0\) and \(w_1(s)=1/s\), including \(s=c\) because \(1/c=\delta\).  Substitution in (14), followed by integration over \(X\), proves the displayed exact identity.  On the lower branch \(1-s>1-c=(\delta-1)/\delta\), while on the saturated branch \(s\ge c=1/\delta\).  Thus both active coefficients have absolute value at most \(\delta\); no undefined inactive quotient is used.

Assume now the hypotheses in the narrowed conclusion and interpret the branch-relevant error as
\begin{equation}
 \|I_s v_0\|_{L_2(P_X)}+\|(1-I_s)v_1\|_{L_2(P_X)}\le\varepsilon.                         \tag{16}
\end{equation}
The second term in the identity is, by the active denominator bounds and Cauchy--Schwarz,
\begin{equation}
 \left|E_P[(s-e)\{I_s(\delta-1)v_0/(1-s)+(1-I_s)v_1/s\}]\right|
 \le\delta\rho\varepsilon.                                                              \tag{17}
\end{equation}
Branch disagreement and \(\|s-e\|_\infty\le\rho\) imply
\(0<|e-c|\le\rho\), except at \(e=c\), where the factor \(|e-c|\) is zero.  Since \(|\tau|\le2M\), the punctured-margin assumption and
\(\rho\le c\wedge(1-c)\) give
\begin{equation}
 \delta E_P[|\tau||e-c|\mathbf1\{I_s\ne I_e\}]
 \le2\delta M\rho\,P\{0<|e-c|\le\rho\}
 \le2\delta M C_{\mathrm m}\rho^{1+\alpha}.                                            \tag{18}
\end{equation}
Combining (17) and (18) proves the claimed rate with, for example,
\(C_{\delta,M,C_{\mathrm m}}=\delta+2\delta M C_{\mathrm m}\).

%%% FIELD proof-sharp-margin-modulus
Under the construction, \(e=c-U\in(c-h,c]\subset(0,1)\), so the Bernoulli treatment law is legal; \(Y=A\in[0,1]\subset[-M,M]\), and hence the bounded-outcome condition holds.  Since \(P(U=0)=0\), there is no propensity atom at \(c\).  For every admissible \(t\),
\begin{equation}
 P_{\mathrm{lf}}\{0<|e(X)-c|\le t\}
 =P(0<U\le t)
 =\begin{cases}(t/h)^\alpha,&t\le h,\\1,&t>h,
 \end{cases}
 \le h^{-\alpha}t^\alpha.                                                               \tag{19}
\end{equation}
Thus \(P_{\mathrm{lf}}\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm m}(\alpha,h^{-\alpha})\).  The candidate satisfies \(s-e=\rho\) everywhere, so
\(\|s-e\|_\infty=\rho\), and it uses \(u_a=\mu_a\), making the propensity--outcome product in the exact remainder zero.

The true propensity is below the cap almost surely.  The candidate is on the saturated branch exactly when \(U\le\rho\), up to the null endpoint convention.  Since \(\tau=1\), the exact remainder identity therefore reduces to
\begin{equation}
 R(P_{\mathrm{lf}},\eta_{\mathrm{lf}})
 =\delta E[U\mathbf1\{U\le\rho\}].                                                     \tag{20}
\end{equation}
The distribution of \(U\) has density \(\alpha u^{\alpha-1}/h^\alpha\) on \((0,h)\).  Because \(\rho<h\), direct integration gives
\begin{equation}
 E[U\mathbf1\{U\le\rho\}]
 =\frac{\alpha}{h^\alpha}\int_0^\rho u^\alpha\,du
 =\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha+1)h^\alpha}\rho^{1+\alpha}.                                   \tag{21}
\end{equation}
Equations (20)--(21) prove the equality.  Any uniform upper modulus of smaller order than \(\rho^{1+\alpha}\) for this same unsmoothed correction and nuisance neighborhood would contradict this member of the class, establishing the stated, procedure-specific sharpness.

%%% FIELD proof-histogram-sieve-equicontinuity
Fix \(P\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{hist}}(b,L)\) and abbreviate \(J=J_n\).  Constants below may depend on \(b,L,\delta\), but not on \(n\) or the bin index.  Since \(J=\lceil n^{1/3}\rceil\), \(J\asymp n^{1/3}\) and \(n/J^2\to\infty\).

We first establish a simultaneous count event.  Uniform design gives \(P_X(B_j)=J^{-1}\), and the arm--bin probabilities are exactly
\begin{equation}
 p_{1j}=\int_{B_j}x\,dx=\frac{2j-1}{2J^2},\qquad
 p_{0j}=\int_{B_j}(1-x)\,dx=\frac{2(J-j)+1}{2J^2}.                                      \tag{22}
\end{equation}
For a binomial variable \(N\) with mean \(\lambda\), the exponential Markov bound applied to \(e^{-tN}\), optimized over \(t>0\), gives
\(P(N\le\lambda/2)\le e^{-\lambda/8}\).  Applying this bound and its upper-tail analogue to \(N_j,N_{1j},N_{0j}\), then taking a union bound, shows that with probability tending to one,
\begin{equation}
 N_j\asymp n/J,\qquad
 N_{1j}\ge \frac{nj}{4J^2},\qquad
 N_{0j}\ge\frac{n(J-j+1)}{4J^2}                                                         \tag{23}
\end{equation}
simultaneously in \(j\).  In particular, the minimum expected and realized arm count has order \(n/J^2\), rather than \(n/J\), and still diverges.

We use the following elementary beta tail calculation.  If \(Z\mid K,m\sim\mathrm{Beta}(K+1,m-K+1)\), integration of the beta density gives
\begin{equation}
 P(Z\ge z\mid K,m)=P\{\mathrm{Bin}(m+1,z)\le K\},qquad z\in(0,1),                     \tag{24}
\end{equation}
with the analogous lower-tail identity.  Applying exponential Markov to the binomial variable on the right of (24), and using
\(\log(1+v)\le v\), proves that for universal constants \(C_0,c_0>0\),
\begin{equation}
 P\!\left(\left.|Z-K/m|>C_0\{\sqrt{x/m}+x/m\}\,\right|K,m\right)
 \le 2e^{-c_0x}                                                                          \tag{25}
\end{equation}
for \(m\ge1\), with the evident interpretation at \(K=0,m\).  This derivation also covers posterior parameters at the endpoints and does not assume an interior empirical proportion.

Conditional on the covariates in bin \(j\), Hoeffding's exponential-moment argument for the independent treatment indicators gives, simultaneously in \(j\),
\begin{equation}
 \left|\frac1{N_j}\sum_{i:X_i\in B_j}A_i-\frac1{N_j}\sum_{i:X_i\in B_j}X_i\right|
 \le C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{N_j}}                                                         \tag{26}
\end{equation}
outside an event of probability \(o(1)\).  Every point in a bin is within \(J^{-1}\) of every other point in that bin.  Combining (23), (25) with \(x=C\log n\), (26), and a union bound over bins shows that, on a frequentist event of probability tending to one, the propensity posterior assigns probability tending to one to
\begin{equation}
 \|s-e\|_\infty\le C\left\{J^{-1}+\sqrt{J\log n/n}\right\}=C\rho_n.                   \tag{27}
\end{equation}

For the outcome coordinates, condition on the arm--bin memberships and covariates.  The observed outcomes in a cell are independent Bernoulli variables.  Their conditional means differ from any fixed value \(\mu_a(x_j)\), \(x_j\in B_j\), by at most \(L/J\).  Hoeffding's bound and (25), now with \(m=N_{aj}\), imply that the joint posterior assigns probability tending to one to
\begin{equation}
 |u_{aj}-\mu_a(x)|
 \le C\left\{J^{-1}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{N_{aj}}}+\frac{\log n}{N_{aj}}\right\},
 \qquad x\in B_j,                                                                        \tag{28}
\end{equation}
simultaneously for both arms and all bins.  The total zero-count fallback is harmless because (23) implies that every \(N_{aj}\) is positive on the event being used.

Integrating the square of (28), invoking (23), and using
\(\sum_{k=1}^Jk^{-1}\le1+\log J\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^Jk^{-2}\le2\), gives for each arm
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \|u_a-\mu_a\|_{L_2(P_X)}^2
 &\le C\left\{J^{-2}+\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{\log n}{N_{aj}}
                   +\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{(\log n)^2}{N_{aj}^2}\right\}\\
 &\le C\left\{J^{-2}+\frac{J(\log n)^2}{n}
                   +\frac{J^3(\log n)^2}{n^2}\right\}
 \le C\varepsilon_n^2.
\end{aligned}                                                                             \tag{29}
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large \(n\), \(c+C\rho_n<(1+c)/2\).  Hence every control cell meeting \(\{x\le c+C\rho_n\}\) has control probability bounded below by \((1-c)/2\), and (23) can be sharpened there to \(N_{0j}\ge C^{-1}n/J\).  Similarly, every treated cell meeting \(\{x\ge c-C\rho_n\}\) has \(N_{1j}\ge C^{-1}n/J\).  Equation (28) consequently yields
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{x\le c+C\rho_n}|u_0(x)-\mu_0(x)|
 +\sup_{x\ge c-C\rho_n}|u_1(x)-\mu_1(x)|\le C\rho_n.                                  \tag{30}
\end{equation}
Choosing the fixed constant in \(H_n\) larger than the constants in (27), (29), and (30), and using the union bounds implicit in (25), proves
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_n(H_n\mid O_1,\ldots,O_n)\longrightarrow1
\end{equation}
in \(P\)-probability.  This establishes joint contraction despite the unequal random arm counts.

It remains to prove the asserted empirical-process statement.  Put
\(I_s(x)=\mathbf1\{s(x)<c\}\), \(I_e(x)=\mathbf1\{x<c\}\), and
\(Z(O)=(1,A,Y,AY)\).  The two exact branch representations of the signal are
\begin{equation}
 m_\eta=
 \begin{cases}
 \delta AY+(1-\delta A)u_0+(1-A)w_0(s)(Y-u_0),&I_s=1,\\
 u_1+(A/s)(Y-u_1),&I_s=0.
 \end{cases}                                                                             \tag{31}
\end{equation}
On the first branch, \(1-s\ge1-c\); on the second, \(s\ge c\).  The coefficient maps in (31) are therefore Lipschitz, with constants depending only on \(\delta\).  By (27) and (30), the candidate coefficients differ from their true coefficients by \(O(\rho_n)\) whenever candidate and truth use the same branch.

If the branches differ, (27) forces \(|x-c|\le C\rho_n\).  At \(x=c\), direct subtraction of the two true branch formulas in (31) equals
\(\delta\tau(x)(A-c)\).  Lipschitz continuity of \(x\), \(\mu_0(x)\), and \(\mu_1(x)\), together with the branch denominator bounds, therefore gives the following explicit finite-basis decomposition, obtained by freezing the coefficients at one point in every bin:
\begin{equation}
 m_\eta-m_P
 =\delta\tau(X)(A-c)(I_s-I_e)+r_n+
   \sum_{j=1}^J\sum_{k=1}^4 a_{jk}(\eta)Z_k(O)\mathbf1\{X\in B_j\},                    \tag{32}
\end{equation}
where \(r_n\) is deterministic, \(\|r_n\|_{L_2(P)}\le C/J\), and
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}\max_{j,k}|a_{jk}(\eta)|\le C(\rho_n+J^{-1}).                      \tag{33}
\end{equation}
To verify (32), freeze separately the lower and upper coefficients in (31).  Their within-bin oscillation is \(O(J^{-1})\).  Subtract the candidate coefficient on its selected branch, which costs \(O(\rho_n)\) by (27) and (30).  On a differing branch subtract the boundary jump \(\delta\tau(A-c)\); the residual costs \(O(|x-c|+J^{-1})=O(\rho_n+J^{-1})\).  Each remaining frozen expression is a linear combination of the four displayed coordinates of \(Z\), proving (32)--(33) equation by equation from (31).

Only bins intersecting \([c-C\rho_n,c+C\rho_n]\) can support \(I_s-I_e\).  Their deterministic number \(B_n\) obeys
\begin{equation}
 B_n\le2JC\rho_n+4.                                                                      \tag{34}
\end{equation}
For \(h(O)=\tau(X)(A-c)\) and their union \(H\), the triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}|\mathbb G_n\{h(I_s-I_e)\}|
 \le\sum_{j:B_j\subset H}|\mathbb G_n(h\mathbf1_{B_j})|
     +|\mathbb G_n(hI_e\mathbf1_H)|.                                                     \tag{35}
\end{equation}
The iid assumption and Cauchy--Schwarz imply
\(E_P|\mathbb G_ng|\le\|g\|_{L_2(P)}\).  Since \(h\) is bounded and every bin has mass \(1/J\), taking expectations in (35) and using (34) yields
\begin{equation}
 E_P\sup_{\eta\in H_n}|\mathbb G_n\{h(I_s-I_e)\}|
 \le C\left\{\sqrt J\rho_n+J^{-1/2}+\sqrt{\rho_n+J^{-1}}\right\}=o(1).               \tag{36}
\end{equation}
Likewise, (33) gives
\begin{equation}
 E_P\sup_{\eta\in H_n}\left|
 \mathbb G_n\sum_{j,k}a_{jk}(\eta)Z_k\mathbf1_{B_j}\right|
 \le C(\rho_n+J^{-1})\sum_{j,k}\|Z_k\mathbf1_{B_j}\|_2
 \le C(\rho_n+J^{-1})\sqrt J=o(1).                                                      \tag{37}
\end{equation}
Finally \(\mathbb G_nr_n=o_P(1)\) because its variance is at most
\(\|r_n\|_2^2=O(J^{-2})\).  Markov's inequality applied to (36)--(37) and (32) proves the outer-probability equicontinuity assertion.  Notice that no branch of a posterior draw was fixed in this argument.

The rate assertion follows directly from \(J\asymp n^{1/3}\):
\begin{equation}
 \rho_n=O(n^{-1/3}\sqrt{\log n}),\qquad
 \varepsilon_n=O(n^{-1/3}\log n),
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n(\rho_n\varepsilon_n+\rho_n^2)
 =O(n^{-1/6}(\log n)^{3/2})\longrightarrow0.                                             \tag{38}
\end{equation}

%%% FIELD proof-histogram-bvm-coverage
Fix a law in the stated class.  Uniform design and \(e(x)=x\) imply
\(P\{e(X)=c\}=0\), so \(K_\delta(P)=0\).  They also give the punctured margin bound
\begin{equation}
 P\{0<|e(X)-c|\le t\}\le2t                                                            \tag{39}
\end{equation}
for every sufficiently small \(t\).  By the exact remainder identity, the definition of \(H_n\), (39), and the rate conclusion of the histogram theorem,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}\sqrt n\,|P(m_\eta-m_P)|
 \le C\sqrt n(\rho_n\varepsilon_n+\rho_n^2)=o(1).                                     \tag{40}
\end{equation}
Together with histogram equicontinuity, (40) implies
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}\sqrt n\,|\mathbb P_n(m_\eta-m_P)|=o_P(1).                       \tag{41}
\end{equation}

We also need the weighted empirical term.  The branch formulas and branch-relevant uniform bounds used in (31)--(34) imply the pointwise envelope
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}|m_\eta(O)-m_P(O)|
 \le C\{\rho_n+\mathbf1(|X-c|\le C\rho_n)\}.                                          \tag{42}
\end{equation}
The empirical mass of the deterministic shell in (42) converges to its probability, which is \(O(\rho_n)\), up to an \(o_P(1)\) term.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\eta\in H_n}\mathbb P_n(m_\eta-m_P)^2=o_P(1).                                 \tag{43}
\end{equation}

Conditionally on the data, Dirichlet weights have
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Var}_W\!\left(\sqrt n\sum_{i=1}^n(W_i-n^{-1})d_i\right)
 =\frac n{n+1}\{\mathbb P_nd^2-(\mathbb P_nd)^2\}.                                    \tag{44}
\end{equation}
Apply (44), (43), and conditional Markov inequality, then integrate over
\(\eta\sim\Pi_n\).  Since the histogram theorem gives \(\Pi_n(H_n\mid O^n)\to1\), while every signal is bounded uniformly over all beta draws by a constant depending only on \(\delta\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\sum_{i=1}^n(W_i-n^{-1})\{m_\eta(O_i)-m_P(O_i)\}=o_{P,\Pi_n,W}(1).             \tag{45}
\end{equation}

It remains to identify the leading conditional law.  Represent the Dirichlet weights as
\(W_i=E_i/\sum_{k=1}^nE_k\), where, conditionally on the data, the \(E_i\) are independent standard exponential variables.  With
\(z_i=m_P(O_i)\) and \(\bar z=\mathbb P_nm_P\), the exact identity is
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\sum_i(W_i-n^{-1})z_i
 =\frac{n}{\sum_iE_i}\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i(E_i-1)(z_i-\bar z).                         \tag{46}
\end{equation}
The multiplier law of large numbers makes the first factor tend conditionally to one.  Because the \(z_i\) are uniformly bounded, expansion of the conditional characteristic function of the second factor in (46), using
\(E(E_i-1)=0\), \(E(E_i-1)^2=1\), and a uniform third-moment remainder, gives a centered normal limit with variance
\(\mathbb P_n(z_i-\bar z)^2\).  The iid law of large numbers and
\(Pm_P=\Psi_\delta(P)\) give
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb P_n(z_i-\bar z)^2\longrightarrow
 P\{m_P-\Psi_\delta(P)\}^2=V_P.                                                        \tag{47}
\end{equation}
Thus (46) converges conditionally, in bounded-Lipschitz distance and in \(P\)-probability, to \(N(0,V_P)\).

Finally decompose the corrected draw exactly as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \sqrt n\{\Psi_\delta^*-\mathbb P_nm_P\}
 &=\sqrt n\sum_i(W_i-n^{-1})m_P(O_i)\\
 &\quad+\sqrt n\sum_i(W_i-n^{-1})\{m_\eta(O_i)-m_P(O_i)\}
       +\sqrt n\mathbb P_n(m_\eta-m_P).
\end{aligned}                                                                             \tag{48}
\end{equation}
The last two terms are negligible by (41) and (45), uniformly on posterior mass tending to one; the complement changes bounded-Lipschitz expectations by at most twice its posterior probability.  Equations (46)--(48) prove the asserted conditional Bernstein--von Mises limit.

Since \(D_P=m_P-\Psi_\delta(P)\) is bounded and has positive variance, the ordinary iid characteristic-function proof gives
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\{\mathbb P_nm_P-\Psi_\delta(P)\}\ \rightsquigarrow\ N(0,V_P).                \tag{49}
\end{equation}
Bounded-Lipschitz convergence to a normal law with \(V_P>0\) implies convergence of each fixed posterior quantile.  If \(z_r\) is the \(r\)-quantile of \(N(0,1)\), the endpoints of \(C_n^\gamma\) are consequently
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb P_nm_P+n^{-1/2}\sqrt{V_P}\,z_{\gamma/2}+o_P(n^{-1/2}),\qquad
 \mathbb P_nm_P+n^{-1/2}\sqrt{V_P}\,z_{1-\gamma/2}+o_P(n^{-1/2}).                      \tag{50}
\end{equation}
Combining (49), (50), and \(z_{\gamma/2}=-z_{1-\gamma/2}\) yields
\(P\{\Psi_\delta(P)\in C_n^\gamma\}\to1-\gamma\).

Finally, \(e(x)=x\) has essential infimum zero and \(1-e(x)=1-x\) also has essential infimum zero, so no positive strong-overlap constant exists.  The beta posterior has full support on \((0,1)\) in every bin with positive parameters; the construction imposes no side-of-cap restriction, and the proof above explicitly retained the changing indicators in (32)--(36).

%%% FIELD proof-smooth-special-case
If \(P\{e(X)=c\}=0\), then the indicator in the definition of \(K_\delta(P)\) vanishes almost surely, and hence \(K_\delta(P)=0\).  The regularity theorem therefore applies.

Away from \(c\), the map \(q(r)=\min(1,\delta r)\) is differentiable with
\(q'(r)=\delta\mathbf1\{r<c\}\).  The ordinary conditional differentiation formula for
\(E[\mu_0+\tau q(e)]\), derived by differentiating the covariate marginal, the two outcome regressions, and the propensity in turn, is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 &\mu_0(X)+\tau(X)q(e(X))-\Psi_\delta(P)\\
 &\quad+A\frac{q(e(X))}{e(X)}\{Y-\mu_1(X)\}
 +(1-A)\frac{1-q(e(X))}{1-e(X)}\{Y-\mu_0(X)\}
 +q'(e(X))\tau(X)\{A-e(X)\}.
\end{aligned}                                                                             \tag{51}
\end{equation}
Every ratio in (51) is used only on a supported arm.  On \(e<c\),
\(q(e)/e=\delta=w_1(e)\) and
\((1-q(e))/(1-e)=w_0(e)\); on \(e>c\), these ratios are
\(1/e=w_1(e)\) and \(0=w_0(e)\).  Substitution in (51) gives exactly \(D_P\) almost surely.  This proves the claimed reduction by direct calculation.

%%% FIELD partial-uniform-local-honest-inference
For a chosen active cell \(B\), let \(\kappa_B=E_P[\tau(X)\mathbf1_B(X)]\ne0\) and use the local propensity path \(e_{h,n}=e+h\mathbf1_B/\sqrt n\).  The finite-cell likelihood experiment is locally asymptotically normal with score information
\begin{equation}
 I_B=E_P[S_B^2]=\frac{P_X(B)}{c(1-c)},
\end{equation}
whereas its local target increment is the kinked map
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt n\{\Psi_\delta(P_{h/\sqrt n})-\Psi_\delta(P)\}
 \longrightarrow \delta\kappa_B\min(h,0).
\end{equation}
Thus the required interval problem reduces, already in one active cell, to confidence intervals for a kinked functional of the mean in a Gaussian shift experiment; ordinary efficient Wald adaptation cannot be justified by the regular-side influence function uniformly across \(h=0\).
