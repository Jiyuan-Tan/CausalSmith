Fix a topological ordering of \((V,D)\).  In this ordering every supported matrix \(L\) is strictly upper triangular, because \(L_{ij}\) can be nonzero only when \(i\to j\).  Hence \(L^n=0\) and
\[
(I_n-L)^{-1}=I_n+L+\cdots+L^{n-1}.
\]
In particular, \(I_n-L\) is invertible.  By positive definiteness of \(\Sigma\), for every nonzero \(v\in\mathbb R^n\),
\[
v^{\mathsf T}\Omega(L)v
=((I_n-L)v)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma((I_n-L)v)>0,
\]
because \((I_n-L)v\ne0\).  Thus \(\Omega(L)\succ0\).

If \(L\in\mathcal F_G(\Sigma)\), Definition~\(\mathrm{def:fiber}\) gives both the support restriction on \(L\) and
\[
\Omega(L)_{ij}=0\qquad(i\ne j,\ \{i,j\}\notin B).
\]
Consequently \((L,\Omega(L))\) is a supported SEM parameterization.  Its induced covariance is
\[
(I_n-L)^{-\mathsf T}\Omega(L)(I_n-L)^{-1}
=(I_n-L)^{-\mathsf T}(I_n-L)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma(I_n-L)(I_n-L)^{-1}
=\Sigma.
\]
Conversely, suppose that a supported pair \((L,\Omega)\), with \(\Omega\succ0\) and the missing-\(B\) entries of \(\Omega\) equal to zero, induces \(\Sigma\).  Multiplication of
\[
\Sigma=(I_n-L)^{-\mathsf T}\Omega(I_n-L)^{-1}
\]
on the left by \((I_n-L)^{\mathsf T}\) and on the right by \(I_n-L\) gives
\[
\Omega=(I_n-L)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma(I_n-L)=\Omega(L).
\]
Its missing entries therefore put \(L\) in \(\mathcal F_G(\Sigma)\).  The displayed equality also proves uniqueness of \(\Omega\) for a given \(L\), so \(L\mapsto(L,\Omega(L))\) and projection to \(L\) are inverse maps.  This proves the asserted bijection and shows that no positive-definiteness inequality remains after the missing-edge equations are imposed.

For every nonroot vertex \(i\), let \(p(i)\) be its unique parent and put \(x_i=L_{p(i),i}\).  The \(i\)-th column of \(I_n-L\) is \(e_i-x_i e_{p(i)}\).  Hence, when \(i\) and \(j\) are nonroots,
\[
\Omega(L)_{ij}
=\Sigma_{ij}-\Sigma_{p(i),j}x_i-\Sigma_{i,p(j)}x_j
+\Sigma_{p(i),p(j)}x_ix_j.
\]
If exactly one vertex is a root this formula loses the terms containing its nonexistent coefficient and becomes affine unary; if both are roots it is constant.  Thus Definition~\(\mathrm{def:biaffine\mbox{-}system}\), together with the preceding fiber reduction, identifies the fiber exactly with a rational system of constant, unary, and bivariate bi-affine equations.

Consider a bivariate equation
\[
f(x,y)=axy+bx+cy+d=0,
\qquad
M_f=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If \(\det M_f=ad-bc\ne0\), then
\[
y=-\frac{bx+d}{ax+c}.
\]
At a zero of \(ax+c\), the numerator \(bx+d\) cannot also vanish, since a common zero would make the two rows of \(M_f\) dependent.  Therefore the displayed fractional-linear equation, with that pole deleted, is equivalent to \(f=0\); its representing matrix has determinant \(ad-bc\ne0\), so it also has an inverse fractional-linear map.

Join two coefficient variables precisely when they occur in such an invertible relation.  In a connected component \(C\), choose a root variable \(t_C\) and a spanning tree.  Composition along the unique tree paths gives
\[
x_i=\phi_{C,i}(t_C)=\frac{A_i t_C+B_i}{C_i t_C+D_i},
\qquad A_iD_i-B_iC_i\ne0.
\]
Every finite zero of a denominator is recorded.  Induction along the tree proves both directions: any solution of the tree equations has the displayed form with \(t_C\) equal to its root coordinate, and any \(t_C\) avoiding all recorded poles satisfies every tree equation.  The representation is unique because the root map is the identity.

Substitute these maps into every unary equation and every non-tree equation internal to \(C\).  Multiplication by the recorded nonzero denominators yields a rational polynomial of degree at most two.  Let \(\mathcal P_C\) be the collection of the nonzero resulting polynomials.  If \(\mathcal P_C\) is empty, all internal equations are identities and
\[
D_C=\mathbb R\setminus P_C,
\]
where \(P_C\) is the finite set of recorded rational poles.  Otherwise let \(g_C\) be the monic greatest common divisor of the polynomials in \(\mathcal P_C\).  Then
\[
D_C=\{t\in\mathbb R:g_C(t)=0\}\setminus P_C.
\]
The equivalence follows from the elementary property that a real number is a common zero of finitely many univariate polynomials if and only if it is a zero of their greatest common divisor.  Since every member of \(\mathcal P_C\) has degree at most two, so does \(g_C\).  A nonzero constant among the residuals makes \(g_C=1\) and \(D_C=\varnothing\); zero residuals add no restriction; repeated roots and roots that are poles are handled by the displayed set difference.  Thus every \(D_C\) has exactly one of the two forms in the claim.

No invertible relation can remain between two distinct components.  If a remaining bivariate relation has rank one, rational row--column factorization gives
\[
axy+bx+cy+d=\kappa(ux+v)(wy+z)
\]
for rational coefficients and nonzero \(\kappa\).  After substitution into components \(C\) and \(H\), and after multiplication by the already excluded denominators, it is therefore exactly
\[
\ell_C(t_C)=0\quad\text{or}\quad \ell_H(t_H)=0,
\]
where each \(\ell\) is affine rational.  A nonconstant factor names one rational value; a value outside its domain is false, a nonzero constant is false, and an identically zero factor is true.  Rank zero is the zero equation.  Constant equations from two roots are treated as true or false according as the constant is zero or nonzero.

For every distinct named admissible value \(r\) of a component, introduce \(E_{C,r}\), meaning \(t_C=r\).  If \(D_C\) is finite, introduce atoms also for its unnamed elements.  Add every singular disjunction, the clauses
\[
\neg E_{C,r}\lor\neg E_{C,s}\qquad(r\ne s),
\]
and, for a one-point or two-point domain, respectively the unit coverage clause or the two-literal coverage clause.  An empty domain contributes false; a cofinite domain has no coverage clause.  This is exactly the formula \(\Psi\) from Definition~\(\mathrm{def:mobius\mbox{-}sat\mbox{-}handle}\).

Every real solution sets its equality atoms according to its component coordinates and hence satisfies \(\Psi\).  Conversely, pair a satisfying assignment with coordinates that equal every true named atom and avoid every false named atom.  Coverage and exclusion force the unique compatible coordinate in a finite domain.  In a cofinite component a true atom fixes its coordinate, while an all-false atom pattern permits every point outside the finite union of poles and named values; this set is nonempty and contains a rational point.  Every singular clause then guarantees that at least one of its two factors vanishes.  Together with the internal-domain construction, the reconstructed coordinates satisfy every original equation.  A real solution determines exactly one such compatible assignment-coordinate pair, and evaluation of the \(\phi_{C,i}\) maps is its inverse.  This is the asserted bijection.  When the algorithm needs one representative of an all-false cofinite state, it chooses any rational point in that state.

It remains to justify bit complexity.  The number of relations, components, poles, and equality atoms is polynomial in the input length, and pairwise exclusion creates at most quadratically many clauses.  A product of at most polynomially many rational \(2\times2\) matrices has polynomial bit length: after clearing input denominators, each output entry is a sum of at most exponentially many products, whose logarithmic magnitude and denominator bit length are polynomial.  All residuals have degree at most two, so their greatest common divisors, rational roots, discriminants, comparisons, and isolating intervals are obtained by the Euclidean algorithm and the quadratic formula with polynomially many bit operations on polynomial-bit integers.

Finally, satisfiability of the resulting two-clause formula is decided directly from its implication graph.  A clause \(u\lor v\) gives arcs \(\neg u\to v\) and \(\neg v\to u\); the formula is satisfiable if and only if no literal and its negation lie in the same strongly connected component.  The forward implication follows by propagation along arcs.  For the reverse implication, ordering the strongly connected components in reverse topological order and assigning a literal true exactly when its component is later than that of its negation satisfies every arc.  Two depth-first graph traversals compute these components in linear time.  Thus construction of \(\Psi\), its solution, and reconstruction in both directions have deterministic polynomial bit complexity, with every degeneration listed in the statement covered by one of the explicit cases above.

Apply the pointwise normal form to the feasible instance and let \(C\) be the component containing the queried coefficient.  Its coordinate is
\[
L_e=\phi_{C,e}(t_C)=\frac{A t_C+B}{C t_C+D},
\qquad AD-BC\ne0,
\]
so it is injective on its pole-excluded real domain.

If \(D_C\) is finite, it has at most two elements.  For each one, add its equality atom as a unit clause and run the implication-graph test.  Feasibility guarantees that at least one extends.  Thus one extending value gives case \(\mathrm{(i)}\), and two give case \(\mathrm{(ii)}\).

Suppose next that \(D_C\) is cofinite, and let \(S_C\) be the finite set of rational values named by equality atoms.  First add all units \(\neg E_{C,r}\), \(r\in S_C\), and test satisfiability.  If this formula is satisfiable, fix the reconstructed coordinates of all components other than \(C\).  In every singular clause containing a positive \(C\)-literal, the other literal is true under this assignment.  Hence \(t_C\) may range over any rational open interval avoiding \(S_C\) and all poles while all equations remain satisfied.  Such an interval is obtained by sorting the finite rational exceptional set and taking an interval between consecutive values, or an exterior interval.  Moreover,
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{C,e}(t)=\frac{AD-BC}{(Ct+D)^2}\ne0
\]
throughout the interval.  This gives case \(\mathrm{(iii)}\), and its image under the injective fractional-linear map is cofinite in \(\mathbb R\): if \(C\ne0\), the full pole-excluded line maps onto \(\mathbb R\setminus\{A/C\}\), while if \(C=0\) it maps onto all of \(\mathbb R\); deleting finitely many inputs deletes finitely many outputs.

If the all-false test fails, test each named atom separately.  At most two distinct named values can extend.  Indeed, suppose that satisfying assignments \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\) selected three distinct values.  Their coordinatewise Boolean majority \(\alpha\) satisfies every two-literal clause: if a clause were false under \(\alpha\), each of its two literals would be false in at least two of the three assignments, and the two subsets of size at least two would intersect, making the clause false in one \(\alpha_j\).  Yet every equality atom of \(C\) is true in at most one \(\alpha_j\), so all are false under \(\alpha\), contradicting failure of the all-false test.  Feasibility now leaves exactly one or two extending values, giving case \(\mathrm{(i)}\) or \(\mathrm{(ii)}\).

The unique value is rational.  In a cofinite component every isolated extending coordinate is a named rational value.  In a finite component, if one root of its rational polynomial were irrational, the polynomial would be an irreducible quadratic with two distinct real conjugate roots.  Neither root can equal a rational pole or a rational value named by a cross-component singular factor.  The clauses involving the two finite-domain atoms are therefore symmetric under their interchange, so extendibility of one root implies extendibility of the other.  Injectivity of \(\phi_{C,e}\) then gives two distinct queried values, contrary to uniqueness.  Hence a unique root coordinate, and its fractional-linear image, are rational.

For every successful unit test, the converse construction in the normal-form lemma supplies a full feasible matrix.  Each component coordinate is rational or a real root of a rational polynomial of degree at most two, and every matrix entry is obtained from it by a rational fractional-linear map; this is exactly the componentwise encoding \(\mathsf{Cert}\).  In case \(\mathrm{(ii)}\) the two unit tests provide two such matrices with unequal \(e\)-coordinates.  In case \(\mathrm{(iii)}\) the fixed component coordinates, the rational interval, and the maps \(\phi_C\) give the required curve.

There are polynomially many unit tests.  The normal-form proof bounds every rational coefficient, quadratic polynomial, isolating interval, and two-clause instance by polynomial bit length, so the total running time and every componentwise output length are polynomial in the encoded input.  The three cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and their attainable sets are, respectively, a singleton, a two-element set, and a cofinite subset of \(\mathbb R\).  The cited published comparison records that unrestricted numerical edge identifiability was previously known to be decidable in polynomial space; the present deterministic polynomial-bit algorithm on \(\mathcal M_1\) is therefore the asserted strict upper-bound improvement on this class.

Use the standard bit-model definition of \(\exists\mathbb R\) as polynomial-time reducibility to truth of existential first-order sentences over the ordered real field, as surveyed by Schaefer, Cardinal, and Miltzow~\cite{SchaeferCardinalMiltzow2024}.  We first derive the needed equation normal form.  An atomic inequality can be replaced existentially by equations:
\[
p\ge0\Longleftrightarrow \exists u\;(p-u^2=0),\qquad
p>0\Longleftrightarrow \exists u\;(pu^2-1=0),
\]
with the corresponding sign changes for \(p\le0\) and \(p<0\), while
\[
p\ne0\Longleftrightarrow \exists u\;(pu-1=0).
\]
After negations have been pushed to atoms, conjunction is concatenation of equation lists.  If two existentially augmented systems have residual lists \((f_i)\) and \((g_j)\), their disjunction is equivalent to
\[
\left(\sum_i f_i^2\right)\left(\sum_j g_j^2\right)=0,
\]
because each sum of squares vanishes exactly when all residuals in that branch vanish.  Applying this recursively gives, without expanding any polynomial, a polynomial-size arithmetic circuit whose output is zero exactly for the original existential sentence.  Introduce one variable for every circuit wire and one defining equation for every addition or multiplication gate.  Rational constants, negation, and subtraction are implemented using constant wires and multiplication by \(-1\).  The gate definitions have unique outputs once their inputs are fixed, so this transformation preserves feasibility and has polynomial bit complexity.  It yields a finite system \(R\) using precisely constant, addition, multiplication, and equality constraints of the forms permitted by Definition~\(\mathrm{def:source\mbox{-}encoding}\).

We now add the planted selector with the polarity required by the frozen promise.  Write \(w=(w_1,\ldots,w_r)\) for every coordinate of \(R\), including its gate variables, and let \(r_j(w)=0\) be the residual form of its \(j\)-th normalized constraint.  Replace the constraints of \(R\) by their gated residuals below; their residual-calculation circuits, including separate rational constant wires, remain unconditionally defined.  Introduce a selector \(s\), a complement wire \(t\), and normalized gate equations implementing
\[
s(s-1)=0,qquad t+s=1,qquad tw_i=0\ (1\le i\le r),qquad s\,r_j(w)=0\ (1\le j\le q).
\]
Each displayed expression is converted to the allowed gate list with only a constant number of new wires per operation.  All defining gates are retained, so every assignment of \((s,t,w)\) has at most one extension to their outputs.

If \(s=0\), then \(t=1\) and \(tw_i=0\) forces \(w=0\).  All residual wires then have explicitly computable rational values and every product \(s r_j(w)\) is zero.  This supplies a rational planted vector \(z_Q\) whose selector coordinate is zero.  If \(s=1\), then \(t=0\), and the last family is equivalent to \(r_j(w)=0\) for every \(j\), namely \(w\in\operatorname{Sol}(R)\).  The first equation also proves that every real solution has \(s\in\{0,1\}\).  Consequently the constructed string lies in \(\mathcal P_{\mathrm{sel}}\), and
\[
\exists w\in\operatorname{Sol}(R)
\Longleftrightarrow
\exists x\in\operatorname{Sol}(Q)\text{ with }x_{s_Q}=1.
\]
The complete binary encoding, including all reduced rational coordinates of the planted assignment, has polynomial length and is produced in polynomial time.  This proves \(\exists\mathbb R\)-hardness of \(\mathsf{SEL\mbox{-}PNQF}\).

Membership follows because, on the promise domain, its YES predicate is the existential real formula
\[
\exists x\in\mathbb R^m\;\left(\bigwedge_j f_j(x)=0\ \land\ x_{s_Q}=1\right),
\]
where the \(f_j\) are the encoded degree-at-most-two gate residuals.  Thus the uncomplemented promise language is \(\exists\mathbb R\)-complete.  Its promise complement has predicate
\[
\forall x\in\mathbb R^m\;\left(\bigwedge_j f_j(x)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ x_{s_Q}=0\right),
\]
and the same reductions with YES and NO interchanged show that it is \(\forall\mathbb R=\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R\)-complete.  This also fixes the claimed polarity.

We first verify the compiler at the level of its entire fiber.  Use a depth-one directed graph in which every coefficient-holding sink \(i\) has a private parent set \(P_i\) of size at most two, every parent is a root used by only that sink, and all programmed equations are assigned to distinct missing pairs of sinks.  If \(i\) and \(j\) are such a pair, then direct expansion gives
\[

=\Sigma_{ij}
-\sum_{a\in P_i}\Sigma_{aj}L_{ai}
-\sum_{b\in P_j}\Sigma_{ib}L_{bj}
+\sum_{a\in P_i}\sum_{b\in P_j}\Sigma_{ab}L_{ai}L_{bj}.
\]

\[

\]

Because every root is private, each slot \(\Sigma_{aj}\), \(\Sigma_{ib}\), or \(\Sigma_{ab}\) identifies the same unordered pair of owner sinks \(\{i,j\}\); hence it cannot be used by a different programmed equation.  The constant slot \(\Sigma_{ij}\) is likewise pair-specific.

An addition \(a+b=c\) uses a two-coefficient sink carrying \((a,b)\) and a singleton sink carrying \(c\); a multiplication \(ab=c\) uses a two-coefficient sink carrying \((a,c)\) and a singleton carrying \(b\).  Equality and constant constraints use singleton occurrence sinks, paired when necessary with a fresh zero-parent sink. Moreover, \(\mathcal A_e(G,\Sigma)\) is respectively a singleton, a two-element set, or a cofinite subset of \(\mathbb R\), and every output has polynomial bit length in the componentwise encoding \(\mathsf{Cert}\). On the fan-in-one class this strictly improves the published polynomial-space numerical edge-identification upper bound of Dörfler et al. to deterministic polynomial bit complexity.

The promise problem \(\mathsf{NUMEDGE}_2\) is \(\forall\mathbb R\)-complete. For every \(Q\in\mathcal P_{\mathrm{sel}}\), \(\mathsf C\) outputs in polynomial time an acyclic mixed graph \(G_Q\) of maximum directed indegree at most two, a rational strictly diagonally dominant matrix \(\Sigma_Q\), the selector-master edge \(e_Q\), and the rational feasible matrix \(\rho_Q\). The master projection \(\pi_Q\) is a bijection \(\mathcal F_{G_Q}(\Sigma_Q)\to\operatorname{Sol}(Q)\), with occurrence replication as its inverse, and \(L_{e_Q}=(\pi_Q(L))_{s_Q}\) for every \(L\in\mathcal F_{G_Q}(\Sigma_Q)\). Consequently, \(Q\in\mathsf{coSEL\mbox{-}PNQF}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal A_{e_Q}(G_Q,\Sigma_Q)=\{0\}\Longleftrightarrow\mathsf C(Q)\in\mathsf{NUMEDGE}_2\), whereas \(Q\in\mathsf{SEL\mbox{-}PNQF}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal A_{e_Q}(G_Q,\Sigma_Q)=\{0,1\}\). Thus \(\mathsf C\) is a promise-preserving many-one reduction from the complemented language, not from the uncomplemented language. Relative to Dörfler et al., the strict tightening is maximum directed indegree two together with a fiber-bijective compiler.
