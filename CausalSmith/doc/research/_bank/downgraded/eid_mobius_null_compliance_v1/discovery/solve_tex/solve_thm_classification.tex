Put \(V=U\setminus t\).  Because \(\lambda_t(z)=0\) unless \(z\supseteq t\), define
\[
\alpha(S)=\lambda_t(t\cup S),\qquad S\subseteq V.
\]
For every \(B\subseteq V\), take \(C_0=t\cup B\).  By the definition of \(g_{C_0}\),
\[
g_{t\cup B}(t\cup S)=(t\cup B)\cap(t\cup S)=t
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\cap B=\varnothing.
\]
The assumed isolation equations therefore give
\[
\sum_{S\subseteq V\setminus B}\alpha(S)=\mathbf 1\{B=V\}.
\]
On writing \(Q=V\setminus B\), this becomes
\[
F(Q):=\sum_{S\subseteq Q}\alpha(S)=\mathbf 1\{Q=\varnothing\},
\qquad Q\subseteq V. \tag{1}
\]
For completeness, finite Boolean inversion follows directly by interchanging two finite sums:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{R\subseteq S}(-1)^{|S|-|R|}F(R)
&=\sum_{R\subseteq S}(-1)^{|S|-|R|}\sum_{T\subseteq R}\alpha(T)\\
&=\sum_{T\subseteq S}\alpha(T)
  \sum_{R:T\subseteq R\subseteq S}(-1)^{|S|-|R|}\\
&=\alpha(S),
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
\]
because the inner sum is \((1-1)^{|S\setminus T|}\), hence is one when \(T=S\) and zero otherwise.  Substituting (1) into (2) yields
\[
\alpha(S)=(-1)^{|S|}.
\]
Consequently, for \(z=t\cup S\),
\[
\lambda_t(z)=(-1)^{|z|-|t|}=a_t(z),
\]
and both sides are zero when \(z\not\supseteq t\).  Conversely, summing these signs over \(S\subseteq V\setminus B\) gives \((1-1)^{|V\setminus B|}=\mathbf 1\{B=V\}\), while a separable map indexed by \(C_0\not\supseteq t\) never chooses \(t\).  Thus \(a_t\) satisfies every displayed isolation equation.  Equation (2) proves uniqueness, since it reconstructs every \(\alpha(S)\) from the prescribed values of \(F\).

%%% FIELD proof-classification
We first record the revealed-choice implication used throughout.  If \(g(z)=r\) and \(r\subseteq w\subseteq z\), strict rationalization gives \(r\succ_g d\) for every \(d\subseteq z\) with \(d\ne r\).  Every bundle feasible from \(w\) is feasible from \(z\), while \(r\) is feasible from \(w\); hence
\[
g(z)=r,\quad r\subseteq w\subseteq z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(w)=r. \tag{3}
\]
This is contraction consistency, derived here from the strict-order hypothesis.

For a pair \(e\subset U\) and a singleton \(\{j\}\subset U\), the nonempty-cell null equations are, respectively,
\[
0=\sigma_e(g)=\mathbf 1\{g(e)=e\}-\mathbf 1\{g(U)=e\}, \tag{4}
\]
and, writing \(U=\{j,k,\ell\}\),
\[
0=\sigma_{\{j\}}(g)
=\mathbf 1\{g(\{j\})=\{j\}\}
-\mathbf 1\{g(\{j,k\})=\{j\}\}
-\mathbf 1\{g(\{j,\ell\})=\{j\}\}
+\mathbf 1\{g(U)=\{j\}\}. \tag{5}
\]
Also \(0=\sigma_U(g)=\mathbf 1\{g(U)=U\}\).  One-sidedness therefore leaves only \(|g(U)|=0,1,2\).

If \(g(U)=\varnothing\), (3), applied to every \(w\subseteq U\), gives \(g(w)=\varnothing\).  This is family (i).

Suppose next that \(g(U)=\{a\}\), where \(U=\{a,b,c\}\).  Equation (3) forces
\[
g(\{a\})=g(\{a,b\})=g(\{a,c\})=\{a\}. \tag{6}
\]
By (4), \(g(\{b,c\})\ne\{b,c\}\); one-sidedness thus makes it one of \(\varnothing,\{b\},\{c\}\).  Applying (5) to \(b\) and \(c\), and using (6), gives exactly
\[
\mathbf 1\{g(\{b\})=\{b\}\}
=\mathbf 1\{g(\{b,c\})=\{b\}\},
\qquad
\mathbf 1\{g(\{c\})=\{c\}\}
=\mathbf 1\{g(\{b,c\})=\{c\}\}. \tag{7}
\]
Thus, after choosing \(a\), there are exactly three maps, according as the secondary choice from \(\{b,c\}\) is \(\varnothing\), \(\{b\}\), or \(\{c\}\).  They are rationalized by ranking \(\{a\}\) first, then the selected secondary singleton if there is one, then \(\varnothing\), and putting every unlisted bundle below \(\varnothing\) in an arbitrary strict order.  These are precisely family (ii), giving \(3\cdot3=9\) maps.

It remains to treat the full-offer-pair branch.  Let \(g(U)=\{a,b\}\) and let \(c\) be the remaining factor.  Contraction and (4) imply
\[
g(\{a,b\})=\{a,b\},\qquad
x:=g(\{a,c\})\in\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{c\}\},\qquad
y:=g(\{b,c\})\in\{\varnothing,\{b\},\{c\}\}. \tag{8}
\]
The three equations (5) reduce exactly to
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf 1\{g(\{a\})=\{a\}\}&=\mathbf 1\{x=\{a\}\},\\
\mathbf 1\{g(\{b\})=\{b\}\}&=\mathbf 1\{y=\{b\}\},\\
\mathbf 1\{g(\{c\})=\{c\}\}&=\mathbf 1\{x=\{c\}\}+\mathbf 1\{y=\{c\}\}.
\end{aligned} \tag{9}
\]
If \(g(\{c\})=\varnothing\), the last line of (9) excludes \(\{c\}\) from both \(x\) and \(y\).  Hence \(x\in\{\varnothing,\{a\}\}\) and \(y\in\{\varnothing,\{b\}\}\), independently, and the first two lines of (9) determine the singleton choices.  There are exactly four maps.  Each is rationalized by placing \(\{a,b\}\) first, then whichever of \(\{a\}\) and \(\{b\}\) are responsive in either strict order, then \(\varnothing\), with all unlisted bundles below \(\varnothing\) in an arbitrary strict order.  These are family (iii).

If \(g(\{c\})=\{c\}\), the last line of (9) says that exactly one of \(x,y\) equals \(\{c\}\).  Suppose \(x=\{c\}\).  Choice of \(\{c\}\) from its singleton menu gives \(\{c\}\succ_g\varnothing\).  Since \(\{c\}\) is feasible from \(\{b,c\}\), one cannot have \(y=\varnothing\).  The last line of (9) also excludes \(y=\{c\}\), so (8) forces \(y=\{b\}\).  The first two lines of (9) then force \(g(\{a\})=\varnothing\) and \(g(\{b\})=\{b\}\).  The strict ranking
\[
\{a,b\}\succ_g\{b\}\succ_g\{c\}\succ_g\varnothing\succ_g\{a\}, \tag{10}
\]
with all other unlisted bundles below \(\varnothing\), rationalizes this map.  The case \(y=\{c\}\) is symmetric and is rationalized by
\[
\{a,b\}\succ_g\{a\}\succ_g\{c\}\succ_g\varnothing\succ_g\{b\}. \tag{11}
\]
Thus the responsive-\(c\) branch has exactly two maps, not merely at most two.  They are precisely family (iv).  Across the three choices of the unordered pair \(\{a,b\}\), families (iii) and (iv) have \(3\cdot4=12\) and \(3\cdot2=6\) maps.

It remains only to check the empty-cell equation and the converse inclusion.  For every one-sided map,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{t\subseteq U}(-1)^{|t|}\sigma_t(g)
&=\sum_{z\subseteq U}(-1)^{|z|}
  \sum_{t\subseteq z}\mathbf 1\{g(z)=t\}\\
&=\sum_{z\subseteq U}(-1)^{|z|}=(1-1)^3=0. 
\end{aligned} \tag{12}
\]
The inner indicator sum is one by one-sidedness.  Hence the seven nonempty equations imply \(\sigma_\varnothing(g)=0\).  Conversely, every map just listed has the displayed strict rationalization; equations (4), (5), and (9) verify all its nonempty signatures, and (12) verifies its empty signature.  The cases are disjoint because their full-offer choices differ, except for families (iii) and (iv), which differ according as \(g(\{c\})\) is empty or nonempty.  Therefore
\[
\mathcal N_3=\mathcal F_3,qquad
|\mathcal N_3|=1+9+12+6=28,qquad
|\mathcal E_3|=29. \tag{13}
\]

We finally establish the two qualitative counts.  For \(C_0\ne U\), if \(t\not\subseteq C_0\), then \(g_{C_0}(z)\ne t\) for every \(z\); if \(t\subseteq C_0\), then
\[
\sigma_t(g_{C_0})
=\sum_{R\subseteq U\setminus C_0}(-1)^{|R|}
=(1-1)^{|U\setminus C_0|}=0. \tag{14}
\]
Thus the seven maps \(g_{C_0}\) with \(C_0\ne U\) are null, whereas \(g_U=g^\star\) and \(\sigma_t(g^\star)=1\) for every \(t\).  Hence exactly eight members of \(\mathcal E_3\) are coordinate-separable, and (13) leaves exactly \(29-8=21\) interactive maps.

Family (i) has every coordinate irrelevant.  In family (ii), any coordinate distinct from the primary and optional secondary singleton is irrelevant.  In family (iii), the remaining coordinate \(c\) is irrelevant.  In contrast, for the representative (10), removal of \(a\) from \(\{a,b\}\) changes choice from \(\{a,b\}\) to \(\{b\}\), removal of \(b\) from \(\{b,c\}\) changes choice from \(\{b\}\) to \(\{c\}\), and removal of \(c\) from \(\{c\}\) changes choice from \(\{c\}\) to \(\varnothing\).  Thus \(I(g)=\varnothing\) for (10); the same argument after interchanging \(a,b\) proves it for (11).  These are all six family-(iv) maps, and no map in the other families has empty \(I(g)\).  The final count is therefore exactly six.

%%% FIELD proof-signed-identification
Full assignment support makes every conditional cell functional below observable.  By receipt consistency and instrument independence, for every \(z,t\in\mathcal B\),
\[
q_{zt}
=P(G(z)=t\mid Z=z)
=P(G(z)=t). \tag{15}
\]
On \(\{G(z)=t\}\), receipt consistency gives \(D=t\), and outcome exclusion gives \(Y=Y(t)\).  Therefore, using integrability and then instrument independence,
\[
\begin{aligned}
r_{zt}
&=\mathbb E_P\!\left[Y\mathbf 1\{D=t\}\mid Z=z\right]\\
&=\mathbb E_P\!\left[Y(t)\mathbf 1\{G(z)=t\}\mid Z=z\right]\\
&=\mathbb E_P\!\left[Y(t)\mathbf 1\{G(z)=t\}\right].
\end{aligned} \tag{16}
\]
The support assumption and \(\mathcal M\subseteq\mathcal E_3\) imply \(G\in\mathcal E_3\) almost surely.  For every \(g\in\mathcal E_3\), the null-envelope definition gives
\[
\sigma_t(g)=\mathbf 1\{g=g^\star\}. \tag{17}
\]
Indeed null maps have zero signature, while \(g^\star(z)=z\) makes the sole nonzero summand in \(\sigma_t(g^\star)\) the assignment \(z=t\), with coefficient one.  Taking the finite signed sums in (15) and (16), and using (17), yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
p_t
&=\sum_z a_t(z)\,\mathbb E_P[\mathbf 1\{G(z)=t\}]
=\mathbb E_P[\sigma_t(G)]
=P(G=g^\star)=p^\star,\\
m_t
&=\sum_z a_t(z)\,\mathbb E_P[Y(t)\mathbf 1\{G(z)=t\}]
=\mathbb E_P[Y(t)\sigma_t(G)]\\
&=\mathbb E_P[Y(t)\mathbf 1\{G=g^\star\}]
=p^\star\mu_t^\star.
\end{aligned} \tag{18}
\]
The positive-share assumption licenses division by \(p_t=p^\star>0\), so \(\mu_t^\star=m_t/p_t\).  Substitution into the Walsh definition gives, for every nonempty \(S\subseteq U\),
\[
\theta_S=2^{-2}\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\chi_S(t)\frac{m_t}{p_t}.
\]
Thus the causal means and effects are proved equal to, rather than defined as, the displayed observable functionals.

Fix \(h\in\mathcal G^{\mathrm{WARP}}_3\setminus\mathcal E_3\).  Since \(\mathcal E_3=\{g^\star\}\cup\mathcal N_3\), one has \(h\ne g^\star\) and there is a bundle \(t\) such that \(\sigma_t(h)\ne0\).  Put \(V=U\setminus t\) and
\[
v_h(S)=\mathbf 1\{h(t\cup S)=t\},\qquad S\subseteq V.
\]
For \(B\subseteq V\), define the integer
\[
\beta_{t\cup B}=\sum_{Q\subseteq B}(-1)^{|B|-|Q|}v_h(V\setminus Q),
\]
and put \(\beta_C=0\) when \(C\not\supseteq t\).  Direct finite-sum cancellation gives, for every \(S\subseteq V\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{B\subseteq V\setminus S}\beta_{t\cup B}
&=\sum_{Q\subseteq V\setminus S}v_h(V\setminus Q)
  \sum_{B:Q\subseteq B\subseteq V\setminus S}(-1)^{|B|-|Q|}\\
&=v_h(S),
\end{aligned} \tag{19}
\]
because the inner sum is zero unless \(Q=V\setminus S\), in which case it is one.  Moreover, after the substitution \(S=V\setminus Q\),
\[
\beta_U=\sum_{S\subseteq V}(-1)^{|S|}v_h(S)=\sigma_t(h)\ne0. \tag{20}
\]

On the nine distinct types \(\{g_C:C\subseteq U\}\cup\{h\}\), set
\[
n_h=1,qquad n_{g_C}=-\beta_C.
\]
If \(z\not\supseteq t\), one-sidedness makes every indicator \(\mathbf 1\{g(z)=t\}\) vanish.  If \(z=t\cup S\), then
\[
g_{t\cup B}(z)=t
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\subseteq V\setminus S.
\]
Consequently, (19) proves the observable-null identity
\[
\sum_g n_g\mathbf 1\{g(z)=t\}
=v_h(S)-\sum_{B\subseteq V\setminus S}\beta_{t\cup B}=0
\quad\text{for every }z\in\mathcal B, \tag{21}
\]
while (20) gives
\[
n_{g^\star}=n_{g_U}=-\beta_U=-\sigma_t(h)\ne0. \tag{22}
\]

Give each of the nine types mass \(q_g=1/9\), let \(M_n=\max_g|n_g|\), and set \(\varepsilon=(36M_n)^{-1}\).  Since \(n_h=1\), \(M_n\ge1\), and
\[
\left|\varepsilon n_g/q_g\right|\le\frac14. \tag{23}
\]
Choose any common assignment law with \(\pi_z>0\) for all \(z\), independently of type and potential outcomes.  Conditional on \(G=g\), let the eight potential outcomes be mutually independent Bernoulli variables, with success probability \(1/2\) for every bundle \(s\ne t\), and with
\[
P_\pm(Y(t)=1\mid G=g)=\frac12\pm\varepsilon n_g/q_g. \tag{24}
\]
The finite product construction defines probability laws \(P_+\) and \(P_-\).  Equation (23) places every probability in \([1/4,3/4]\); all moments are finite.  Defining \(D=G(Z)\) and \(Y=Y(D)\) enforces receipt consistency and exclusion, and the product construction enforces instrument independence.  Both laws have the same positive type masses and \(p^\star=1/9\).

It remains to verify equality of the entire observed law.  Receipt probabilities coincide because the type and assignment laws coincide.  For \(d\ne t\), the conditional Bernoulli law of \(Y(d)\) is identical under \(P_+\) and \(P_-\).  For \(d=t\), assignment \(z\), and the success atom, (21) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&P_+(D=t,Y=1\mid Z=z)-P_-(D=t,Y=1\mid Z=z)\\
&\qquad=\sum_{g:g(z)=t}q_g
\left[2\varepsilon n_g/q_g\right]
=2\varepsilon\sum_g n_g\mathbf 1\{g(z)=t\}=0.
\end{aligned} \tag{25}
\]
The failure atom also coincides because its probability is the common receipt probability minus the success probability.  Thus every conditional atom of \((D,Y)\) given \(Z=z\) is the same, and the common assignment law implies
\[
\mathcal L_{P_+}(Z,D,Y)=\mathcal L_{P_-}(Z,D,Y). \tag{26}
\]
On the other hand, (22)--(24) imply
\[
\mu_t^\star(P_+)-\mu_t^\star(P_-)
=2\varepsilon n_{g^\star}/q_{g^\star}\ne0. \tag{27}
\]
Both laws are supported on \(\mathcal S_3\cup\{h\}\), so (26)--(27) are the required finite Bernoulli observational-equivalence witness for every excluded \(h\).

%%% FIELD statement-finite-cell-asymptotics
Under independent and identically distributed sampling, positive probabilities for the finitely many assignment arms, and a finite second moment for the observed outcome, the within-assignment receipt and outcome cell means admit a joint asymptotically linear representation with influence coordinates \(\phi_{q,zt}\) and \(\phi_{r,zt}\), and their empirical plug-in covariance is consistent.  Fixed linear combinations therefore have influence coordinates \(\phi_{p,t}\) and \(\phi_{m,t}\).  The multivariate delta method applies to differentiable finite-dimensional transformations whose denominators are nonzero; each fixed nondegenerate linear moment \(\widehat m_t-u\widehat p_t\) has a standard-normal studentized limit, and a seven-dimensional transformation with positive-definite covariance has the usual chi-square Wald limit.

%%% FIELD proof-joint-inference
All assertions are pointwise at a fixed law satisfying the displayed assumptions.  Since \(\mathcal B\) is finite and every \(\pi_z>0\), the finite-cell asymptotic lemma applies jointly to all cell estimators.  Linear combination with the fixed coefficients \(a_t(z)\) yields
\[
\sqrt n(\widehat p_t-p_t)
=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n\phi_{p,t}(O_i)+o_P(1),
\qquad
\sqrt n(\widehat m_t-m_t)
=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n\phi_{m,t}(O_i)+o_P(1). \tag{29}
\]
By signed identification, \(p_t=p^\star\) and \(m_t=p^\star\mu_t^\star\).  The lower bound \(p^\star\ge\kappa>0\) and (29) imply \(\widehat p_t\xrightarrow{P}p^\star\) and hence license the ratio expansion
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt n(\widehat\mu_t-\mu_t^\star)
&=\frac{1}{\widehat p_t}\sqrt n
 \left[(\widehat m_t-m_t)-\mu_t^\star(\widehat p_t-p_t)\right]\\
&=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n
 \frac{\phi_{m,t}(O_i)-\mu_t^\star\phi_{p,t}(O_i)}{p^\star}
 +o_P(1)\\
&=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n\phi_{\mu,t}(O_i)+o_P(1).
\end{aligned} \tag{30}
\]
Stacking the eight equalities and left-multiplying by the fixed Walsh matrix \(H\) gives
\[
\sqrt n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}-\boldsymbol\theta)
=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n\phi_\theta(O_i)+o_P(1). \tag{31}
\]
Finite second moments follow for all coordinates because assignment weights are finite constants, receipt indicators are bounded, \(\mathbb E_P[Y^2]<\infty\), and \(p^\star\ge\kappa\).  The multivariate central limit conclusion in the cited lemma therefore gives
\[
n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n\phi_\theta(O_i)\ \Rightarrow\ N(0,\Omega). \tag{32}
\]

To make the plug-in assertion explicit, take \(\widehat\Omega\) to be the empirical second moment of \(\phi_\theta\) after replacing every arm probability and cell moment by its empirical counterpart and replacing \(p^\star,\mu_t^\star\) by \(\widehat p_t,\widehat\mu_t\), with any fixed convention on the event that a denominator is zero.  Positive arm probabilities and \(p^\star\ge\kappa\) make the probability of that exceptional event tend to zero.  The plug-in consistency part of the finite-cell lemma and continuous mapping yield
\[
\widehat\Omega\to_P\Omega. \tag{33}
\]
Positive definiteness of \(\Omega\) licenses inversion.  From (31)--(33) and the Wald conclusion of the cited lemma,
\[
n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}-\boldsymbol\theta)^{\mathsf T}
\widehat\Omega^{-1}
(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}-\boldsymbol\theta)
\Rightarrow\chi_7^2. \tag{34}
\]
Taking the \((1-\alpha_0)\)-quantile in (34) proves
\[
P(\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathcal C_\theta)\longrightarrow1-\alpha_0.

%%% FIELD proof-fieller-inversion
Fix a bundle \(t\) and put \(u=\mu_t^\star\).  Signed identification gives the zero population moment
\[
m_t-u p_t=p^\star\mu_t^\star-\mu_t^\star p^\star=0. \tag{35}
\]
The finite-cell asymptotic lemma and linearity give
\[
\sqrt n\bigl[(\widehat m_t-u\widehat p_t)-(m_t-u p_t)\bigr]
=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n
\bigl(\phi_{m,t}(O_i)-u\phi_{p,t}(O_i)\bigr)+o_P(1). \tag{36}
\]
The assumed variance is strictly positive at this fixed \(u\), and finite second moments hold by the outcome moment assumption and bounded receipt indicators.  Thus the consistent plug-in variance in the definition of \(\mathcal F_t\) and the studentized conclusion of the cited lemma imply
\[
\frac{\sqrt n(\widehat m_t-u\widehat p_t)}
{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\phi_{m,t}-u\phi_{p,t})^{1/2}}
\Rightarrow N(0,1). \tag{37}
\]
Since \(z_{1-\alpha_0/16}\) is the two-sided normal critical value with tail probability \(\alpha_0/8\), (37) and the definition of \(\mathcal F_t\) give
\[
P(\mu_t^\star\in\mathcal F_t)=1-\frac{\alpha_0}{8}+o(1). \tag{38}
\]
There are exactly eight bundles.  The union bound, which requires no independence among the eight statistics, yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
P\left((\mu_t^\star)_{t\in\mathcal B}
 \in\prod_{t\in\mathcal B}\mathcal F_t\right)
&\ge1-\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}P(\mu_t^\star\notin\mathcal F_t)\\
&\ge1-\alpha_0+o(1).
\end{aligned} \tag{39}
\]
Whenever the event in (39) occurs, the identity \(\boldsymbol\theta=H\boldsymbol\mu^\star\) implies
\[
\boldsymbol\theta\in H\left(\prod_{t\in\mathcal B}\mathcal F_t\right),
\]
which proves the simultaneous Walsh coverage claim.

For fixed data, the defining Fieller inequality is a quadratic inequality in \(u\), because both \((\widehat m_t-u\widehat p_t)^2\) and the empirical variance of \(\phi_{m,t}-u\phi_{p,t}\) are quadratic polynomials in \(u\).  Its leading coefficient need not be positive; consequently its solution can be a bounded interval, a half-line, all of \(\mathbb R\), or the union of two rays, the last case being disconnected.  Products of such sets can therefore be unbounded or disconnected, and because every column of \(H\) is nonzero, configurations with separated components can retain that disconnection after the Walsh map.  Hence neither boundedness nor connectedness is asserted for the simultaneous construction.  The argument is pointwise for a fixed law with \(p^\star>0\) and positive studentizing variances; it makes no uniform claim for sequences with \(p^\star\) vanishing.
