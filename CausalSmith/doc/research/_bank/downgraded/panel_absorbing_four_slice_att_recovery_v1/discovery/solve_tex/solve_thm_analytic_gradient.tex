Fix \(P\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{face}}\) and \(d\in\{0,1\}\).  By \(\texttt{ass:cohort-overlap}\), conditioning on \(D=d\) is legitimate.  For \(x,y\in\mathcal A\), define the proof intermediates
\[
 B_x[u,j]=P(X_0=u,X_1=x,Z=j\mid D=d),\qquad
 D_{xy}=\operatorname{diag}\{Q_1[x,y],Q_2[x,y]\},
\]
and let
\[
 C_y[j,v]=P(X_3=v\mid X_2=y,Z=j,D=d),\qquad v\in\mathcal S.
\]
The rows of \(C_y\) are probability vectors, so \(C_y\mathbf 1_3=\mathbf 1_2\).  Consistency and no anticipation identify the observed path through date \(2\) with either potential path.  Conditional first-order Markov factorization and the common date-\(2\) kernel then give, entry by entry,
\[
 A_{xy}^d[u,v]
 =\sum_{j=1}^2B_x[u,j]Q_j[x,y]C_y[j,v],
 \qquad A_{xy}^d=B_xD_{xy}C_y.                              \tag{1}
\]
This is a date-specific factorization; no equality between kernels at different dates is used.

Let \(\bar C_y=C_y[:,\mathcal A]\).  Restricting (1) to active terminal columns yields
\[
 \bar A_{xy}^d=B_xD_{xy}\bar C_y.                           \tag{2}
\]
Every factor in (2) is \(2\times2\).  By \(\texttt{ass:four-slice-rank}\), every matrix on the left is nonsingular.  Hence the determinant identity for (2) implies that \(B_a,B_b,\bar C_a,\bar C_b\) and every \(D_{xy}\) are nonsingular.  In particular,
\[
 Q_j[x,y]>0\quad(j=1,2;\ x,y\in\mathcal A),                 \tag{3}
\]
because these entries are nonnegative probabilities, and both \(C_a\) and \(C_b\) have row rank two.

Substituting (2) into the observable cycle product and cancelling only invertible factors gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 K^d
 &=B_aD_{aa}\bar C_a(B_bD_{ba}\bar C_a)^{-1}
   B_bD_{bb}\bar C_b(B_aD_{ab}\bar C_b)^{-1}\\
 &=B_aD_{aa}D_{ba}^{-1}D_{bb}D_{ab}^{-1}B_a^{-1}\\
 &=B_a\operatorname{diag}(r_1,r_2)B_a^{-1},
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{4}
\]
where
\[
 r_j=\frac{Q_j[a,a]Q_j[b,b]}{Q_j[b,a]Q_j[a,b]}>0.            \tag{5}
\]
The two eigenvalues are distinct by \(\texttt{ass:simple-cycle-spectrum}\).  Thus the definitions of \(g^d\) and \(\rho_j^d\) order them decreasingly, and, with \(E_j\) the diagonal coordinate projector,
\[
 P_j^d=B_aE_jB_a^{-1}.                                      \tag{6}
\]

Absorption implies \(X_0=e\Rightarrow X_1=e\).  Consequently every path with \(X_1=a\) has \(X_0\in\mathcal A\), and summing over types gives
\[
 m_a^d=B_a\mathbf 1_2.
\]
Combining this equality with (6),
\[
 P_j^dm_a^d=B_aE_j\mathbf 1_2=B_a[:,j].                     \tag{7}
\]
Hence the matrix called \(B_a^d\) by the recovery map is exactly the unnormalized latent component predecessor matrix, including its scale.

Using (1), (3), and (7),
\[
 F_y^d=(B_a^d)^{-1}A_{ay}^d=D_{ay}C_y,
 \qquad q_{ay}^d=F_y^d\mathbf 1_3=D_{ay}\mathbf 1_2.        \tag{8}
\]
Therefore \(q_{ay}^d[j]=Q_j[a,y]>0\), and stochastic row-sum normalization gives
\[
 C_y^d=\operatorname{diag}(q_{ay}^d)^{-1}F_y^d=C_y.          \tag{9}
\]
The use of all three terminal columns in (8) is what recovers the normalization in the presence of exit.

Because \(C_a\) has row rank two,
\[
 C_a^\dagger=C_a^\top(C_aC_a^\top)^{-1},\qquad
 C_aC_a^\dagger=I_2.
\]
Equations (1) and (9) imply
\[
 W^d=A_{ba}^dC_a^\dagger=B_bD_{ba}.                         \tag{10}
\]
This matrix is nonsingular.  Absorption again gives \(m_b^d=B_b\mathbf 1_2\), and hence
\[
 c^d=(W^d)^{-1}m_b^d=D_{ba}^{-1}\mathbf 1_2>0,
 \qquad B_b^d=W^d\operatorname{diag}(c^d)=B_b.              \tag{11}
\]
Repeating (8) with \(x=b\) recovers \(Q_j[b,y]\) for \(y\in\mathcal A\).  Row stochasticity recovers
\[
 Q_j[x,e]=1-Q_j[x,a]-Q_j[x,b],\qquad x\in\mathcal A,
\]
and absorption fixes the exit row as \((0,0,1)\).  Equation (9) recovers the active rows of \(R_j^d\), while absorption again fixes its exit row.

Define the recovered unnormalized active-date-\(1\) row vector by
\[
 \widehat s_j^d
 =\bigl(\mathbf 1_2^\top B_a^d[:,j],
          \mathbf 1_2^\top B_b^d[:,j],0\bigr).              \tag{12}
\]
Equations (7), (11), and absorption show directly from the probability definition that
\[
 \widehat s_j^d=s_j^d,
 \qquad \widehat\alpha_j^d=\widehat s_j^d\mathbf 1_3
 =P(Z=j,X_1\in\mathcal A\mid D=d)=\alpha_j^d.               \tag{13}
\]
Each column of the nonsingular nonnegative matrices \(B_a\) and \(B_b\) has positive sum, so \(\alpha_j^d>0\).  Thus every division by \(\alpha_j^d\) used for an at-risk conditional law is legitimate.

Equations (1), (7)--(13) recover the entire unnormalized component sublaw on \(\{X_1\in\mathcal A\}\).  For example, for \(u,x,y\in\mathcal A\) and \(v\in\mathcal S\), its cell mass conditional on \(D=d\) is
\[
 B_x[u,j]Q_j[x,y]R_j^d[y,v],                                \tag{14}
\]
whereas for \(y=e\) it is
\[
 B_x[u,j]Q_j[x,e]\mathbf 1\{v=e\}.                         \tag{15}
\]
Summing (14)--(15) also gives the at-risk date-\(2\) law
\[
 P(X_2\in\cdot\mid D=d,Z=j,X_1\in\mathcal A)
 =(\alpha_j^d)^{-1}s_j^dQ_j.                                \tag{16}
\]

The cross-cohort labels agree.  Indeed, the ratios (5) depend only on the common matrices \(Q_j\), by \(\texttt{ass:common-date-two-transition}\), and their strict separation gives the same descending order in both cohorts.  By consistency, the control active rows satisfy \(R_j^0=U_j\).  By no anticipation, the two treated-cohort potential paths have the same \(X_2\); by \(\texttt{ass:common-first-post-untreated-transition}\), the untreated continuation in that cohort also uses \(U_j=R_j^0\).  Conditioning on \((Z,X_1,X_2)\), using (16), therefore proves the causal rather than definitional identity
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \nu_j
 &=E\!\left[\mathbf 1\{Z=j\}
  \{\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(1))-\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(0))\}
  \mid D=1\right]\\
 &=s_j^1Q_j(R_j^1-R_j^0).
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{17}
\]
Here paths with \(X_1=e\) contribute zero: no anticipation gives \(X_1(1)=X_1(0)=e\), and repeated use of \(\texttt{ass:absorbing-exit}\) gives \(X_3(1)=X_3(0)=e\).  Because \(\alpha_j^1>0\), division of (17) yields
\[
 \eta_j
 =E\!\left[\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(1))-\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(0))
 \mid D=1,Z=j,X_1\in\mathcal A\right]
 =\frac{\nu_j}{\alpha_j^1}.                                \tag{18}
\]
Summing (17) over the partition \(Z=1,2\) proves
\[
 \tau
 =E\!\left[\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(1))-\operatorname{onehot}(X_3(0))\mid D=1\right]
 =\sum_{j=1}^2\nu_j.                                       \tag{19}
\]
Equations (12)--(19) are exactly the final formula defining \(\Phi(p)\), hence \(\Phi(p)=\theta\).

Every calculation used only the observed slices and margins and is valid for every \(\widetilde P\in\mathcal F(p)\).  Therefore all quantities listed in the theorem are constant on that fiber.  The argument used neither date \(4\) nor time homogeneity.  It also shows why the conclusion stops at the active sublaw: the observable slices contain no allocation of the mass on \(X_1=e\) across latent types.

 \pi_j^d=P(Z=j\mid D=d)

%%% FIELD analytic-gradient-proof
Let \(q_d(p)=\sum_{s:D(s)=d}p_s\).  Cohort overlap gives \(q_d(p)>0\).  Every cohort-conditional entry in \(A_{xy}^d\) and \(m_x^d\) is a linear marginal of the \(126\)-cell vector divided by \(q_d(p)\).  The proof of \(\texttt{thm:active-frontier-recovery}\) shows that, at every compatible regular \(p\), all slice determinants, \(\det(B_a^d)\), \(\det(W^d)\), \(\det(B_b^d)\), row-sum normalizers, active masses, and determinants \(\det\{C_y^d(C_y^d)^\top\}\) used in a division are nonzero, while \((g^d)^2>0\).

Let \(\mathcal U_p\) be the subset of the affine hyperplane \(\{z:\mathbf 1^\top z=1\}\) on which the same finitely many denominators remain nonzero and both discriminants remain positive.  Strict inequalities of continuous functions define a relative open neighborhood, so \(\mathcal U_p\) contains \(p\).  Cohort conditioning and matrix inversion are rational maps there; the positive square root is real analytic on \((0,\infty)\); and
\[
 C^\dagger=C^\top(CC^\top)^{-1}                             \tag{1}
\]
is analytic wherever \(CC^\top\) is nonsingular.  The construction of \(\Phi\) is a finite composition of these maps, sums, and products.  Thus \(\Phi\) has a real-analytic off-model extension on \(\mathcal U_p\).  Coordinates of \(z\in\mathcal U_p\) need not be nonnegative.

The derivative is computable by the following recurrence.  For a tangent direction \(h\) satisfying \(\mathbf 1^\top h=0\), put a dot over each recovered quantity.  First,
\[
 \dot q_d=\sum_{s:D(s)=d}h_s,
 \qquad
 \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}
 \frac{p_s+\varepsilon h_s}{q_d(p+\varepsilon h)}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
 =\frac{h_s}{q_d}-\frac{p_s\dot q_d}{q_d^2}.                \tag{2}
\]
All slice derivatives are the corresponding marginal sums.  Next,
\[
 \dot M^{-1}=-M^{-1}\dot M M^{-1},
 \qquad
 \dot g=\frac{2\operatorname{tr}(K)\operatorname{tr}(\dot K)-4\dot{\det K}}{2g},              \tag{3}
\]
where
\[
 \dot{\det K}=K_{22}\dot K_{11}+K_{11}\dot K_{22}
              -K_{21}\dot K_{12}-K_{12}\dot K_{21}.       \tag{4}
\]
Furthermore,
\[
 \dot\rho_{1,2}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\dot K)\mathbin{\pm}\dot g}{2},\qquad
 \dot P_1=\frac{\dot K-\dot\rho_2I_2}{g}-\frac{\dot g}{g}P_1,
 \qquad \dot P_2=-\dot P_1,                               \tag{5}
\]
and
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \dot B_a[:,j]&=\dot P_jm_a+P_j\dot m_a,\\
 \dot F_y&=-B_a^{-1}\dot B_aF_y+B_a^{-1}\dot A_{ay},\\
 \dot q_{ay}&=\dot F_y\mathbf 1_3,\\
 \dot C_y[j,:]&=\{\dot F_y[j,:]-\dot q_{ay}[j]C_y[j,:]\}/q_{ay}[j].
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{6}
\]
Writing \(C=C_a\) and \(N=(CC^\top)^{-1}\), differentiation of (1) gives
\[
 \dot C^\dagger
 =\dot C^\top N-C^\top N(\dot C C^\top+C\dot C^\top)N,   \tag{7}
\]
followed by
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \dot W&=\dot A_{ba}C^\dagger+A_{ba}\dot C^\dagger,\\
 \dot c&=W^{-1}(\dot m_b-\dot Wc),\\
 \dot B_b&=\dot W\operatorname{diag}(c)+W\operatorname{diag}(\dot c).
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{8}
\]
For \(y\in\mathcal A\),
\[
 \dot q_{by}=-B_b^{-1}\dot B_bq_{by}+B_b^{-1}\dot A_{by}\mathbf 1_3.                         \tag{9}
\]
The active entries of \(\dot{\widehat Q}_j^d\) are supplied by (6) and (9), its exit entry is minus their sum, and its absorbing-row derivative is zero.  The active rows of \(\dot{\widehat R}_j^d\) are the corresponding rows of \(\dot C_y^d\), and its absorbing-row derivative is zero.  Finally,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat s_j^d&=(\mathbf 1_2^\top B_a^d[:,j],\mathbf 1_2^\top B_b^d[:,j],0),\\
 \dot{\widehat\alpha}_j^d&=\dot{\widehat s}_j^d\mathbf 1_3,\\
 \dot{\widehat\nu}_j
 &=\dot{\widehat s}_j^1\widehat Q_j^1(\widehat R_j^1-\widehat R_j^0)
  +\widehat s_j^1\dot{\widehat Q}_j^1(\widehat R_j^1-\widehat R_j^0)\\
 &\quad+\widehat s_j^1\widehat Q_j^1(\dot{\widehat R}_j^1-\dot{\widehat R}_j^0),\\
 \dot{\widehat\eta}_j
 &=\{\dot{\widehat\nu}_j\widehat\alpha_j^1
      -\widehat\nu_j\dot{\widehat\alpha}_j^1\}/(\widehat\alpha_j^1)^2,\\
 \dot{\widehat\tau}&=\sum_{j=1}^2\dot{\widehat\nu}_j.
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{10}
\]
Equations (2)--(10) determine \(J_{\Phi}(p)h\).  In particular, they propagate the estimated unnormalized active masses rather than treating mixture weights as fixed.

We now derive the saturated canonical gradient.  Let \(\mathcal S_0\) be the finite support stratum containing \(p\), and assume \(p_s>0\) for every \(s\in\mathcal S_0\), which is relative interiority on that stratum.  A saturated score is exactly a function \(a:\mathcal S_0\to\mathbb R\) satisfying
\[
 E_Pa(O)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_0}p_sa(s)=0.                 \tag{11}
\]
Conversely, because \(\mathcal S_0\) is finite, \(p_{\varepsilon,s}=p_s\{1+\varepsilon a(s)\}\) is a probability law for all sufficiently small \(|\varepsilon|\), and it has score \(a\).  Thus the tangent space is all of \(L_2^0(P)\).  Differentiability and (11) yield
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Phi(p_\varepsilon)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
 &=J_{\Phi}(p)\sum_s p_se_sa(s)\\
 &=E_P\!\left[J_{\Phi}(p)(e_O-p)a(O)\right]
 =E_P\{G_p(O)a(O)\}.
 \end{aligned}                                               \tag{12}
\]
Also \(E_PG_p(O)=0\).  If \(H\) is any other centered gradient, the score equations imply \(E_P\{(H-G_p)a\}=0\) for every \(a\in L_2^0(P)\).  Taking each coordinate of \(H-G_p\) as \(a\) proves \(H=G_p\) almost surely.  Hence \(G_p\) is the canonical gradient, and its covariance is
\[
 E_P\{G_p(O)G_p(O)^\top\}
 =J_{\Phi}(p)\{\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top\}J_{\Phi}(p)^\top
 =\Sigma(p).                                                \tag{13}
\]
Because every regular asymptotically linear estimator has a centered influence function satisfying the same score equations, (12) forces its influence component to be \(G_p\); thus (13) is the saturated information bound.

Finally, the exact multinomial identity is
\[
 \sqrt n(\widehat p-p)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^n(e_{O_i}-p),
 \qquad \widehat p-p=O_P(n^{-1/2}).                         \tag{14}
\]
Taylor expansion of the analytic map at fixed interior \(p\), \(\texttt{ass:iid-panels}\), and (14) give
\[
 \sqrt n\{\Phi(\widehat p)-\Phi(p)\}
 =n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nJ_{\Phi}(p)(e_{O_i}-p)+o_P(1)
 =n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nG_p(O_i)+o_P(1).                     \tag{15}
\]
The empirical-cell plug-in therefore has canonical influence function \(G_p\) and attains (13).

Let \(\mathcal K_\kappa\) be the set of \(126\)-cell vectors induced by \(\mathcal P_\kappa\).  It is compact by \(\texttt{def:buffered-class}\).  Every inverse, division, eigengap, normalization, and reported variance required by the recovery algorithm appears in \(\mathfrak m(p)\) or in the variance buffer.  The population margins are at least \(2\kappa\), whereas the empirical guard uses \(\kappa\).

Analyticity, compactness, and the finite margin list give a fixed \(r>0\) such that the relative closed \(r\)-neighborhood of \(\mathcal K_\kappa\) lies in the analytic domain, every algebraic guard there exceeds \(\kappa\), and every reported variance there exceeds \(\kappa\).  On that neighborhood there are finite constants
\[
 L_1=\sup_q\|J_{\Phi}(q)\|<\infty,
 \qquad L_2=\sup_q\|D^2\Phi(q)\|<\infty.                 \tag{1}
\]
The support-legality guard holds almost surely because every law in the class assigns zero probability to cells forbidden by the enlarged absorbing graph.  Direct multinomial calculation gives
\[
 E_P\|\widehat p-p\|_2^2
 =\frac{1-\|p\|_2^2}{n}\leq\frac1n.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \sup_{P\in\mathcal P_\kappa}P\{\|\widehat p-p\|_2>r\}
 \leq\frac1{nr^2}.                                         \tag{2}
\]
On the complementary event every sequential calculation is legal and every empirical margin exceeds \(\kappa\).  This proves
\[
 \inf_{P\in\mathcal P_\kappa}P(\mathcal E_\kappa)\longrightarrow1.                           \tag{3}
\]

Taylor's formula and (1) give, on this event,
\[
 \left\|\sqrt n\{\Phi(\widehat p)-\Phi(p)-J_{\Phi}(p)(\widehat p-p)\}\right\|
 \leq\frac{L_2}{2}\sqrt n\|\widehat p-p\|_2^2.           \tag{4}
\]
For each \(\varepsilon>0\), Markov's inequality bounds the probability that the right side exceeds \(\varepsilon\) by \(L_2/(2\varepsilon\sqrt n)\).  Combining this with (2), \(\Phi(p)=\theta\) from \(\texttt{thm:active-frontier-recovery}\), and
\[
 J_{\Phi}(p)\sqrt n(\widehat p-p)
 =n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nG_p(O_i)
\]
proves the asserted uniform asymptotic linear representation.  Assigning any fixed value off the guard event is asymptotically irrelevant by (3).

The empirical covariance has the exact finite-cell form
\[
 \widehat\Sigma
 =J_{\Phi}(\widehat p)
   \{\operatorname{diag}(\widehat p)-\widehat p\widehat p^\top\}
   J_{\Phi}(\widehat p)^\top.                              \tag{5}
\]
Indeed, (5) follows by expanding the empirical second moment of \(e_{O_i}-\widehat p\).  The right side is uniformly continuous on the compact neighborhood in (1).  Equations (2) and (5) imply, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
 \sup_{P\in\mathcal P_\kappa}
 P\{\|\widehat\Sigma-\Sigma(p)\|>\varepsilon\}\longrightarrow0.                            \tag{6}
\]
Thus each reported empirical diagonal exceeds \(\kappa\) with probability tending to one uniformly.  No full covariance inverse is used.

It remains to establish simultaneous coverage, including singular correlations.  Take an arbitrary sequence \(P_n\in\mathcal P_\kappa\).  Compactness yields a subsequence, not relabelled, with \(p_n\to p_\star\).  Continuity and the variance buffer give
\[
 \Sigma(p_n)\to\Sigma(p_\star),
 \qquad \sigma_{n,\ell}^2:=\Sigma_{\ell\ell}(p_n)\geq2\kappa.                               \tag{7}
\]
For \(\ell\in\mathcal L\), define
\[
 Y_{n,i,\ell}=G_{p_n,\ell}(O_i)/\sigma_{n,\ell},
 \qquad \Gamma_n=E_{P_n}(Y_{n,i}Y_{n,i}^\top).
\]
The diagonal of \(\Gamma_n\) is one.  Equations (1) and (7) make the summands uniformly bounded, and after taking a further subsequence \(\Gamma_n\to\Gamma_\star\), where \(\Gamma_\star\) may be singular.

For every fixed \(u\), centering and the third-order Taylor formula for the exponential, with a uniform remainder because the summands are bounded, yield
\[
 E_{P_n}\exp\{iu^\top Y_{n,1}/\sqrt n\}
 =1-\frac{u^\top\Gamma_nu}{2n}+O(n^{-3/2}).                \tag{8}
\]
Independence then implies
\[
 E_{P_n}\exp\!\left\{iu^\top n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nY_{n,i}\right\}
 =\left(1-\frac{u^\top\Gamma_nu}{2n}+O(n^{-3/2})\right)^n
 \longrightarrow\exp(-u^\top\Gamma_\star u/2).           \tag{9}
\]
Thus the studentized linear term converges to \(Z_\star\sim N(0,\Gamma_\star)\), including a singular limit.

Conditionally on the sample, the multiplier numerator is exactly Gaussian.  Coordinatewise studentization gives empirical correlation
\[
 \widehat\Gamma_{\ell k}
 =\frac{\widehat\Sigma_{\ell k}}
 {\sqrt{\widehat\Sigma_{\ell\ell}\widehat\Sigma_{kk}}}.  \tag{10}
\]
Equations (6)--(7) imply \(\widehat\Gamma\to\Gamma_\star\) in \(P_n\)-probability.  Applying \(\texttt{lem:gaussian-max-quantile-continuity}\) at \(u=1-\alpha\) gives
\[
 \widehat c_{1-\alpha}\longrightarrow c_\star             \tag{11}
\]
in probability, where \(c_\star\) is the unique \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of \(\max_{\ell\in\mathcal L}|Z_{\star,\ell}|\), and
\[
 P\{\max_{\ell\in\mathcal L}|Z_{\star,\ell}|\leq c_\star\}=1-\alpha.                    \tag{12}
\]
The uniform linearization, (6), (9), and positive diagonals show that the coverage event differs by probability \(o(1)\) from
\[
 \left\{\max_{\ell\in\mathcal L}
 \frac{|n^{-1/2}\sum_iG_{p_n,\ell}(O_i)|}{\sigma_{n,\ell}}
 \leq\widehat c_{1-\alpha}\right\}.
\]
Equations (11)--(12) give coverage tending to \(1-\alpha\) on the selected subsequence.  Since the original sequence was arbitrary, the contradiction-subsequence criterion yields
\[
 \sup_{P\in\mathcal P_\kappa}
 \left|P\{\theta_\ell\in I_{\ell,n}\text{ for every }\ell\in\mathcal L\}-(1-\alpha)\right|
 \longrightarrow0.                                         \tag{13}
\]
The theorem's displayed infimum follows.  The proof nowhere assumes a positive smallest eigenvalue of \(\Sigma(p)\) or its correlation matrix.

Fix \(P\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{5d}}\), and write \(\langle f,h\rangle_P=E_P\{f(O)h(O)\}\).  The active-frontier theorem proves that the causal target equals \(\Phi(p)\) on every constrained parametric submodel through \(P\), while the analytic-gradient theorem gives
\[
 \dot\theta_P[h]=E_P\{G_p(O)h(O)\},\qquad h\in\mathcal T_P.                                  \tag{1}
\]
The tangent space is closed by definition.  Hence its orthogonal projection exists.  Define coordinatewise
\[
 \psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell}=\Pi_{\mathcal T_P}G_{p,\ell}.
\]
Since \(G_{p,\ell}-\psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell}\) is orthogonal to \(\mathcal T_P\),
\[
 \dot\theta_{P,\ell}[h]
 =\langle G_{p,\ell},h\rangle_P
 =\langle\psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell},h\rangle_P
 \quad(h\in\mathcal T_P).                                  \tag{2}
\]
If another element of \(\mathcal T_P\) represents the same derivative, its difference from \(\psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell}\) lies in \(\mathcal T_P\) and is orthogonal to it, hence is zero.  This proves the efficient-gradient characterization and the covariance formula for \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}(P)\).

Put \(R_\ell=(I-\Pi_{\mathcal T_P})G_{p,\ell}\).  Orthogonality gives
\[
 \Sigma_{\ell k}(p)
 =\langle\psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell},\psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,k}\rangle_P
  +\langle R_\ell,R_k\rangle_P.                            \tag{3}
\]
Thus, for every \(a\in\mathbb R^9\),
\[
 a^\top\{\Sigma(p)-V_{\mathrm{eff}}(P)\}a
 =\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^9a_\ell R_\ell\right\|_{L_2(P)}^2
 =\|(I-\Pi_{\mathcal T_P})a^\top G_p\|_{L_2(P)}^2.        \tag{4}
\]
This proves both positive-semidefinite dominance and the exact strictness criterion.

For the promised computation, enumerate the actual support as \(s=1,\ldots,m_P\), let \(W_P=\operatorname{diag}(p_1,\ldots,p_{m_P})\), and let the columns of a finite matrix \(S_P\) span the score space obtained by differentiating the absorbing support, two-type factorization, common-kernel, and date-\(4\) restrictions defining \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{5d}}\).  \min_{\beta\in\mathbb R^{k_P}}
 \sum_{s=1}^{m_P}p_s\{G_{p,\ell}(s)-S_P[s,:]\beta\}^2.     \tag{5}
\]
The normal equations and unique fitted value are
\[
 S_P^\top W_PS_P\beta=S_P^\top W_PG_{p,\ell},
 \qquad
 \psi_{\mathrm{eff},P,\ell}
 =S_P(S_P^\top W_PS_P)^\dagger S_P^\top W_PG_{p,\ell}.     \tag{6}
\]
The Moore--Penrose inverse makes the fitted value invariant to redundant score generators.

On a smooth local stratum of constant tangent rank, choose independent score coordinates \(\zeta\) for which \(I_P=S_P^\top W_PS_P\) is nonsingular.  The local likelihood score expansion is
\[
 \sqrt n(\widehat\zeta-\zeta)
 =I_P^{-1}n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nS_P[O_i,:]^\top+o_P(1).       \tag{7}
\]
Equation (1) gives the target derivative
\[
 \dot\theta_{\ell,\zeta}=G_{p,\ell}^\top W_PS_P.           \tag{8}
\]
Combining (7)--(8) produces exactly (6), so the corresponding local likelihood estimator, or its one-step equivalent, attains \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}(P)\) pointwise on that stratum.

%%% FIELD gaussian-max-proof
Let \(L=\operatorname{range}(\Gamma^{1/2})\).  Since every diagonal entry of \(\Gamma\) is one, \(L\neq\{0\}\), and the law of \(Z\) has a strictly positive Gaussian density with respect to Lebesgue measure on \(L\).  The restriction of \(x\mapsto\|x\|_\infty\) to \(L\) is a norm.  For \(0\leq s<t\), choose nonzero \(x\in L\) and rescale it so that \(s<\|x\|_\infty<t\).  The set
\[
 \{y\in L:s<\|y\|_\infty<t\}
\]
contains a nonempty relatively open subset of \(L\), and hence has strictly positive Gaussian probability.  Thus the distribution function of \(\|Z\|_\infty\) is strictly increasing on \([0,\infty)\).

For every \(t\geq0\),
\[
 \{\|Z\|_\infty=t\}\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^q\{|Z_k|=t\}.
\]
Every marginal \(Z_k\) is \(N(0,1)\), even when the joint covariance is singular, so the finite union has probability zero.  The distribution function is continuous, starts at zero, and tends to one.  Its \(u\)-quantile \(c_u(\Gamma)\) is therefore unique for every \(u\in(0,1)\).

For every \(v\in\mathbb R^q\),
\[
 E\exp(i v^\top Z_n)=\exp(-v^\top\Gamma_nv/2)
 \longrightarrow\exp(-v^\top\Gamma v/2)=E\exp(i v^\top Z).
\]
Thus \(Z_n\) converges in distribution to \(Z\), including when \(\Gamma\) is singular.  Continuity of the maximum norm implies convergence in distribution of the maxima.  If \(F_n,F\) are their distribution functions and \(c=c_u(\Gamma)\), strict increase and continuity give, for every sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
 F(c-\varepsilon)<u<F(c+\varepsilon).
\]
Convergence at the two continuity points makes the same inequalities true for \(F_n\) for all large \(n\), whence
\[
 c-\varepsilon<c_u(\Gamma_n)<c+\varepsilon.
\]
Letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) proves quantile convergence without a lower eigenvalue bound.
