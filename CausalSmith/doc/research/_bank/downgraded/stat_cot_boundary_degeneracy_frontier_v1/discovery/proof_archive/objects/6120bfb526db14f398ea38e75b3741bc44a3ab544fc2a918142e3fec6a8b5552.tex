Put \(K_h=\|h/q\|_\infty\). For \(|t|K_h<2\),
\[
q_{0,t}=q\left(1-\frac{t h}{2q}\right)
\ge c\left(1-\frac{|t|K_h}{2}\right)>0,
\qquad
q_{1,t}=q\left(1+\frac{t h}{2q}\right)
\ge c\left(1-\frac{|t|K_h}{2}\right)>0.
\tag{1}
\]
Moreover, the conditional-zero-mass condition on \(h\) gives
\[
\int_{\mathcal Y}q_{a,t}(y,z)dy=\int_{\mathcal Y}q(y,z)dy=1
\quad(a=0,1)
\tag{2}
\]
for \(R_n\)-almost every \(z\). Thus (1)--(2), together with the common marginal in \(\mathrm{ass{:}shared\text{-}covariate\text{-}marginal}\), prove the asserted admissibility of the path. The convention in the statement covers \(K_h=0\).

Fix a covariate value \(z\) for which (2) holds, and define
\[
F(y,z)=\int_0^yq(x,z)dx,
\qquad
H_h(y,z)=\int_0^yh(x,z)dx.
\tag{3}
\]
Then \(H_h(0,z)=H_h(1,z)=0\), and the two perturbed distribution functions are
\[
F_{0,t}=F-\frac{t}{2}H_h,
\qquad
F_{1,t}=F+\frac{t}{2}H_h.
\tag{4}
\]
By \(c\le q\le C\), \(F(\cdot,z)\) is strictly increasing and bi-Lipschitz. Let \(Q=F^{-1}\) and \(Q_{a,t}=F_{a,t}^{-1}\). The inverse identities and the lower Lipschitz bound for \(F\) imply
\[
\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|Q_{a,t}(u,z)-Q(u,z)|
\le \frac{|t|}{2c}\|H_h(\cdot,z)\|_\infty.
\tag{5}
\]
At every Lebesgue point \(Q(u,z)\) of \(q(\cdot,z)\), divide
\[
F\{Q_{1,t}(u,z),z\}-F\{Q(u,z),z\}
=-\frac{t}{2}H_h\{Q_{1,t}(u,z),z\}
\tag{6}
\]
by \(t\), use (5), and apply the one-dimensional differentiation theorem to the secant quotient of the absolutely continuous function \(F\). This gives
\[
\frac{Q_{1,t}(u,z)-Q(u,z)}{t}
\longrightarrow-\frac{H_h\{Q(u,z),z\}}{2q\{Q(u,z),z\}}.
\tag{7}
\]
The same argument applied to the first identity in (4) gives
\[
\frac{Q_{0,t}(u,z)-Q(u,z)}{t}
\longrightarrow\frac{H_h\{Q(u,z),z\}}{2q\{Q(u,z),z\}}.
\tag{8}
\]
The exceptional set in (7)--(8) has Lebesgue measure zero in \(u\), because the image under \(F\) of a Lebesgue-null set has measure \(\int q\,dy=0\) on that set. Also, (5) bounds both difference quotients by \(\|H_h(\cdot,z)\|_\infty/(2c)\). Hence dominated convergence, first in \(u\) and then in \(z\), is applicable because \(h\) is bounded and \(R_n\) is a probability measure.

For scalar distributions on an interval, the increasing quantile coupling is optimal for squared cost: if \(x\le x'\) and \(y\le y'\), direct expansion yields
\[
(x-y)^2+(x'-y')^2\le(x-y')^2+(x'-y)^2,
\tag{9}
\]
and successive uncrossing proves the quantile formula. Therefore (7)--(9) give
\[
\begin{split}
\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\theta_n(t)}{t^2}
&=\int_{\mathcal Z_d}\int_0^1
\frac{H_h\{Q(u,z),z\}^2}{q\{Q(u,z),z\}^2},du\,dR_n(z)\\
&=\int_{\mathcal Z_d}\int_0^1\frac{H_h(y,z)^2}{q(y,z)},dy\,dR_n(z),
\end{split}
\tag{10}
\]
where the second equality uses \(u=F(y,z)\) and \(du=q(y,z)dy\). Since \(\theta_n(0)=0\), (10) proves \(\theta_n'(0)=0\) and that one half of its second pathwise derivative equals the last integral in (10).

At the common density, \(\bar q_n=q\). Let \(u_h=\mathcal G_{n,z}h\). Integrating its defining weak Neumann equation and using \(H_h(0,z)=0\) gives
\[
q(y,z)\partial_yu_h(y,z)=-H_h(y,z).
\tag{11}
\]
The zero-mass condition gives \(H_h(1,z)=0\), so integration by parts has no boundary term. Consequently,
\[
\int_0^1h(y,z)u_h(y,z)dy
=\int_0^1q(y,z)|\partial_yu_h(y,z)|^2dy
=\int_0^1\frac{H_h(y,z)^2}{q(y,z)}dy.
\tag{12}
\]
Integrating (12) against \(R_n\) identifies (10) with the asserted inverse-elliptic quadratic form.

This form has the claimed \(L^2\)-continuous extension. Indeed, the Volterra map \(Vh(y,z)=\int_0^yh(x,z)dx\) satisfies, by Cauchy--Schwarz and Fubini,
\[
\|Vh\|_{L^2(dy\,dR_n)}^2
\le\|h\|_{L^2(dy\,dR_n)}^2.
\tag{13}
\]
Thus, using \(q\ge c\),
\[
0\le\int\frac{|Vh|^2}{q}\,dy\,dR_n
\le c^{-1}\|h\|_{L^2(dy\,dR_n)}^2.
\tag{14}
\]
Polarization and (14) show continuity of the associated bilinear form, and bounded zero-mass directions are dense in \(L_0^2\). This proves only extension of the quadratic form, not differentiability on an \(L^2\)-open set of densities.

It remains to compute the announced constants. For the displayed cosine pair put \(e=b(z)/2\). Conditional on \(z\), the distribution functions are
\[
F_{1,e}(y)=y+\frac{e}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi y),
\qquad
F_{0,e}(y)=y-\frac{e}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi y).
\tag{15}
\]
For \(\|b\|_\infty\) sufficiently small, their derivatives are uniformly bounded away from zero. Taylor expansion of the two inverse identities, uniformly in \(u\) and \(z\), yields
\[
F_{1,e}^{-1}(u)-F_{0,e}^{-1}(u)
=-\frac{b(z)}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi u)+O\{|b(z)|^2\}.
\tag{16}
\]
The left-hand side of (16) is \(O\{|b(z)|\}\). Squaring, integrating, and using \(\int_0^1\sin^2(2\pi u)du=1/2\) gives
\[
W_2^2\{Q_{0,n}(\cdot\mid z),Q_{1,n}(\cdot\mid z)\}
=\frac{b(z)^2}{8\pi^2}+O\{|b(z)|^3\}.
\tag{17}
\]
Since \(R_n\) is a probability measure, integrating (17) gives the first display of the statement with remainder \(O(\|b\|_\infty^3)\).

Finally, for the one-sided path \(q_{0,n}=1\), \(q_{1,n}=1+t b(z)\cos(2\pi y)\), the same inverse calculation gives
\[
Q_{1,t}(u,z)-Q_{0,t}(u,z)
=-\frac{t b(z)}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi u)+O(t^2b(z)^2).
\tag{18}
\]
Hence
\[
\theta_n(t)=\frac{t^2}{8\pi^2}\int b(z)^2dR_n(z)+o(t^2),
\tag{19}
\]
which proves the stated second derivative. Every downstream use of this lemma is within the repaired domain: the lower-bound and regular two-point arguments use bounded cosine directions, the spectral argument uses finite wavelet sums, and the pilot expansion uses bounded differences of clipped conditional densities. Thus the domain repair changes no downstream formula or coefficient.
