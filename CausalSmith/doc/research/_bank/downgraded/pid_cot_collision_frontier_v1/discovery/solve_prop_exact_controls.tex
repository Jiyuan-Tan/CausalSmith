Write \(h(\rho)=a-b\rho\).  This is the target in the marked-Poisson experiment, whereas \(R(\lambda)\) and \(C_\alpha(\lambda)\) are the decision functionals of the observable law defined in \(\mathrm{def{:}poisson\mbox{-}decision\mbox{-}frontiers}\).  The declared ranges give \(b>0\), \(c>0\), \(0<r<1\), and \(cr<1\).  Consequently all mark probabilities are strictly positive, and every division below by \(b\), \(c\), or \(1-cx\), \(0\leq x\leq r\), is valid.

First take \(\lambda=0\).  By \(\mathrm{def{:}marked\mbox{-}poisson\mbox{-}experiment}\), \(N=0\) almost surely, so every randomized action has a distribution independent of \(\rho\).  Put \(t_-=a-br=h(r)\) and \(t_+=a+br=h(-r)\).  For any randomized endpoint action \(D\), the triangle inequality gives pointwise
\[
 |D-t_-|+|D-t_+|\geq |t_+-t_-|=2br.
\]
Taking expectations and then the larger of the two endpoint risks yields
\[
 \sup_{\rho\in[-r,r]}\mathbb E_{0,\rho}|D-h(\rho)|
 \geq \frac12\left\{\mathbb E|D-t_-|+\mathbb E|D-t_+|\right\}
 \geq br.
\]
The deterministic action \(D=a\) has loss \(b|\rho|\leq br\), with equality at \(\rho=\pm r\).  Thus \(R(0)=br\).

For a randomized closed interval \(I\subseteq\mathcal A=[t_-,t_+]\), the same absence of data makes its law independent of \(\rho\).  The elementary indicator representation of interval length and nonnegative integration give
\[
 \mathbb E\operatorname{len}(I)
 =\int_{t_-}^{t_+}\Pr\{t\in I\}\,dt.
\]
Honesty requires the integrand to be at least \(1-\alpha\) for every \(t=h(\rho)\in[t_-,t_+]\), because \(h\) maps \([-r,r]\) bijectively onto that interval.  Hence
\[
 \mathbb E\operatorname{len}(I)\geq (t_+-t_-)(1-\alpha)=2br(1-\alpha).
\]
This lower bound is attained by reporting \(\mathcal A\) with probability \(1-\alpha\) and the degenerate interval \(\{a\}\) with probability \(\alpha\), using parameter-free randomization.  Every target value other than \(a\) is then covered with probability \(1-\alpha\), while \(a\) is covered with probability one, and the expected length is \(2br(1-\alpha)\).  Therefore \(C_\alpha(0)=2br(1-\alpha)\).

Now fix any \(\lambda>0\), condition the experiment of \(\mathrm{def{:}marked\mbox{-}poisson\mbox{-}experiment}\) on exactly one mark, and denote that mark by \(S\).  (The resulting conditional experiment does not depend on \(\lambda\).)  Then
\[
 \Pr_\rho(S=s)=\frac{1+sc\rho}{2},\qquad s\in\{-1,1\}.
\]
For endpoint estimation, transform an action \(D\in[a-b,a+b]\) to \(q=(a-D)/b\in[-1,1]\).  The loss obeys
\[
 |D-h(\rho)|=b|q-\rho|.
\]
Projection of \(q\) onto \([-r,r]\) cannot increase \(|q-\rho|\) for any \(\rho\in[-r,r]\).  Next apply reflection symmetrization: mix a rule with the rule obtained by replacing \(S\) by \(-S\) and its action \(q\) by \(-q\).  At \(\rho\), the symmetrized risk is the average of the original risks at \(\rho\) and \(-\rho\), so its worst risk is no larger.  It is therefore enough for a lower bound to consider equivariant rules.  Such a rule is described by a random variable \(Q\in[-r,r]\): conditionally on \(S=1\) output \(Q\), and conditionally on \(S=-1\) output a variable distributed as \(-Q\).  Let \(m=\mathbb E Q\).  Its correlation-scale risks at \(0\) and \(r\) are
\[
 \mathcal R(0)=\mathbb E|Q|\geq m
\]
and, using \(Q\in[-r,r]\),
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal R(r)
 &=\mathbb E\left[\frac{1+cr}{2}|Q-r|+\frac{1-cr}{2}|-Q-r|\right]\\
 &=\mathbb E\left[\frac{1+cr}{2}(r-Q)+\frac{1-cr}{2}(r+Q)\right]
 =r-crm.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Set \(t=r/(1+cr)\), so that \(r-crt=t\).  If \(m\geq t\), then \(\mathcal R(0)\geq t\); if \(m<t\), then \(\mathcal R(r)=r-crm>r-crt=t\).  Thus every rule has worst correlation-scale absolute risk at least \(t\).

For attainment, use \(q=tS\).  By reflection symmetry it suffices to take \(x=|\rho|\in[0,r]\).  Direct substitution gives
\[
 \mathcal R(x)=
 \begin{cases}
 t-cx^2,&0\leq x\leq t,\\
 x(1-ct),&t\leq x\leq r.
 \end{cases}
\]
The first expression is at most \(t\).  Since \(ct<1\), the second is increasing in \(x\) and is at most
\[
 r(1-ct)=r-crt=t.
\]
Hence the one-mark minimax endpoint risk is \(bt=br/(1+cr)\).

It remains to solve the one-mark honest-interval problem.  Map every endpoint interval \(I\subseteq\mathcal A\) to
\[
 J=\{(a-y)/b:y\in I\}\subseteq[-r,r].
\]
Then \(h(\rho)\in I\) if and only if \(\rho\in J\), and \(\operatorname{len}(I)=b\operatorname{len}(J)\).  Reflection symmetrization of an arbitrary honest rule preserves honesty and replaces its expected-length function at \(\rho\) by the average of the original functions at \(\rho\) and \(-\rho\); hence it cannot increase worst expected length.  Under a symmetric rule, the conditional law of \(J\) given \(S=-1\) is the reflection of its conditional law given \(S=1\), so the two conditional expected lengths agree and the unconditional expected length is constant in \(\rho\).

Let \(J_+\) have the conditional law given \(S=1\), and for \(0<x\leq r\) define
\[
 u(x)=\Pr\{x\in J_+\},\qquad v(x)=\Pr\{-x\in J_+\}.
\]
Honesty at \(\rho=x\) is exactly
\[
 (1+cx)u(x)+(1-cx)v(x)\geq 2(1-\alpha).
\]
Assume \(cr\leq1-2\alpha\).  Then \(1-cx>0\) and \(1+cx\leq2(1-\alpha)\) for \(0<x\leq r\).  Since \(0\leq u(x)\leq1\),
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 2(1-\alpha)
 &\leq (1-cx)\{u(x)+v(x)\}+2cx\,u(x)\\
 &\leq (1-cx)\{u(x)+v(x)\}+2cx,
 \end{aligned}
\]
and therefore
\[
 u(x)+v(x)\geq 2-\frac{2\alpha}{1-cx}.
\]
Integrating inclusion indicators over \([-r,r]\) gives the common conditional expected length
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathbb E\operatorname{len}(J_+)
 &=\int_0^r\{u(x)+v(x)\}\,dx\\
 &\geq\int_0^r\left(2-\frac{2\alpha}{1-cx}\right)dx
 =2r+\frac{2\alpha}{c}\log(1-cr).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Because symmetrization cannot increase the original worst expected length, this is a lower bound for every honest one-mark rule.

For attainability, the condition \(cr\leq1-2\alpha\) ensures \(2\alpha/(1-cr)\leq1\).  Define a random variable \(T\in[0,r]\) by the distribution function
\[
 \Pr(T\leq t)=\frac{2\alpha}{1-ct},\qquad 0\leq t<r,
\]
and set \(\Pr(T\leq r)=1\).  This is a valid distribution: it has mass \(2\alpha\) at zero, is increasing on \((0,r)\), and has the nonnegative remaining mass \(1-2\alpha/(1-cr)\) at \(r\).  In particular,
\[
 \Pr(T\geq x)=1-\frac{2\alpha}{1-cx},\qquad 0<x\leq r.
\]
Given \(S=1\), report \(J=[-T,r]\); given \(S=-1\), report \(J=[-r,T]\), with fresh randomization having the displayed law.  For \(0<x\leq r\), its coverage at \(\rho=x\) is
\[
 \frac{1+cx}{2}+\frac{1-cx}{2}\Pr(T\geq x)
 =\frac{1+cx}{2}+\frac{1-cx}{2}-\alpha
 =1-\alpha.
\]
Coverage at \(-x\) is the same by reflection, and coverage at zero is one because both reported intervals contain zero.  Thus the rule is uniformly honest, including at the boundary points.  Its conditional expected correlation-scale length is
\[
 r+\mathbb ET
 =r+\int_0^r\Pr(T\geq x)\,dx
 =2r+\frac{2\alpha}{c}\log(1-cr).
\]
Mapping back through \(I=a-bJ\) multiplies length by \(b\).  The lower bound and this attaining interval rule prove that the one-mark minimax expected endpoint-interval length is
\[
 b\left\{2r+\frac{2\alpha}{c}\log(1-cr)\right\}.
\]
