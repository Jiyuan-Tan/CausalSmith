\section*{exp\_\allowbreak{}weaknull\_\allowbreak{}alphabet\_\allowbreak{}symmetry\_\allowbreak{}frontier}

\noindent\textit{Specialization:} v1\par

\paragraph{TL;DR.} Exact weak-null symmetry has an allocation-balance elbow and an exact sparse-separator phase diagram. Under balanced complete randomization, binary outcomes retain precisely the arm-transpose classes and have worst-case separator number zero. Alphabets with \(K\geq3\) retain only the opposing endpoint-corner pair; their worst-case separator number is two for \(m\in\{1,2\}\) and three for \(m\geq3\). Every unequal complete-randomization allocation has only singleton classes, identity maximal common group, and worst-case separator number two. Consequently conventional nonrandomized common-orbit ranks lie in \(\{1/2,1\}\) when balanced and are identically \(1\) when unequal. An explicit non-orbit exact p-value prevents any broader impossibility interpretation.

\section{Project justification}

\paragraph{Gap.} Weak-null randomization theory supplies asymptotic studentization or exact inversion over compatible schedules, while general permutation theory begins with a specified invariant group. It does not compute how the probability-equivalence partition of all fixed zero-average-effect schedules changes exactly at equal versus unequal arm allocation.

\paragraph{Niche.} For a finite outcome alphabet, fixed potential-outcome schedules and complete randomization produce an exact multivariate-hypergeometric coefficient system. Computing its common stabilizer exposes allocation balance, rather than alphabet size alone, as the decisive symmetry boundary.

\paragraph{Fill.} The paper classifies the balanced and unequal-arm equivalence partitions, derives their maximal common bijection groups, and certifies the complete finite-sample sparse-separator phase diagram. The balanced value is zero for \(K=2\), two for \(K\geq3\) with \(m\in\{1,2\}\), and three for \(K\geq3\) with \(m\geq3\); every unequal allocation has value two. It then derives the corresponding conventional orbit-rank granularity while retaining a constructive non-orbit exact counterexample to any general impossibility claim.

\section{Related work}

\cite{WuDing2021} frames the weak-null obstacle through missing potential outcomes and obtains asymptotic control by studentization. \cite{HemerikGoeman2018} supplies orbit-counting validity once a finite invariant group is given; here the group is computed from the full composite causal null. \cite{DutzZhang2026} concerns invariant bijections for i.i.d. finite-support product laws. The present paper instead conditions on a fixed potential-outcome schedule and studies assignment-induced multivariate-hypergeometric laws. Neither model class is a subclass of the other for purposes of these results, and no converse, invariance classification, or separator bound is transferred between them. \cite{RigdonHudgens2015} and \cite{LiDing2016} obtain exact binary average-effect confidence sets through compatible-table reasoning rather than a common group, while \cite{AronowChangLopatto2025} gives a balanced computational acceleration for that inversion problem. \cite{ChungRomano2013} studies unequal group proportions through asymptotic multinomial--hypergeometric coupling; the unequal-allocation result here is an exact fixed-schedule coefficient classification. \cite{ZhaoDing2021} supplies the robustly studentized weak-null workflow motivating the applied audit, but its weak-null guarantee is asymptotic rather than the common-invariance and sparse-separator statements studied here.

\section{Assumptions}

\begin{assumption}[ass:table-mass]\label{ass:table-mass}
\(\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal A_K}R_{ab}=n\)
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (finite response-table normalization, \cite{RigdonHudgens2015}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:weak-null]\label{ass:weak-null}
\(\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal A_K}(a-b)R_{ab}=0\)
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (Neyman weak null of zero finite-population average effect, \cite{WuDing2021}).
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[ass:balanced-complete-randomization]\label{ass:balanced-complete-randomization}
\(\Pr(Z=z)=\binom{2m}{m}^{-1}\mathbf 1\{z\in\mathcal Z_m\}\)
\par\smallskip\noindent\emph{Standard} (balanced complete randomization, \cite{Fisher1935}).
\end{assumption}

\section{Main results}

\begin{proposition}[prop:diagonal-reduction]\label{prop:diagonal-reduction}
For every \(C=(h,g)\in\mathcal X_{m,K}\) with \(r=h+g\), the diagonal response table \(D_r\) is legal and \(W_{D_r}(C)=Q_r(h)=\prod_{a\in\mathcal A_K}\binom{r_a}{h_a}\). If a profile has pooled histogram different from \(r\), its count under \(D_r\) is zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let \(C=(h,g)\in\mathcal X_{m,K}\) and put \(r=h+g\). By the two arm-size equalities in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}profile\text{-}space}\),
\[
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_K}r_a
=\sum_a h_a+\sum_a g_a=2m=n.
\]
Thus \(D_r\) has mass \(n\), as required by Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}table\text{-}mass}\). Every occupied cell of \(D_r\) is diagonal, so
\[
\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal A_K}(a-b)(D_r)_{ab}=0,
\]
which is Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\). Hence \(D_r\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\) by Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}legal\text{-}response\text{-}tables}\).

If \(T\in\mathcal T_{D_r}(C)\), all its off-diagonal entries vanish because \(0\leq T_{ab}\leq(D_r)_{ab}=0\) when \(a\neq b\). Its row constraints then force \(T_{aa}=h_a\), and its control-column constraints say \(r_a-T_{aa}=g_a\). The latter equalities hold because \(r=h+g\). Thus there is exactly one realizing treated-count table, and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\) gives
\[
W_{D_r}(C)
=\prod_{a\in\mathcal A_K}\binom{r_a}{h_a}
=Q_r(h),
\]
where the last equality is Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}diagonal\text{-}coefficient}\). Each factor is positive because \(0\leq h_a\leq r_a\), so this count is positive.

Finally, under a diagonal response table every unit displays the same alphabet value in either arm. Hence the pooled observed histogram is deterministically \(r\). Equivalently, summing the row and control-column constraints for any realizing table gives \(\widetilde h_a+\widetilde g_a=(D_r)_{aa}=r_a\) for every \(a\). Therefore a profile with pooled histogram different from \(r\) has no realizing table and has assignment count zero. Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}balanced\text{-}complete\text{-}randomization}\) and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}randomization\text{-}kernel}\) convert these counts to the stated multivariate-hypergeometric probabilities by the common positive divisor \(\binom{2m}{m}\).
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This is the sharp-null sanity reduction and the first coefficient probe: pooled histograms and their ordinary multivariate-hypergeometric weights are observable invariants. \textit{Gap.} The formula is standard hypergeometric bookkeeping, used here as a supporting reduction rather than claimed against \cite{RigdonHudgens2015} or \cite{LiDing2016}. \textit{Consumer.} It gives an exact fast pre-screen that discards profile comparisons already separated by a diagonal schedule.

\begin{lemma}[lem:transpose-reduction]\label{lem:transpose-reduction}
For every \(m\geq1\) and \(K\geq2\), if \(C\sim C'\), then \(C'=C\) or \(C'=C^\top\). More precisely, after Proposition 1 fixes \(r\), the legal symmetric switch \(D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{ab}+E_{ba}\) implies \((h_a-g_a)(h_b-g_b)=(h'_a-g'_a)(h'_b-g'_b)\) for every distinct supported \(a,b\), and hence the two arm-imbalance vectors agree up to a common sign.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose \(C=(h,g)\sim C'=(h',g')\), and let \(r=h+g\).  Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) shows that \(D_r\) is legal, that \(W_{D_r}(C)=Q_r(h)>0\), and that any profile with a different pooled histogram has count zero.  Equality of probabilities in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}probability\text{-}equivalence}\), together with the common positive divisor in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}randomization\text{-}kernel}\), therefore forces
\[
 h'+g'=r,
 \qquad
 Q_r(h')=Q_r(h)>0.                                      \tag{1}
\]

Fix distinct \(a,b\in\mathcal A_K\) with \(r_a,r_b>0\), and define
\[
 R^{ab}=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{ab}+E_{ba}.
\]
The diagonal filler is nonnegative, the table has mass \(2m\), and its two off-diagonal effects are \(a-b\) and \(b-a\).  Hence Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\) and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}legal\text{-}response\text{-}tables}\) imply \(R^{ab}\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\).

There are four treatment patterns for the two special units.  If both are treated, their pooled observed contribution is \(e_a+e_b\); the same is true if both are controlled.  Either mixed pattern instead contributes \(2e_a\) or \(2e_b\), and so cannot realize a profile whose pooled histogram is \(r\), because the diagonal filler has pooled histogram \(r-e_a-e_b\).  Expanding Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\), with the convention that an out-of-range binomial coefficient is zero, consequently gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
W_{R^{ab}}(C)
={}&\prod_{c\ne a,b}\binom{r_c}{h_c}
 \left\{
  \binom{r_a-1}{h_a-1}\binom{r_b-1}{h_b-1}
 +\binom{r_a-1}{h_a}\binom{r_b-1}{h_b}
 \right\}\\
={}&Q_r(h)\frac{h_a h_b+g_a g_b}{r_a r_b}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
All divisions are legitimate because \(r_a r_b>0\).  Applying the same calculation to \(C'\), and using (1) and probability equivalence under the legal table \(R^{ab}\), yields
\[
 h_a h_b+g_a g_b=h'_a h'_b+g'_a g'_b.                  \tag{3}
\]

Set \(d=h-g\) and \(d'=h'-g'\).  Since both profiles have pooled histogram \(r\),
\[
 h_a h_b+g_a g_b=\frac{r_a r_b+d_a d_b}{2},
\]
and similarly for the primed profile.  Thus (3) gives
\[
 d_a d_b=d'_a d'_b                                      \tag{4}
\]
for every pair of distinct supported coordinates.  Coordinates outside the support of \(r\) vanish in both vectors.  The equal arm sizes in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}profile\text{-}space}\) give \(\sum_a d_a=\sum_a d'_a=0\); hence, for every \(a\),
\[
 d_a^2=-\sum_{b\ne a}d_a d_b
      =-\sum_{b\ne a}d'_a d'_b=(d'_a)^2.               \tag{5}
\]
If \(d=0\), (5) gives \(d'=0\).  Otherwise choose \(a\) with \(d_a\ne0\).  Equation (5) gives \(d'_a=\varepsilon d_a\) for one \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\), and (4), divided by the nonzero number \(d'_a=\varepsilon d_a\), gives \(d'_b=\varepsilon d_b\) for every \(b\).  Therefore \(d'=d\) or \(d'=-d\).  Since
\[
 h=\frac{r+d}{2},\quad g=\frac{r-d}{2},\quad
 h'=\frac{r+d'}{2},\quad g'=\frac{r-d'}{2},
\]
we obtain \(C'=C\) or \(C'=(g,h)=C^\top\), as asserted.  Notice also that the proof is constructive: failure of a diagonal check is separated by \(D_r\), while, after the diagonal checks pass, failure of (4) is separated by the corresponding two-unit switch \(R^{ab}\).
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} The switch identity turns an exponential family of nuisance schedules into an arm-imbalance outer product, reducing the classification to the survival of transposition. \textit{Gap.} Generic orbit theory in \cite{HemerikGoeman2018} starts from invariance; it does not provide the causal zero-sum switches that force this two-point reduction. \textit{Consumer.} It converts any future implementation of equivalence checking from comparison over all profiles to tests of a profile and its arm transpose.

\begin{lemma}[lem:endpoint-saturation]\label{lem:endpoint-saturation}
For every \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\), \(W_R(C_+)=W_R(C_-)=1\) if \(R=mE_{LL}+mE_{00}\), and \(W_R(C_+)=W_R(C_-)=0\) otherwise.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a labeled schedule having response table \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\).  Suppose an assignment realizes \(C_+\).  If \(S\) is its set of \(m\) treated units, the observed profile says \(a_u=L\) for \(u\in S\) and \(b_u=0\) for \(u\notin S\).  The weak-null equality in Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\) is equality of the two total potential-outcome sums, so
\[
 mL
 \leq mL+\sum_{u\notin S}a_u
 =\sum_{u\in I}a_u
 =\sum_{u\in I}b_u
 =\sum_{u\in S}b_u
 \leq mL.                                                \tag{1}
\]
The endpoint bounds \(0\leq a_u,b_u\leq L\), which follow from the definition of \(\mathcal A_K\), make both inequalities in (1) valid.  Equality throughout forces \(a_u=0\) for every \(u\notin S\) and \(b_u=L\) for every \(u\in S\).  Thus every treated unit has type \((L,L)\), every control unit has type \((0,0)\), and necessarily
\[
 R=mE_{LL}+mE_{00}.                                     \tag{2}
\]
Conversely, for the table in (2), precisely one aggregate assignment chooses all \(m\) units of type \((L,L)\) for treatment, and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\) gives \(W_R(C_+)=\binom m m\binom m0=1\).

The same argument with the arms and potential-outcome coordinates interchanged proves that an assignment realizing \(C_-\) is possible only for the table in (2), and there it is the unique assignment treating all \((0,0)\) units.  Therefore \(W_R(C_-)=1\) for (2) and is zero otherwise.  This establishes the two asserted equalities.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} Endpoint saturation is the only universal nonbinary tie and supplies the exact probability bound behind the non-orbit corner test. \textit{Gap.} The compatible-table literature in \cite{RigdonHudgens2015} enumerates nuisance schedules for inference but does not isolate this schedule-universal endpoint identity. \textit{Consumer.} It yields an analytic weak-null p-value with granularity \(\binom{2m}{m}^{-1}\), avoiding nuisance enumeration for this rare event.

\begin{theorem}[thm:alphabet-frontier]\label{thm:alphabet-frontier}
For every \(m\geq1\): if \(K=2\), then \(C\sim C'\) if and only if \(C'=C\) or \(C'=C^\top\); if \(K\geq3\), then \(C\sim C'\) if and only if \(C'=C\) or \(\{C,C'\}=\{C_+,C_-\}\). Whenever \(C\not\sim C'\), one has \(d(C,C')\leq3\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Because \(\binom{2m}{m}>0\), Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}randomization\text{-}kernel}\) shows that equality or inequality of two probabilities under a fixed legal table is equivalent to equality or inequality of their assignment counts. Reflexivity of \(\sim\) follows directly from Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}probability\text{-}equivalence}\).

We first treat \(K=2\), whose levels are \(0\) and \(1\). Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\) reads
\[
 R_{10}-R_{01}=0.
 \tag{1}
\]
Consequently every legal binary response table satisfies \(R=R^{\mathsf T}\). For any response table, not necessarily binary, and any \(T\in\mathcal T_R(h,g)\), define
\[
 T^\dagger_{ba}=R_{ab}-T_{ab}.
 \tag{2}
\]
The row sums of \(T^\dagger\) equal \(g\), and the column sums of \(R^{\mathsf T}-T^\dagger\) equal \(h\). Thus (2) is a bijection from \(\mathcal T_R(h,g)\) onto \(\mathcal T_{R^{\mathsf T}}(g,h)\), its inverse being given by the same formula. Binomial symmetry gives, term by term,
\[
 \prod_{a,b}\binom{R_{ab}}{T_{ab}}
 =\prod_{a,b}\binom{R_{ab}}{R_{ab}-T_{ab}}
 =\prod_{a,b}\binom{R^{\mathsf T}_{ba}}{T^\dagger_{ba}}.
\]
Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\), (1), and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}randomization\text{-}kernel}\) therefore imply
\[
 P_R(h,g)=P_R(g,h)
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\mathcal R_0(n,2).
 \tag{3}
\]
Hence every binary transpose pair is equivalent. Conversely, Lemma \(\mathrm{lem{:}transpose\text{-}reduction}\) says that no equivalence class contains any other profile.

We record a sharper binary separation fact needed below. Parameterize a profile with pooled number of ones \(s\) by
\[
 h_1=i,\qquad g_1=s-i,
\]
where \(\max\{0,s-m\}\leq i\leq\min\{m,s\}\). Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) gives the positive diagonal count
\[
 A_i=\binom{s}{i}\binom{2m-s}{m-i}.
 \tag{4}
\]
For consecutive feasible indices,
\[
 \frac{A_{i+1}}{A_i}
 =\frac{(s-i)(m-i)}{(i+1)(m-s+i+1)},
 \tag{5}
\]
and both denominator factors are positive. Direct expansion yields
\[
 (s-i)(m-i)-(i+1)(m-s+i+1)
 =(m+1)(s-2i-1).
 \tag{6}
\]
Thus \(A_i\) strictly increases while \(i<(s-1)/2\), strictly decreases while \(i>(s-1)/2\), and the only adjacent equality is the central pair \(i,i+1\) with \(s=2i+1\). Moreover binomial symmetry gives \(A_i=A_{s-i}\). It follows by reflecting any index to the half-interval on which the sequence is strict that
\[
 A_i=A_{i'}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i'=i\ \text{or}\ i'=s-i.
 \tag{7}
\]
The second alternative is exactly arm transposition. Hence a diagonal table separates every inequivalent binary pair: differing pooled histograms are separated by \(D_{h+g}\), and under a common pooled histogram (7) separates every pair that is neither equal nor transposed.

Now let \(K\geq3\). Lemma \(\mathrm{lem{:}endpoint\text{-}saturation}\), followed by division by \(\binom{2m}{m}\), proves \(C_+\sim C_-\). Lemma \(\mathrm{lem{:}transpose\text{-}reduction}\) restricts every possible non-singleton equivalence class to a transpose pair. We prove constructively that every nontrivial transpose pair other than \(\{C_+,C_-\}\) is separated.

Fix \(C=(h,g)\neq C^{\mathsf T}\), put \(r=h+g\), and let
\[
 S=\{a\in\mathcal A_K:r_a>0\}.
\]
Suppose first that \(S\) contains an interior level. Join every pair of distinct vertices in \(S\), except possibly \(\{0,L\}\). This graph is connected because an interior vertex is adjacent to every other vertex. If
\[
 h_ag_b-g_ah_b=0
 \tag{8}
\]
on every edge, then \(g=r-h\) turns (8) into \(h_ar_b=r_ah_b\). Therefore \(h_a/r_a\) is constant on \(S\). The profile constraints give \(\sum_a h_a=m\) and \(\sum_a r_a=2m\), so this constant is \(1/2\), implying \(h=g\), a contradiction. Hence there are \(a<b\) in \(S\), with \(\{a,b\}\neq\{0,L\}\), for which (8) is nonzero.

Choose \(u,v\in\mathcal A_K\) by
\[
 (u,v)=
 \begin{cases}
 (b-a,0),&a>0,\\
 (b+1,1),&a=0.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{9}
\]
In the second case \(b<L\), so the displayed levels lie in \(\mathcal A_K\). In either case \(a+u=v+b\), and both response types below are heterogeneous. Define
\[
 R_2=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{av}+E_{ub}.
 \tag{10}
\]
The support conditions make the diagonal filler nonnegative; the mass is \(2m\); and the two individual effects sum to \((a-v)+(u-b)=0\). Thus Assumptions \(\mathrm{ass{:}table\text{-}mass}\) and \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\), through Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}legal\text{-}response\text{-}tables}\), make \(R_2\) legal, while Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}heterogeneous\text{-}count}\) gives \(q(R_2)=2\).

The four treatment-status patterns of the two special units have pooled observed histograms
\[
 e_a+e_u,\qquad e_a+e_b,\qquad e_v+e_u,\qquad e_v+e_b,
\]
respectively. Direct substitution from (9) shows that only the second equals \(e_a+e_b\). Thus only treating the \((a,v)\) unit and controlling the \((u,b)\) unit can realize pooled histogram \(r\). Using
\[
 \binom{x-1}{y-1}=\frac yx\binom xy,
 \qquad
 \binom{x-1}{y}=\frac{x-y}{x}\binom xy,
\]
with the positive denominators \(r_a,r_b\), Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\) gives
\[
 W_{R_2}(C)=Q_r(h)\frac{h_ag_b}{r_ar_b},
 \qquad
 W_{R_2}(C^{\mathsf T})=Q_r(h)\frac{g_ah_b}{r_ar_b}.
 \tag{11}
\]
Here \(Q_r(g)=Q_r(h)>0\) by coordinatewise binomial symmetry. Equations (8) and (11) prove separation.

It remains to consider a nontrivial transpose pair supported on \(\{0,L\}\). Write
\[
 h_0=m-i,\quad h_L=i,\qquad
 g_0=m-j,\quad g_L=j,
 \qquad i\neq j.
 \tag{12}
\]
Both pooled endpoint counts are positive, because single-level support would force \(h=g\). Since \(L\geq2\), the table
\[
 R'_2=D_{r-e_0-e_L}+E_{L,1}+E_{0,L-1}
 \tag{13}
\]
has a nonnegative filler, mass \(2m\), two heterogeneous units, and total effect \((L-1)-(L-1)=0\). Hence it is legal. An endpoint-only profile is possible only if both special units are treated, because both of their control outcomes are interior, also when \(L=2\). The same binomial cancellation as in (11) yields
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 W_{R'_2}(C)-W_{R'_2}(C^{\mathsf T})
 &=\frac{Q_r(h)}{r_0r_L}\{h_0h_L-g_0g_L\}\\
 &=\frac{Q_r(h)}{r_0r_L}(i-j)(m-i-j).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{14}
\]
Thus \(R'_2\) separates unless \(i+j=m\).

Suppose \(i+j=m\). Then \(r_0=r_L=m\), \(h_0=j\), and \(g_0=i\). If \(i=0\) or \(j=0\), the unordered profile pair is \(\{C_+,C_-\}\). Otherwise \(i,j>0\); since \(i\neq j\), necessarily \(m\geq3\). Define
\[
 R_3=D_{r-2e_0-e_L}+E_{L,0}+E_{0,1}+E_{0,L-1}.
 \tag{15}
\]
Its diagonal filler is nonnegative, its mass is \(2m\), and its three effects sum to \(L-1-(L-1)=0\), so it is legal and \(q(R_3)=3\). To yield an endpoint-only profile, the last two special units must be treated because their control outcomes are interior. Conditional on that, the first special unit must also be treated: otherwise the special pooled histogram is \(3e_0\), whereas the filler in (15) requires \(2e_0+e_L\). Consequently exactly one special-unit status pattern contributes, including when \(L=2\), when the last two response types coincide. Since \(m\geq3\), all denominators below are positive, and direct coefficient cancellation gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 W_{R_3}(C)
 &=Q_r(h)\frac{h_0(h_0-1)h_L}{m^2(m-1)},\\
 W_{R_3}(C^{\mathsf T})
 &=Q_r(h)\frac{g_0(g_0-1)g_L}{m^2(m-1)},\\
 W_{R_3}(C)-W_{R_3}(C^{\mathsf T})
 &=Q_r(h)\frac{ij(j-i)}{m^2(m-1)}\neq0.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{16}
\]
The last inequality uses \(i,j>0\) and \(i\neq j\). This completes the classification for \(K\geq3\).

It remains to verify the advertised separator bound for arbitrary inequivalent profiles, rather than only transposes. Let \(C=(h,g)\) and \(C'=(h',g')\). If their pooled histograms differ, Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) makes \(D_{h+g}\) a separator with \(q=0\). If their pooled histogram is the same vector \(r\), but \(Q_r(h)\neq Q_r(h')\), the same table separates with \(q=0\). Suppose henceforth that both \(r\) and \(Q_r\) agree. Put \(x=h-g\) and \(x'=h'-g'\). For distinct supported \(a,b\), the legal symmetric switch
\[
 S_{ab}=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{ab}+E_{ba}
\]
has two heterogeneous units, and its two pooled-compatible special allocations give
\[
 W_{S_{ab}}(C)
 =Q_r(h)\frac{h_ah_b+g_ag_b}{r_ar_b}
 =\frac{Q_r(h)}{2r_ar_b}\{r_ar_b+x_ax_b\}.
 \tag{17}
\]
The same identity holds for \(C'\). If no such switch separates, then \(x_ax_b=x'_ax'_b\) for all distinct coordinates, including trivially those outside the support. Both vectors have coordinate sum zero. If \(x=0\), the product identities imply that \(x'\) has at most one nonzero coordinate, and its zero sum then gives \(x'=0\). If \(x\neq0\), choose two nonzero coordinates \(p,q\), which exist because \(\sum_a x_a=0\). The product identities show that \(x'_p,x'_q\neq0\) and that \(x\) and \(x'\) have the same support. If the support has at least three elements, the ratios \(\lambda_a=x'_a/x_a\) obey \(\lambda_p\lambda_q=\lambda_p\lambda_a=\lambda_q\lambda_a=1\), so every ratio is the same element of \(\{-1,1\}\). If the support is exactly \(\{p,q\}\), the two zero-sum identities and \(x_px_q=x'_px'_q\) give the same conclusion. Thus \(x'=x\) or \(x'=-x\). Since
\[
 h=\frac{r+x}{2},\qquad g=\frac{r-x}{2},
\]
this says \(C'=C\) or \(C'=C^{\mathsf T}\). Therefore every pair that is neither equal nor transposed is separated by a switch with \(q=2\). The remaining inequivalent transposes are separated by (10), (13), or (15), with \(q=2,2,3\), respectively; in the binary case (4)--(7) already give \(q=0\). Since probability inequalities are equivalent to count inequalities, Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}separator\text{-}size}\) now yields \(d(C,C')\leq3\) for every inequivalent pair.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This theorem supplies the balanced side of the allocation-balance elbow and an explicit legal separator with at most three heterogeneous units for every inequivalent profile pair. \textit{Gap.} The i.i.d. finite-support product-law criterion of \cite{DutzZhang2026} and the asymptotic fixed-population result of \cite{WuDing2021} do not classify this all-schedule assignment-induced hypergeometric kernel. \textit{Consumer.} DeclareDesign's balanced \(250\)-of-\(500\) binary reply audit can use the exact transpose classification to distinguish a justified histogram symmetry from a sharp-null imputation or an asymptotic HC2 argument.

\begin{theorem}[thm:sharp-separator]\label{thm:sharp-separator}
Let \(K\geq3\), \(m\geq3\), \(1\leq i\leq m-1\), and \(2i\neq m\). For \(C=((m-i)e_0+ie_L,\ ie_0+(m-i)e_L)\) and \(C'=C^\top\), one has \(d(C,C')=3\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put \(j=m-i\). The endpoint coordinates of the profiles in the statement are
\[
 h_0=j,\quad h_L=i,\qquad g_0=i,\quad g_L=j.
 \tag{1}
\]
The inequalities \(1\leq i\leq m-1\) give \(i,j>0\), so the pair is not \(\{C_+,C_-\}\); the condition \(2i\neq m\) gives \(i\neq j\), so the profiles are distinct. Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) therefore proves that they are inequivalent and that \(d(C,C')\leq3\). We prove the matching lower bound.

Fix \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\) with \(q(R)\leq2\). If \(q(R)=0\), every response type is diagonal. Thus \(R=D_s\) for its diagonal histogram \(s\). Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) gives zero count for both profiles unless \(s=me_0+me_L\); for that table it gives
\[
 W_R(C)=\binom mj\binom mi
 =\binom mi\binom mj=W_R(C').
 \tag{2}
\]
The case \(q(R)=1\) is impossible: every diagonal unit contributes zero to the weak-null sum, while the unique heterogeneous unit contributes the nonzero integer \(a-b\), contradicting Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\).

It remains to consider \(q(R)=2\). Label the two heterogeneous units by their response types \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\), allowing the two labeled units to occupy the same response-table cell. The weak-null condition is
\[
 a+c=b+d.
 \tag{3}
\]
If a diagonal filler unit has an interior outcome, both endpoint-only profiles have count zero, because that outcome is observed under either assignment. We may therefore assume that all diagonal filler units are at \(0\) or \(L\). We partition the assignments by the four treatment-status patterns of the two heterogeneous units.

If both special units are treated and a contribution is nonzero, then \(a,c\in\{0,L\}\). They cannot both be zero, because (3) and nonnegativity would force \(b=d=0\); they cannot both be \(L\), because (3) and the bounds \(b,d\leq L\) would force \(b=d=L\). Either conclusion would contradict heterogeneity. Hence \(\{a,c\}=\{0,L\}\), and realizing the common pooled histogram forces the diagonal filler to contain \(m-1\) units at each endpoint. The filler-selection coefficients for \(C\) and \(C'\) are
\[
 \binom{m-1}{j-1}\binom{m-1}{i-1}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \binom{m-1}{i-1}\binom{m-1}{j-1},
 \tag{4}
\]
so they agree. If both special units are controlled, the same argument applied to \(b,d\) gives the equal coefficients
\[
 \binom{m-1}{j}\binom{m-1}{i}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \binom{m-1}{i}\binom{m-1}{j}.
 \tag{5}
\]

Suppose next that the first special unit is treated and the second is controlled. If either observed outcome \(a,d\) is interior, both contributions vanish. If \(a,d\in\{0,L\}\) are different, (3) forces both types to be diagonal. Indeed, \((a,d)=(0,L)\) gives \(c-b=L\), hence \((c,b)=(L,0)\); the reversed case is symmetric. This contradicts \(q(R)=2\). Thus a nonzero contribution requires \(a=d=v\) for some \(v\in\{0,L\}\). Equation (3) then gives \(b=c=u\), so the response types are \((v,u)\) and \((u,v)\), with \(u\neq v\). One observation of \(v\) lies in each arm. Consequently the diagonal filler contains \(m-2\) units at \(v\) and \(m\) at the opposite endpoint \(\bar v=L-v\). The two coefficients are
\[
 \binom{m-2}{h_v-1}\binom m{h_{\bar v}}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \binom{m-2}{g_v-1}\binom m{g_{\bar v}}.
 \tag{6}
\]
They agree by binomial symmetry, because (1) gives
\[
 (h_v-1)+(g_v-1)=m-2,
 \qquad h_{\bar v}+g_{\bar v}=m.
\]
The other mixed status is identical after exchanging the two labels.

The four cases exhaust the labeled assignments of the heterogeneous units. When the response types coincide, treating the units as labeled merely separates the binomial multiplicity into its individual subsets, so their sum is still exactly Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\). Equations (4)--(6) therefore imply
\[
 W_R(C)=W_R(C')
 \qquad\text{for every legal }R\text{ with }q(R)\leq2.
 \tag{7}
\]
The positive randomization normalizer transfers (7) to \(P_R(C)=P_R(C')\). Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}separator\text{-}size}\) yields \(d(C,C')\geq3\), and the upper bound proves equality.

For the smallest cubic stress test, take \(K=3\), \(m=3\), \(L=2\), and \(i=1\). The table from (15) in the proof of Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) becomes
\[
 R=E_{20}+2E_{01}+E_{00}+2E_{22}.
\]
It has mass six and weak-null sum \(2-2=0\). Formula (16) gives count one for \(C=((2,0,1),(1,0,2))\) and count zero for \(C'=C^{\mathsf T}\). The exhaustive four-status calculation above simultaneously covers every legal table with at most two heterogeneous units and proves that none separates this pair; a literal enumeration of the \(103\) such nonnegative \(3\times3\) tables gives the same zero-discrepancy check, but is not used as a premise.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This theorem certifies that three heterogeneous units are genuinely necessary in the worst nonbinary balanced case, rather than an artifact of the cubic construction. \textit{Gap.} Neither exact binary test inversion in \cite{RigdonHudgens2015} nor generic group testing in \cite{HemerikGoeman2018} studies the optimal sparse legal-schedule certificate number. \textit{Consumer.} It fixes the smallest worst-case adversarial schedule size that a balanced nonbinary symmetry classifier must include in a software audit.

\begin{theorem}[thm:maximal-common-group]\label{thm:maximal-common-group}
The group \(G_\star\) is the direct product of the symmetric groups on the \(\sim\)-classes. Consequently, for \(K=2\), \(G_\star\cong(S_2)^{\binom{m+1}{2}}\); for \(K\geq3\), \(G_\star\cong S_2\), acting only on \(\{C_+,C_-\}\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a bijection \(f\) of \(\mathcal X_{m,K}\), Definitions \(\mathrm{def{:}maximal\text{-}common\text{-}group}\) and \(\mathrm{def{:}probability\text{-}equivalence}\) give
\[
 f\in G_\star
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 f(C)\sim C\ \text{for every }C\in\mathcal X_{m,K}.
 \tag{1}
\]
Thus every such \(f\) maps each equivalence class into itself. Since the profile space is finite and \(f\) is bijective, its restriction to each class is a permutation. Conversely, any independently chosen permutation within each equivalence class preserves every \(P_R\) by Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}probability\text{-}equivalence}\), and hence belongs to \(G_\star\). Composition and inversion act classwise. Therefore
\[
 G_\star\cong\prod_{A\in\mathcal X_{m,K}/\sim}S_A.
 \tag{2}
\]

When \(K=2\), each arm histogram is determined by its number of ones, so \(|\mathcal X_{m,2}|=(m+1)^2\). Exactly \(m+1\) profiles have identical treated and control histograms and hence are fixed by transposition. Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) says all other equivalence classes are two-point transpose classes. Their number is
\[
 \frac{(m+1)^2-(m+1)}2
 =\frac{m(m+1)}2
 =\binom{m+1}{2}.
 \tag{3}
\]
The singleton factors in (2) are trivial, so (3) yields
\[
 G_\star\cong(S_2)^{\binom{m+1}{2}}.
\]
When \(K\geq3\), Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) gives exactly one two-point class, \(\{C_+,C_-\}\), and singleton classes otherwise. Equation (2) then gives \(G_\star\cong S_2\), whose only nonidentity action exchanges those two endpoint corners.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This translates the balanced probability partition into the largest schedule-universal transformation group; paired with the unequal frontier, it isolates identity as the exact off-balance group. \textit{Gap.} \cite{HemerikGoeman2018} proves validity for a specified invariant group, whereas the new step is computing the maximal group forced by the composite causal null and showing its discontinuous change with allocation balance. \textit{Consumer.} It supplies an allocation-specific validation target for software advertised as a weak-null common-invariance randomization test.

\begin{theorem}[thm:orbit-rank-granularity]\label{thm:orbit-rank-granularity}
For every fixed \(G\leq G_\star\), every fixed statistic \(T:\mathcal X_{m,K}\to\mathbb R\), and every \(C\in\mathcal X_{m,K}\), the conventional nonrandomized group-rank p-value satisfies \(p_G(C)\in\{1/2,1\}\) and \(\Pr_R\{p_G(C)\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha\) for every \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix \(G\leq G_\star\), a statistic \(T\), and a profile \(C\). Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}maximal\text{-}common\text{-}group}\) shows that every \(G\)-orbit has one or two points. If the orbit of \(C\) is a singleton, every \(f\in G\) fixes \(C\), and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}orbit\text{-}rank\text{-}pvalue}\) gives \(p_G(C)=1\).

Suppose the orbit is \(\{C,C^*\}\). Choose \(f_*\in G\) with \(f_*(C)=C^*\). Left multiplication by \(f_*\) is a bijection between the group elements that fix \(C\) and those that send \(C\) to \(C^*\). Thus exactly \(|G|/2\) elements have each image. Since \(T(C)\geq T(C)\), Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}orbit\text{-}rank\text{-}pvalue}\) yields
\[
 p_G(C)=
 \begin{cases}
 1/2,&T(C^*)<T(C),\\
 1,&T(C^*)\geq T(C).
 \end{cases}
 \tag{1}
\]
Hence \(p_G(C)\in\{1/2,1\}\) in every case, including the trivial subgroup.

Now fix \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\). By Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}maximal\text{-}common\text{-}group}\),
\[
 P_R(f(C))=P_R(C)\qquad(f\in G).
 \tag{2}
\]
Therefore, conditional on any positive-probability \(G\)-orbit, the observed profile is uniform on that orbit. If \(0\leq\alpha<1/2\), (1) makes the rejection event empty. If \(1/2\leq\alpha<1\), a singleton orbit contributes no rejection. On a two-point orbit, at most the point with strictly larger \(T\)-value rejects, because two real numbers cannot each be strictly larger than the other; by (2), that point has conditional probability \(1/2\). Summing over the disjoint positive-probability orbits gives
\[
 \Pr_R\{p_G(C)\leq\alpha\}\leq\frac12\leq\alpha.
 \tag{3}
\]
For \(\alpha=1\), the event is the whole profile space and has probability one. These cases prove finite-sample validity for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}balanced\text{-}complete\text{-}randomization}\) and Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}randomization\text{-}kernel}\) identify \(P_R\) in (2) with the actual assignment-induced profile law, so (3) is the asserted design-based probability statement.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This theorem gives the balanced inferential consequence of maximal common symmetry; Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}unbalanced\text{-}orbit\text{-}rank\text{-}degeneracy}\) supplies the unequal-arm corollary \(p_G\equiv1\). \textit{Gap.} The rank argument is elementary once the group is known; the substantive new input is the exact orbit-size elbow derived from the fixed-schedule coefficient kernel. \textit{Consumer.} For the binary reply audit, the result explains why conventional common-orbit ranks cannot reproduce a \(5\%\) HC2 or robust-\(t\) workflow, while making no claim against nuisance-aware or otherwise non-orbit exact tests.

\begin{proposition}[prop:nonorbit-exact-pvalue]\label{prop:nonorbit-exact-pvalue}
For every \(K\geq2\), the function \(p_{\mathrm{corner}}\) satisfies \(\Pr_R\{p_{\mathrm{corner}}(C)\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha\) for every \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\) and every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). Its nontrivial value is \(\binom{2m}{m}^{-1}\), which can be strictly below \(1/2\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let \(B=\binom{2m}{m}\).  Since \(m\geq 1\), \(B\) is a positive integer.  Fix \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\).  By the balanced complete-randomization assumption and the definition of the exact randomization kernel, every balanced assignment has probability \(B^{-1}\), and hence
\[
 \Pr_R\{C=C_+\}=P_R(C_+)=B^{-1}W_R(C_+).
\]
The endpoint-saturation lemma gives \(W_R(C_+)\in\{0,1\}\), so
\[
 \Pr_R\{C=C_+\}\leq B^{-1}. \tag{1}
\]
By the definition of \(p_{\mathrm{corner}}\),
\[
 p_{\mathrm{corner}}(C)=
 \begin{cases}
 B^{-1},&C=C_+,\\
 1,&C\neq C_+.
 \end{cases} \tag{2}
\]
Let \(\alpha\in[0,1]\).  If \(0\leq\alpha<B^{-1}\), equation (2) makes the rejection event empty, and therefore its probability is zero.  If \(B^{-1}\leq\alpha<1\), equation (2) gives
\[
 \{p_{\mathrm{corner}}(C)\leq\alpha\}=\{C=C_+\};
\]
consequently (1) yields
\[
 \Pr_R\{p_{\mathrm{corner}}(C)\leq\alpha\}
 \leq B^{-1}\leq\alpha.
\]
Finally, if \(\alpha=1\), the rejection event is the entire profile space and has probability \(1=\alpha\).  These cases exhaust \([0,1]\), proving exact validity for every legal response table.

Equation (2) also shows that the unique value of \(p_{\mathrm{corner}}\) below one is \(B^{-1}=\binom{2m}{m}^{-1}\).  For \(m=1\) it equals \(1/2\).  For every \(m\geq2\),
\[
 B=\binom{2m}{m}\geq\binom{4}{2}=6,
\]
where the inequality follows because \(\binom{2(m+1)}{m+1}/\binom{2m}{m}=2(2m+1)/(m+1)>1\).  Thus \(B^{-1}<1/2\) whenever \(m\geq2\), establishing the asserted possibility without imposing any restriction on other exact tests.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This analytic counterpoint prevents the group classification from being misread as nonexistence of all other exact weak-null tests. \textit{Gap.} Exact binary procedures in \cite{RigdonHudgens2015} already show that nuisance-aware tests can exist; this proposition isolates a particularly transparent non-orbit rule inside the present model. \textit{Consumer.} It tells DeclareDesign developers that an exact weak-null module may use analytic calibration or nuisance maximization even though common-orbit ranks are coarse.

\begin{theorem}[thm:unbalanced-frontier]\label{thm:unbalanced-frontier}
Let \(n=2m\), \(K\geq 2\), \(1\leq n_1,n_0\leq n-1\), \(n_1+n_0=n\), and \(n_1\neq n_0\). Under complete randomization, the treated set is uniform over the \(\binom n{n_1}\) subsets of size \(n_1\), while the potential-outcome response table \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\), and hence the causal weak-null condition \(\tau_R=0\), is fixed. Complete the rectangular coefficient handle \(\mathsf H_{\mathrm{unbal}}\) by putting
\[
 \mathcal X_{n_1,n_0,K}
 =\left\{(h,g)\in\mathbb Z_+^K\times\mathbb Z_+^K:
       \sum_a h_a=n_1,\ \sum_a g_a=n_0\right\},
\]
\[
 \mathcal T_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)
 =\left\{T\in\mathbb Z_+^{K\times K}:0\leq T_{ab}\leq R_{ab},\quad
       \sum_bT_{ab}=h_a,\quad \sum_a(R_{ab}-T_{ab})=g_b\right\},
\]
and
\[
 W_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)
 =\sum_{T\in\mathcal T_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)}
       \prod_{a,b\in\mathcal A_K}\binom{R_{ab}}{T_{ab}},
 \qquad
 P_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)=\binom n{n_1}^{-1}W_R^{(n_1)}(h,g).
\]
For \(C,C'\in\mathcal X_{n_1,n_0,K}\), define \(C\sim_{n_1,n_0}C'\) when \(P_R^{(n_1)}(C)=P_R^{(n_1)}(C')\) for every \(R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\). Then
\[
 C\sim_{n_1,n_0}C'\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C=C'.
\]
Consequently the maximal common bijection group
\[
 G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}
 =\left\{f\in\operatorname{Bij}(\mathcal X_{n_1,n_0,K}):
 P_R^{(n_1)}(f(C))=P_R^{(n_1)}(C)\ \text{for every }C,R\right\}
\]
is \(\{\operatorname{id}\}\). Moreover, if
\[
 s_\star(n_1,n_0,K)
 =\max_{C\neq C'}\ \min\left\{q(R):R\in\mathcal R_0(n,K),\quad
       P_R^{(n_1)}(C)\neq P_R^{(n_1)}(C')\right\},
\]
then
\[
 s_\star(n_1,n_0,K)=2.
\]
Thus two heterogeneous units always suffice, by an explicit legal response table, and this uniform bound cannot be reduced.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write \(C=(h,g)\) and \(C'=(h',g')\), and adopt the convention that \(\binom{x}{y}=0\) for integers \(x,y\) unless \(0\leq y\leq x\).  The causal quantity \(\tau_R\) is fixed, rather than random, because \(R\) is fixed; membership in \(\mathcal R_0(n,K)\), by Definition~\(\mathrm{def:legal\mbox{-}response\mbox{-}tables}\) and Assumption~\(\mathrm{ass:weak\mbox{-}null}\), gives \(\tau_R=0\).  The kernel \(P_R^{(n_1)}\) below is instead an observed-profile probability induced solely by complete randomization.  We first verify that kernel and then characterize its common level sets.

For fixed \(R\), a treated-count matrix \(T\) records that exactly \(T_{ab}\) of the \(R_{ab}\) labeled units of type \((a,b)\) are treated.  There are
\[
 \prod_{a,b\in\mathcal A_K}\binom{R_{ab}}{T_{ab}}
\]
assignments giving that matrix.  The row constraints on \(T\) are exactly the requirement that the treated histogram be \(h\), and the column constraints on \(R-T\) are exactly the requirement that the control histogram be \(g\).  Distinct matrices describe disjoint assignments.  Hence summing these products over \(\mathcal T_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)\) gives \(W_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)\).  Complete randomization is uniform on the \(\binom n{n_1}\) treated sets; this denominator is positive because \(1\leq n_1\leq n-1\).  Consequently
\[
 P_R^{(n_1)}(h,g)=\binom n{n_1}^{-1}W_R^{(n_1)}(h,g).
 \tag{1}
\]

Let \(r=h+g\).  Then \(r\in\mathbb Z_+^K\) and \(\sum_a r_a=n_1+n_0=n\).  The diagonal table \(D_r\) has mass \(n\) by Assumption~\(\mathrm{ass:table\mbox{-}mass}\), and all of its units have zero individual effect, so Assumption~\(\mathrm{ass:weak\mbox{-}null}\) gives \(D_r\in\mathcal R_0(n,K)\).  Directly from the treated-count constraints and Definition~\(\mathrm{def:assignment\mbox{-}count}\),
\[
 W_{D_r}^{(n_1)}(u,v)
 =\mathbf 1\{u+v=r\}\prod_{a\in\mathcal A_K}\binom{r_a}{u_a}.
 \tag{2}
\]
In particular,
\[
 W_{D_r}^{(n_1)}(h,g)=Q_r(h)>0,
 \tag{3}
\]
because \(0\leq h_a\leq r_a\) for every \(a\).  If \(C\sim_{n_1,n_0}C'\), equality of the probabilities at \(D_r\), together with (1)--(3), first forces
\[
 h'+g'=r
 \tag{4}
\]
and then forces
\[
 Q_r(h)=Q_r(h').
 \tag{5}
\]

We next show that legal response tables having two heterogeneous units recover the arm-imbalance outer product.  Fix distinct \(a,b\in\mathcal A_K\) with \(r_a,r_b>0\), and define
\[
 R^{ab}=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{ab}+E_{ba}.
 \tag{6}
\]
The diagonal filler in (6) is nonnegative.  The table has mass \(n\), and the effects of its two heterogeneous units sum to \((a-b)+(b-a)=0\).  Thus (6) belongs to \(\mathcal R_0(n,K)\), and Definition~\(\mathrm{def:heterogeneous\mbox{-}count}\) gives \(q(R^{ab})=2\).  The two special units contribute pooled observed histogram \(e_a+e_b\) if they are both treated or both controlled, \(2e_a\) if the \((a,b)\) unit alone is treated, and \(2e_b\) under the other mixed allocation.  Since the diagonal filler has pooled histogram \(r-e_a-e_b\), precisely the both-treated and both-controlled cases can realize a profile whose pooled histogram is \(r\).  Using
\[
 \binom{s-1}{t-1}=\frac{t}{s}\binom{s}{t},
 \qquad
 \binom{s-1}{t}=\frac{s-t}{s}\binom{s}{t}
 \tag{7}
\]
with the positive denominators \(s=r_a,r_b\), the two cases give
\[
 W_{R^{ab}}^{(n_1)}(h,g)
 =Q_r(h)\frac{h_ah_b+g_ag_b}{r_ar_b}.
 \tag{8}
\]
Define the integer imbalance vectors
\[
 x=h-g=2h-r,
 \qquad
 x'=h'-g'=2h'-r,
 \tag{9}
\]
where (4) is used for the second equality defining \(x'\).  Since
\[
 h_ah_b+g_ag_b=\frac{r_ar_b+x_ax_b}{2},
 \tag{10}
\]
equivalence, (1), (5), and (8)--(10) imply
\[
 x_ax_b=x'_ax'_b
 \quad\text{for distinct }a,b\text{ with }r_a,r_b>0.
 \tag{11}
\]

The diagonal entries of the same outer product are obtained by a second placement of the symmetric switch.  Fix \(a\) with \(r_a\geq2\), choose any \(b\neq a\), which is possible because \(K\geq2\), and put
\[
 \widetilde R^{a\mid b}=D_{r-2e_a}+E_{ab}+E_{ba}.
 \tag{12}
\]
As in (6), this is a nonnegative table of mass \(n\), its two heterogeneous effects cancel, and \(q(\widetilde R^{a\mid b})=2\).  Exactly one treatment pattern of the two special units supplies the missing pooled histogram \(2e_a\): the \((a,b)\) unit is treated and the \((b,a)\) unit is controlled.  Therefore the coefficient formula gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 W_{\widetilde R^{a\mid b}}^{(n_1)}(h,g)
 &=\prod_c\binom{r_c-2\mathbf 1\{c=a\}}
                         {h_c-\mathbf 1\{c=a\}}\\
 &=Q_r(h)\frac{h_ag_a}{r_a(r_a-1)}.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{13}
\]
The division in (13) is legitimate because \(r_a\geq2\); the equality also covers the boundary cases \(h_a=0\) and \(h_a=r_a\) under the binomial convention.  Equivalence, (1), (5), and (13) imply \(h_ag_a=h'_ag'_a\), and hence
\[
 x_a^2=r_a^2-4h_ag_a=r_a^2-4h'_ag'_a=(x'_a)^2.
 \tag{14}
\]
If \(r_a=0\), both imbalance coordinates are zero.  If \(r_a=1\), nonnegativity and integrality with \(h_a+g_a=h'_a+g'_a=1\) imply \(x_a^2=(x'_a)^2=1\).  Thus (14) holds for every \(a\).  Products involving a coordinate with \(r_a=0\) vanish, so (11) and (14) together yield
\[
 xx^{\mathsf T}=x'(x')^{\mathsf T}.
 \tag{15}
\]

The unequal allocation now eliminates the sign ambiguity in (15).  The profile constraints give
\[
 \sum_a x_a=\sum_a x'_a=n_1-n_0=:\delta,
 \qquad \delta\neq0.
 \tag{16}
\]
Thus \(x\neq0\).  Choose \(j\) with \(x_j\neq0\).  The \((j,j)\) entry of (15) gives \(x'_j=\varepsilon x_j\) for some \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\); the \((j,a)\) entries then give \(x'_a=\varepsilon x_a\) for every \(a\).  Summing and using (16) gives \(\delta=\varepsilon\delta\), so \(\varepsilon=1\).  Therefore \(x'=x\), and (4) and (9) give
\[
 h'=\frac{r+x'}2=\frac{r+x}2=h,
 \qquad
 g'=\frac{r-x'}2=\frac{r-x}2=g.
 \tag{17}
\]
This proves that every \(\sim_{n_1,n_0}\)-class is a singleton; the converse implication \(C=C'\Rightarrow C\sim_{n_1,n_0}C'\) is immediate from the definition.

The preceding argument is also a constructive separator algorithm.  For distinct \(C,C'\), first use \(D_{h+g}\).  Equation (2) separates them if their pooled histograms differ, and (2)--(3) separate them if the pooled histograms agree but their diagonal coefficients differ.  Suppose instead that their common pooled histogram is \(r\) and (5) holds.  If some product in (11) differs, the explicitly defined table (6) separates the profiles by (8).  If all such off-diagonal products agree but the profiles remain distinct, then some square in (14) must differ: otherwise (15)--(17), whose only additional premise is the common nonzero sum \(\delta\), would imply \(C=C'\).  A coordinate with unequal squares must satisfy \(r_a\geq2\), since the square is forced to be zero for \(r_a=0\) and one for \(r_a=1\); the table (12) then separates the profiles by (13).  Each table in this algorithm is legal and has respectively zero or two heterogeneous units.  It follows that
\[
 s_\star(n_1,n_0,K)\leq2.
 \tag{18}
\]

If \(f\in G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}\), its defining probability identity implies \(C\sim_{n_1,n_0}f(C)\) for every \(C\).  The singleton result forces \(f(C)=C\) for every profile, and hence
\[
 G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}=\{\operatorname{id}\}.
 \tag{19}
\]

It remains to prove that (18) is sharp.  The assumptions permit no unequal positive allocation when \(m=1\), so every admissible instance has \(m\geq2\).  Define
\[
 \ell=\max\{0,n_1-m\},
 \qquad
 u=\min\{m,n_1\}.
 \tag{20}
\]
If \(n_1<m\), then \((\ell,u)=(0,n_1)\); if \(n_1>m\), then \((\ell,u)=(n_1-m,m)\).  Using \(1\leq n_1\leq2m-1\) and \(n_1\neq m\), both cases give
\[
 0\leq\ell<u\leq m,
 \qquad \ell+u=n_1.
 \tag{21}
\]
Because \(K\geq2\), the levels \(0,1\) belong to \(\mathcal A_K\).  Define
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 h&=\ell e_0+u e_1,
 &g&=(m-\ell)e_0+(m-u)e_1,\\
 h'&=u e_0+\ell e_1,
 &g'&=(m-u)e_0+(m-\ell)e_1.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{22}
\]
Equations (21)--(22) show that the treated totals are \(n_1\), the control totals are \(2m-n_1=n_0\), all entries are nonnegative, and the two profiles are distinct.  Both have pooled histogram
\[
 r=me_0+me_1.
 \tag{23}
\]
If a legal table \(R\) has \(q(R)=0\), nonnegativity in Definition~\(\mathrm{def:heterogeneous\mbox{-}count}\) forces every off-diagonal entry to vanish, so \(R\) is diagonal.  By (2), both counts in (22) vanish unless \(R=D_r\); for that table,
\[
 W_{D_r}^{(n_1)}(h,g)
 =\binom m\ell\binom mu
 =\binom mu\binom m\ell
 =W_{D_r}^{(n_1)}(h',g').
 \tag{24}
\]
Thus no legal table with zero heterogeneous units separates this pair.  There is no legal table with exactly one heterogeneous unit: all diagonal units contribute zero to the weak-null sum, while the sole off-diagonal unit of type \((a,b)\) contributes \(a-b\neq0\), contradicting Assumption~\(\mathrm{ass:weak\mbox{-}null}\).  Therefore the pair (22) has separator number at least two.  Combining this lower bound with (18) proves
\[
 s_\star(n_1,n_0,K)=2.
 \tag{25}
\]

As an exact smallest-instance check, take \((n,K,n_1,n_0)=(4,2,1,3)\).  Then (22) gives \((h,g)=((0,1),(2,1))\) and \((h',g')=((1,0),(1,2))\).  Under \(D_{2e_0+2e_1}\), both assignment counts equal \(2\), and under every other diagonal table both vanish.  The legal table
\[
 2E_{11}+E_{01}+E_{10}
\]
has two heterogeneous units and, by direct substitution in (13) with \(a=0\) and \(b=1\), gives the respective counts \(0\) and \(1\).  This verifies at the boundary both the explicit upper construction and the obstruction to a separator with fewer than two heterogeneous units.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[prop:unbalanced-orbit-rank-degeneracy]\label{prop:unbalanced-orbit-rank-degeneracy}
Under the hypotheses and notation of Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}unbalanced\text{-}frontier}\), for every \(G\leq G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}\), every \(T:\mathcal X_{n_1,n_0,K}\to\mathbb R\), and every \(C\in\mathcal X_{n_1,n_0,K}\), the conventional nonrandomized orbit-rank value
\[
 |G|^{-1}\bigl|\{f\in G:T(f(C))\geq T(C)\}\bigr|
\]
is identically \(1\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}unbalanced\text{-}frontier}\) proves \(G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}=\{\operatorname{id}\}\). Therefore every subgroup \(G\leq G_\star^{(n_1,n_0)}\) is \(\{\operatorname{id}\}\), so \(|G|=1\), its orbit of every \(C\) is the singleton \(\{C\}\), and \(T(\operatorname{id}(C))=T(C)\). Substitution into the displayed rank formula gives \(1^{-1}|\{\operatorname{id}\}|=1\).
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This records the requested unequal-arm consequence of the identity maximal common group. \textit{Gap.} Without this corollary, the orbit-rank narrative would stop at the balanced \(\{1/2,1\}\) granularity despite the discharged unequal-arm classification. \textit{Consumer.} It tells design-audit software that changing only the arm allocation from equal to unequal removes even the nontrivial common orbit.

\begin{theorem}[thm:sparse-separator-frontier]\label{thm:sparse-separator-frontier}
For every \(m\geq1\) and \(K\geq2\), the balanced worst-case separator number satisfies
\[
s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,K)=
\begin{cases}
0,&K=2,\\
2,&K\geq3\ \text{and}\ m\in\{1,2\},\\
3,&K\geq3\ \text{and}\ m\geq3.
\end{cases}
\]
Moreover, whenever \(n=2m\), \(1\leq n_1,n_0\leq n-1\), \(n_1+n_0=n\), and \(n_1\neq n_0\), writing \(s_{\mathrm{unbal}}(n_1,n_0,K)\) for the unequal-allocation worst-case separator number defined in Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}unbalanced\text{-}frontier}\), one has
\[
s_{\mathrm{unbal}}(n_1,n_0,K)=2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The maximization in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}balanced\text{-}worst\text{-}separator}\) is over a nonempty finite set. Indeed, the two profiles \((me_0,me_0)\) and \((me_L,me_L)\) have different pooled histograms, so Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) separates them. For any inequivalent pair the set in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}separator\text{-}size}\) is nonempty by the negation of Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}probability\text{-}equivalence}\), and all heterogeneous counts lie in \(\{0,\ldots,2m\}\). Thus \(s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,K)\) is well defined.

We first treat \(K=2\). Write \(s=r_1\) for the pooled number of ones and \(i=h_1\) for the treated number of ones. Feasibility of the two arm histograms is exactly
\[
\max\{0,s-m\}\leq i\leq\min\{m,s\},
\]
and Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) gives
\[
A_i:=Q_r(h)=\binom{s}{i}\binom{2m-s}{m-i}. \tag{1}
\]
For two consecutive feasible indices, every denominator below is positive and the elementary adjacent-binomial identities give
\[
\frac{A_{i+1}}{A_i}
=
\frac{(s-i)(m-i)}
{(i+1)(m-s+i+1)}. \tag{2}
\]
The numerator in (2) minus its denominator equals
\[
(s-i)(m-i)-(i+1)(m-s+i+1)
=(m+1)(s-2i-1). \tag{3}
\]
Hence \(A_i\) strictly increases while \(2i<s-1\), is unchanged only across the two central indices when \(s=2i+1\), and strictly decreases while \(2i>s-1\). Also, by binomial symmetry,
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_{s-i}
&=\binom{s}{s-i}\binom{2m-s}{m-s+i}\\
&=\binom{s}{i}\binom{2m-s}{(2m-s)-(m-i)}
=A_i. \tag{4}
\end{aligned}
\]
The feasible interval is invariant under \(i\mapsto s-i\). Equations (2)--(4) show that \(A_i=A_j\) for feasible \(i,j\) only when \(j=i\) or \(j=s-i\): on either side of \(s/2\) the sequence is strict, and (4) pairs the two sides, including the possible equal central pair.

Now take two binary profiles. If their pooled histograms differ, Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) separates them by the diagonal table belonging to the first profile. If their pooled histogram is the same but their coefficients in (1) differ, that same diagonal table separates them. In the remaining case, (4) and the preceding uniqueness show that the profiles are identical or arm transposes; Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) says precisely that both possibilities are equivalent when \(K=2\). Consequently every inequivalent binary pair has a separator \(D_r\) with \(q(D_r)=0\). Since \(q\) is nonnegative, Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}separator\text{-}size}\) yields
\[
s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,2)=0. \tag{5}
\]

Suppose next that \(K\geq3\) and \(m\in\{1,2\}\). We prove the uniform upper bound two. Let \(C=(h,g)\not\sim C'=(h',g')\). A difference between their pooled histograms, or between their diagonal coefficients after the pooled histograms agree, is separated with \(q=0\) by Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\). We may therefore assume
\[
r:=h+g=h'+g',
\qquad
Q_r(h)=Q_r(h'). \tag{6}
\]
Put \(x=h-g\) and \(x'=h'-g'\). For distinct \(a,b\) with \(r_a,r_b>0\), the symmetric switch
\[
S_{ab}=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{ab}+E_{ba} \tag{7}
\]
is nonnegative, has total mass \(2m\), and has weak-null effect sum \((a-b)+(b-a)=0\). Hence Assumptions \(\mathrm{ass{:}table\text{-}mass}\) and \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\), together with Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}legal\text{-}response\text{-}tables}\), make it legal; Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}heterogeneous\text{-}count}\) gives \(q(S_{ab})=2\). Expanding the both-treated and both-controlled allocations of its two special units gives
\[
W_{S_{ab}}(C)
=Q_r(h)\frac{h_ah_b+g_ag_b}{r_ar_b}
=\frac{Q_r(h)}{2r_ar_b}\{r_ar_b+x_ax_b\}. \tag{8}
\]
The same formula holds for \(C'\). All divisions in (8) are valid because \(r_ar_b>0\). Thus a difference \(x_ax_b\neq x'_ax'_b\) supplies a two-unit separator.

Suppose all the products in (8) agree. Coordinates outside the support of \(r\) vanish for both imbalance vectors. The balanced arm-size constraints in Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}profile\text{-}space}\) give \(\sum_a x_a=\sum_a x'_a=0\), and therefore, for every \(a\),
\[
x_a^2=-\sum_{b\neq a}x_ax_b
=-\sum_{b\neq a}x'_ax'_b=(x'_a)^2. \tag{9}
\]
If \(x=0\), (9) gives \(x'=0\). Otherwise, choose \(a\) with \(x_a\neq0\). Equation (9) gives \(x'_a=\varepsilon x_a\) for some \(\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\), and the product identities then give \(x'_b=\varepsilon x_b\) for every \(b\). Since
\[
h=\frac{r+x}{2},\quad g=\frac{r-x}{2},\quad
h'=\frac{r+x'}{2},\quad g'=\frac{r-x'}{2},
\]
the profiles are identical or transposes. They are not identical because they are inequivalent, so \(C'=C^\top\).

It remains to separate this transpose pair. It is not the endpoint-corner pair, by Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\). First suppose the support
\[
S=\{a\in\mathcal A_K:r_a>0\}
\]
contains an interior level. Form the graph on \(S\) having every edge except possibly \(\{0,L\}\). This graph is connected. If
\[
h_ag_b-g_ah_b=0 \tag{10}
\]
on every edge, then \(g=r-h\) turns (10) into \(h_ar_b=r_ah_b\). Connectivity would make \(h_a/r_a\) constant on \(S\). Its value would be
\[
\frac{\sum_a h_a}{\sum_a r_a}=\frac{m}{2m}=\frac12,
\]
forcing \(h=g\), contrary to a nontrivial transpose. Hence there are \(a<b\) in \(S\), with \(\{a,b\}\neq\{0,L\}\), for which (10) is nonzero.

Define
\[
(u,v)=
\begin{cases}
(b-a,0),&a>0,\\
(b+1,1),&a=0.
\end{cases} \tag{11}
\]
In the second case \(b<L\), so all four values in (11) lie in \(\mathcal A_K\). In both cases \(a+u=v+b\), and both response types below are heterogeneous. The table
\[
R_2=D_{r-e_a-e_b}+E_{av}+E_{ub} \tag{12}
\]
is nonnegative and has mass \(2m\); its two heterogeneous effects sum to
\[
(a-v)+(u-b)=0,
\]
so it is legal and has \(q(R_2)=2\). Of the four treatment patterns of the two special units, only treating the \((a,v)\) unit and controlling the \((u,b)\) unit supplies the missing pooled histogram \(e_a+e_b\). Therefore Definition \(\mathrm{def{:}assignment\text{-}count}\) and the adjacent-binomial identities yield
\[
W_{R_2}(C)=Q_r(h)\frac{h_ag_b}{r_ar_b},
\qquad
W_{R_2}(C^\top)=Q_r(h)\frac{g_ah_b}{r_ar_b}. \tag{13}
\]
Here \(Q_r(g)=Q_r(h)\) by coordinatewise binomial symmetry. The nonzero expression in (10) makes the two counts in (13) different, so \(R_2\) separates.

The only remaining possibility is support on the two endpoints. Write
\[
h_0=m-i,\quad h_L=i,\qquad
g_0=m-j,\quad g_L=j,\qquad i\neq j. \tag{14}
\]
Because \(L\geq2\), the table
\[
R'_2=D_{r-e_0-e_L}+E_{L,1}+E_{0,L-1} \tag{15}
\]
is nonnegative, has mass \(2m\), has two heterogeneous units, and is legal because its effects \(L-1\) and \(-(L-1)\) cancel. An endpoint-only profile can occur under (15) only when both special units are treated, since both of their control outcomes are interior. Direct binomial cancellation gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
W_{R'_2}(C)-W_{R'_2}(C^\top)
&=\frac{Q_r(h)}{r_0r_L}
\{h_0h_L-g_0g_L\}\\
&=\frac{Q_r(h)}{r_0r_L}(i-j)(m-i-j). \tag{16}
\end{aligned}
\]
The denominators are positive for a nontrivial endpoint transpose. If \(i+j\neq m\), (16) separates. If \(i+j=m\) and one of \(i,j\) is zero, the pair in (14) is exactly \(\{C_+,C_-\}\), contradicting its inequivalence by Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\). If instead \(i,j>0\) and \(i\neq j\), then \(m=i+j\geq3\), contrary to \(m\in\{1,2\}\). This exhausts the cases and proves
\[
s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,K)\leq2
\quad(K\geq3,\ m\in\{1,2\}). \tag{17}
\]

For the matching lower bound, define the nontrivial transpose pair
\[
\widetilde C_m=
\begin{cases}
(e_0,e_1),&m=1,\\
(2e_0,e_0+e_1),&m=2,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\widetilde C'_m=\widetilde C_m^\top. \tag{18}
\]
Because \(K\geq3\), neither unordered pair in (18) is \(\{C_+,C_-\}\). Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) therefore makes the two profiles inequivalent. A table with \(q(R)=0\) is diagonal. Proposition \(\mathrm{prop{:}diagonal\text{-}reduction}\) makes both counts zero unless its diagonal equals the common pooled histogram in (18); at that diagonal, coordinatewise binomial symmetry gives equal positive counts for a profile and its transpose. Thus no zero-heterogeneity table separates (18). A legal table with \(q(R)=1\) cannot exist: its diagonal units have effect zero, while its unique heterogeneous unit has effect \(a-b\neq0\), contradicting Assumption \(\mathrm{ass{:}weak\text{-}null}\). Hence \(d(\widetilde C_m,\widetilde C'_m)\geq2\), which with (17) proves
\[
s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,K)=2
\quad(K\geq3,\ m\in\{1,2\}). \tag{19}
\]

Finally let \(K\geq3\) and \(m\geq3\). Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}alphabet\text{-}frontier}\) gives \(d(C,C')\leq3\) for every inequivalent pair. In Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}sharp\text{-}separator}\), choose \(i=1\). Its hypotheses hold because \(1\leq m-1\) and \(2\neq m\), and its displayed pair has separator size three. Therefore
\[
s_{\mathrm{bal}}(m,K)=3. \tag{20}
\]
Under the unequal-allocation conditions in the statement, Theorem \(\mathrm{thm{:}unbalanced\text{-}frontier}\) proves directly that its explicitly defined worst-case separator number equals two. This is exactly
\[
s_{\mathrm{unbal}}(n_1,n_0,K)=2, \tag{21}
\]
and completes the phase diagram.
\end{proof}
\par\smallskip\noindent \textit{Justification.} This theorem replaces a coarse uniform three-unit statement by the exact finite-sample separator phase diagram for balanced designs and records the sharp two-unit unequal-allocation value. \textit{Gap.} The invariant-bijection result of \cite{DutzZhang2026} concerns i.i.d. finite-support product laws, not fixed-schedule assignment-induced multivariate-hypergeometric laws, and therefore does not supply this separator-number phase diagram. \textit{Consumer.} A finite-alphabet audit can size its exact sparse schedule search at zero, two, or three heterogeneous units according to the allocation, alphabet, and arm size.

\section*{Technical internal limitation (diagnostic only)}

The cubic separator and the lower bound against every legal schedule with at most two heterogeneous units are needed only when \(K\geq3\) and \(m\geq3\). For \(m\in\{1,2\}\), the exceptional endpoint equation would require distinct positive integers summing to \(m\), so explicit two-unit switches suffice; binary inequivalent pairs are already separated by a diagonal table. The \(K=3,n=6\) enumeration, which gives cubic counts \(1\) and \(0\) and checks all \(103\) legal tables with \(q(R)\leq2\), remains only a diagnostic stress test. Separator complexity counts heterogeneous units, not enumeration time or statistical power. The exact group result also does not justify replacing the HC2 or robust-\(t\) weak-null workflow, whose guarantee and objective are different.

\section{Honest scope}

The results concern fixed no-interference schedules, complete randomization with positive fixed arm sizes, histogram-measurable common bijections, and equally spaced finite alphabets. They cover both balanced and unequal allocations: the balanced partition has the stated transpose or endpoint-corner classes, whereas every unequal partition is singleton. The balanced worst-case separator number is zero for binary outcomes, two for nonbinary outcomes with \(m\in\{1,2\}\), and three for nonbinary outcomes with \(m\geq3\); the unequal worst-case value is two over its stated admissible range. The orbit-rank conclusion applies only to conventional nonrandomized ranks generated by a schedule-universal group. It does not classify every exact weak-null test, validate a particular sharp-null permutation statistic, or cover covariate-adaptive, stratified, or clustered designs.
