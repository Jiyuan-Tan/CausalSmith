Fix \(z\) and first let \(q(\cdot,z)\) be any common density satisfying \(c\le q\le C\).  For a zero-mass direction \(h(\cdot,z)\), put
\[
 H_h(y,z)=\int_0^y h(x,z)\,dx .
\]
The zero-mass condition gives \(H_h(0,z)=H_h(1,z)=0\).  Along the symmetric path \(q_{0,t}=q-th/2\), \(q_{1,t}=q+th/2\), the implicit equation \(F_{a,t}\{Q_{a,t}(u)\}=u\), the density lower bound, and ordinary implicit differentiation give
\[
 \dot Q_{1,0}(u,z)-\dot Q_{0,0}(u,z)
 =-\frac{H_h(Q(u,z),z)}{q(Q(u,z),z)}.                 \tag{1}
\]
Since the two quantiles coincide at \(t=0\), differentiating their squared \(L^2(0,1)\) distance once gives zero.  Differentiating twice, then changing variables \(u=F(y,z)\), gives
\[
 \frac12D^2W_2^2(q\,dy,q\,dy)[h,h]
 =\int_0^1\frac{H_h(y,z)^2}{q(y,z)}\,dy.              \tag{2}
\]
On the other hand, if \(u_h=\mathcal G_{n,z}h\), integration of the declared Neumann equation yields
\[
 q(y,z)\partial_yu_h(y,z)=-H_h(y,z).
\]
The boundary terms vanish, so
\[
 \int_0^1h u_h\,dy
 =\int_0^1q|\partial_yu_h|^2dy
 =\int_0^1\frac{H_h^2}{q}\,dy.                        \tag{3}
\]
Equations (2)--(3), followed by integration with respect to the common marginal \(R_n\), prove the asserted first and second variations.  All divisions use \(q\ge c\), and conditioning and integration over \(z\) use the shared-marginal assumption.

For the displayed cosine pair, write \(e=b(z)/2\).  Its conditional distribution functions are
\[
 F_{1,e}(y)=y+\frac{e}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi y),\qquad
 F_{0,e}(y)=y-\frac{e}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi y).
\]
For \(|e|<1\), their derivatives are uniformly bounded away from zero.  Uniform implicit Taylor expansion of the inverses therefore gives
\[
 F_{1,e}^{-1}(u)-F_{0,e}^{-1}(u)
 =-\frac{2e}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi u)+O(|e|^2)
 =-\frac{b(z)}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi u)+O(|b(z)|^2),         \tag{4}
\]
where the remainder is uniform in \(u,z\).  Squaring (4), integrating in \(u\), and using \(\int_0^1\sin^2(2\pi u)du=1/2\) yields
\[
 W_2^2(Q_{0,n}(\cdot\mid z),Q_{1,n}(\cdot\mid z))
 =\frac{b(z)^2}{8\pi^2}+O(|b(z)|^3).                  \tag{5}
\]
The compact support of \(R_n\) turns the integrated remainder into \(O(\|b\|_\infty^3)\).  Finally, for the one-sided scalar path \(q_0=1\), \(q_1=1+t b(z)\cos(2\pi y)\), equation (2) applies with \(H_h=b(z)\sin(2\pi y)/(2\pi)\); hence the quadratic coefficient is \(\int b^2dR_n/(8\pi^2)\) and the second derivative is \(\int b^2dR_n/(4\pi^2)\).
