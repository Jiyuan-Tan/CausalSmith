Put \(x_0=(0,0,1)\).  We first establish two finite-sample upper bounds from explicit designs and estimators.  Under complete within-pair randomization, let
\[
 H_j=2A_{j1}-1,
 \qquad
 \widehat\tau_{\mathrm P}
 =\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J
 H_j\bigl(Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j1}-Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j2}\bigr).
\]
The definition of this design gives \(A_{j2}=1-A_{j1}\), and the variables \(H_1,\ldots,H_J\) are independent Rademacher variables.  Define
\(
 \mu_{jr}=\{Y_{jr}(1)+Y_{jr}(0)\}/2
\).
Direct substitution, using the definition of \(\tau\), gives the pointwise identity
\[
 \widehat\tau_{\mathrm P}-\tau(Y)
 =\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J H_j(\mu_{j1}-\mu_{j2}).
\]
The estimator is an average of numbers in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), so it belongs to the clipped estimator class.  Independence and \(\mathbb E H_j=0\) imply
\[
 \mathbb E(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm P}-\tau)^2
 =\frac1{J^2}\sum_{j=1}^J(\mu_{j1}-\mu_{j2})^2.
\]
Writing \(\Delta_{ja}=Y_{j1}(a)-Y_{j2}(a)\), the elementary inequality \((u+v)^2\le 2(u^2+v^2)\) and binary support give
\[
 (\mu_{j1}-\mu_{j2})^2
 =\frac14(\Delta_{j0}+\Delta_{j1})^2
 \le \frac12\sum_{a=0}^1\Delta_{ja}^2
 =\frac12\sum_{a=0}^1
 \mathbf 1\{Y_{j1}(a)\ne Y_{j2}(a)\}.
\]
The two armwise discordance budgets therefore yield
\[
 \sup_{Y\in\Theta_{J,s}}
 \mathbb E(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm P}-\tau)^2
 \le \frac{s}{J^2}.
\]

Under independent \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)\) assignment, define the observable estimator
\[
 \widehat\tau_{\mathrm B}
 =\frac1{2J}\sum_{j=1}^J\sum_{r=1}^2
 (2A_{jr}-1)(2Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{jr}-1).
\]
It too lies in \([-1,1]\).  For
\(
 v_{jr}=Y_{jr}(1)+Y_{jr}(0)-1\in\{-1,0,1\}
\), substitution separately for \(A_{jr}=0\) and \(A_{jr}=1\) gives
\[
 \widehat\tau_{\mathrm B}-\tau(Y)
 =\frac1{2J}\sum_{j,r}(2A_{jr}-1)v_{jr}.
\]
The assignment signs are independent Rademacher variables, whence
\[
 \mathbb E(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm B}-\tau)^2
 =\frac1{4J^2}\sum_{j,r}v_{jr}^2
 \le\frac1{2J}.
\]
Both assignment laws are outcome-independent and chosen ex ante, as required by the design class.  The definition of the joint minimax risk thus gives the finite bound
\[
 R^*(J,s)\le \min\left\{\frac{s}{J^2},\frac1{2J}\right\}.
 \tag{1}
\]

We next prove the matching asymptotic lower bound.  This uses a distribution over fixed schedules only as a Bayes lower-bound device; assignment remains the sole randomness after a schedule is fixed.  Suppose first that \(\rho>0\), and fix
\[
 0<r<\min\{\rho,1/2\}.
\]
Let \(b_J=J^{-1/4}\).  Choose a continuously differentiable density \(w\) on \((-1,1)\), positive in the interior, with zero trace at \(-1\) and \(1\), and with information
\(
 0<I_w=\int_{-1}^1\{w'(u)\}^2/w(u)\,du<\infty
\).
Give \(\theta\in(-b_J,b_J)\) density
\(
 w_J(\theta)=b_J^{-1}w(\theta/b_J)
\), whose Fisher information is \(I_w/b_J^2=o(J)\).
Conditional on \(\theta\), draw the \(J\) pairs independently from the following five schedule types:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{type}&H_0&H_1&D&E_+&E_-\\ \hline
\text{probability}
 &(1-r-2b_J)/2&(1-r-2b_J)/2&r
 &b_J+\theta/2&b_J-\theta/2.
\end{array}
\]
Here \(H_y\) consists of two copies of the no-effect type \((y,y)\); \(D\) consists of one \((0,0)\) unit and one \((1,1)\) unit, with the two labelled orientations equally likely; and \(E_+\) and \(E_-\) consist of two copies of \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\), respectively.  All five probabilities are strictly positive for all sufficiently large \(J\).  If \(T_j\in\{-1,0,1\}\) is the average treatment effect in pair \(j\), then
\[
 \tau_J:=\tau(Y)=\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^J T_j,
 \qquad
 \mathbb E(T_j\mid\theta)=\theta,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(T_j\mid\theta)=2b_J-\theta^2.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \mathbb E(\tau_J\mid\theta)=\theta,
 \qquad
 \mathbb E\{(\tau_J-\theta)^2\mid\theta\}
 =\frac{2b_J-\theta^2}{J}\le\frac{2b_J}{J}.
 \tag{2}
\]

Condition on an arbitrary realized assignment.  Design exogeneity makes its probability independent of \(\theta\), and the induced observations from distinct pairs are conditionally independent.  On a mixed pair the two \(\theta\)-informative observation probabilities are
\(
 (r+2b_J+\theta)/2
\) and
\(
 (r+2b_J-\theta)/2
\), so direct summation of \((\partial_\theta p)^2/p\) gives
\[
 I_{\mathrm m}(\theta)
 =\frac{r+2b_J}{(r+2b_J)^2-\theta^2}.
\]
On either an all-treated or an all-control pair the two informative probabilities are
\(
 (1-r+\theta)/2
\) and
\(
 (1-r-\theta)/2
\), up to interchange, and hence
\[
 I_{\mathrm s}(\theta)
 =\frac{1-r}{(1-r)^2-\theta^2}.
\]
Uniformly for \(|\theta|\le b_J\),
\[
 I_{\mathrm m}(\theta)\le \frac1r+o(1),
 \qquad
 I_{\mathrm s}(\theta)\le\frac1{1-r}+o(1)
 \le\frac1r+o(1),
 \tag{3}
\]
where the last inequality uses \(r<1/2\).  Thus the Fisher information in \((A,Y^{\mathrm{obs}})\), averaged over any exogenous design, is at most \(J/r+o(J)\).

We verify the hypotheses of the tightened van Trees lemma rather than assuming a limit law.  The parameter domain \((-b_J,b_J)\) is bounded with piecewise continuously differentiable boundary; the scaled prior is continuously differentiable, has zero boundary trace, and has finite information.  The induced experiment has a finite alphabet and, on the support of any assignment, all its relevant cell probabilities are strictly positive and affine in \(\theta\).  Therefore differentiation under the finite sum is valid and gives the zero-mean likelihood-score identity.  The likelihood and prior scores are square-integrable by (3) and the definition of \(I_w\).  The boundary flux vanishes because the prior density vanishes at both endpoints.  Finally every clipped estimator is square-integrable.  Applying the positive scalar case of the cited van Trees lemma to \(\psi(\theta)=\theta\) therefore gives, uniformly over all designs and estimators,
\[
 \mathbb E(\delta-\theta)^2
 \ge \frac1{I_w/b_J^2+J/r+o(J)}
 =\frac{r+o(1)}J.
 \tag{4}
\]
By the \(L^2\) triangle inequality and (2),
\[
 \{\mathbb E(\delta-\tau_J)^2\}^{1/2}
 \ge
 \{\mathbb E(\delta-\theta)^2\}^{1/2}
 -\{\mathbb E(\tau_J-\theta)^2\}^{1/2},
\]
so (2)--(4) imply
\[
 \mathbb E(\delta-\tau_J)^2\ge\frac{r+o(1)}J.
 \tag{5}
\]

Only pairs of type \(D\) are armwise discordant, and their number \(N_D\) is \(\operatorname{Binomial}(J,r)\), independently of \(\theta\).  Put \(C_J=\{N_D\le s_J\}\).  Since \(s_J/J\to\rho>r\), one may choose \(\lambda>0\) so that, for some \(c>0\) and all sufficiently large \(J\), the exponential Markov calculation
\[
 \Pr(C_J^c)
 \le e^{-\lambda s_J}(1-r+re^\lambda)^J
 \le e^{-cJ}
 \tag{6}
\]
holds.  The event \(C_J\) has probability independent of \(\theta\), and the conditioned schedule prior is supported on \(\Theta_{J,s_J}\).  Since both \(\delta\) and \(\tau_J\) lie in \([-1,1]\), squared loss is at most four.  If \(L_J\) and \(L_{J,C}\) are the Bayes risks before and after conditioning, then
\[
 L_J
 \le \Pr(C_J)L_{J,C}+4\Pr(C_J^c).
\]
Equations (5)--(6) consequently give \(L_{J,C}\ge\{r+o(1)\}/J\).  A supremum over the fixed schedules in \(\Theta_{J,s_J}\) is at least this Bayes average, so
\[
 \liminf_{J\to\infty}J R^*(J,s_J)\ge r.
\]
Letting \(r\uparrow\min\{\rho,1/2\}\), and combining with (1), proves
\[
 \lim_{J\to\infty}J R^*(J,s_J)=\min\{\rho,1/2\}
\]
when \(\rho>0\).  When \(\rho=0\), nonnegativity and (1) give the same conclusion directly.

It remains to prove concentration for \(\rho<1/2\).  First take \(0<\rho<1/2\), fix \(0<r<\rho\), and condition the preceding scalar experiment on an assignment orbit \(n\).  Write \(m_n=n_{\mathrm m}/J\).  Equations (3)--(6), now retained at their orbit-specific values, the scalar van Trees inequality, and the same \(L^2\) transfer give, uniformly in \(n\),
\[
 J\,L_{J,C,n}\ge g_r(m_n)+o(1),
 \qquad
 g_r(m)=\left\{\frac m r+\frac{1-m}{1-r}\right\}^{-1},
 \tag{7}
\]
where \(L_{J,C,n}\) is the Bayes risk after hard-budget conditioning.  Indeed, conditional total likelihood information divided by \(J\) converges uniformly to \(m/r+(1-m)/(1-r)\), the prior information divided by \(J\) vanishes, (2) contributes only \(o(J^{-1})\) after the \(L^2\) transfer, and (6) contributes only \(O(e^{-cJ})\).

Let \(q_J\in\mathcal Q^*(J,s_J)\) and choose the invariant estimator supplied by the definition of \(\mathcal Q^*(J,s_J)\).  Averaging (7) over \(q_J\), the supremum over schedules dominates the conditioned Bayes average, and hence
\[
 J R^*(J,s_J)
 \ge \sum_nq_{J,n}g_r(m_n)+o(1).
 \tag{8}
\]
For \(r<1/2\), direct algebra gives
\[
 g_r(m)
 =\frac{r(1-r)}{r+m(1-2r)},
 \qquad
 g_r(m)-r
 =\frac{r(1-2r)(1-m)}{r+m(1-2r)}
 \ge \frac{r(1-2r)}{1-r}(1-m).
 \tag{9}
\]
Using the already proved value limit in (8)--(9) yields
\[
 \limsup_{J\to\infty}
 \sum_nq_{J,n}(1-m_n)
 \le
 \frac{\rho-r}{r(1-2r)/(1-r)}.
\]
Letting \(r\uparrow\rho\) shows that this limsup is zero.

For \(\rho=0\), use a prior already supported on \(\Theta_{J,0}\): independently over pairs and arms let \(Z_{ja}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)\), and set \(Y_{j1}(a)=Y_{j2}(a)=Z_{ja}\).  Conditional on orbit \(n\), a mixed pair reveals both \(Z_{j0}\) and \(Z_{j1}\), whereas an unmixed pair reveals one and leaves the other as an independent fair Bernoulli variable.  Since
\(
 \tau=J^{-1}\sum_j(Z_{j1}-Z_{j0})
\), its conditional posterior variance is exactly
\[
 \operatorname{Var}(\tau\mid A,Y^{\mathrm{obs}},n)
 =\frac{J-n_{\mathrm m}}{4J^2}.
\]
The posterior mean minimizes conditional squared loss, so every estimator has Bayes risk at least this quantity.  Therefore optimality and the value limit at \(\rho=0\) imply
\[
 J R^*(J,s_J)
 \ge\frac14\sum_nq_{J,n}(1-m_n),
 \qquad
 \sum_nq_{J,n}(1-m_n)\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{10}
\]
Thus the last convergence holds for every \(0\le\rho<1/2\).  For every \(x=(x_{00},x_{11},m)\in\Delta_2\),
\[
 \|x-x_0\|_2^2=x_{00}^2+x_{11}^2+(1-m)^2
 \le2(1-m)^2.
\]
Because a point mass admits only one coupling,
\[
 W_1(\mu_{q_J},\delta_{x_0})
 =\int\|x-x_0\|_2\,d\mu_{q_J}(x)
 \le\sqrt2\sum_nq_{J,n}(1-m_n)\longrightarrow0.
\]
This also gives weak convergence: for any continuous function on the compact simplex, uniform continuity and Markov's inequality applied to \(\|x-x_0\|_2\) show that its integral converges to its value at \(x_0\).  Hence \(\mu_{q_J}\Rightarrow\delta_{(0,0,1)}\), with the asserted equivalent \(W_1\) conclusion.

%%% FIELD proof-first-order-face
Let
\[
 v_J=\max_{h\in\mathcal H_{J,s_J}}\mathcal R_h(q_J,\eta_J).
\]
The hypothesis and Theorem 4 give
\[
 Jv_J=J R^*(J,s_J)+o(1)\longrightarrow\frac12.
 \tag{11}
\]
We first derive the restrictions on an arbitrary weak accumulation point.  Pass to a subsequence, still indexed by \(J\), for which \(\mu_{q_J}\Rightarrow\mu\).  Fix \(r\in(0,\rho)\); when \(\rho=1/2\), take \(r<1/2\).  Put \(b_J=J^{-1/4}\), and give \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) independent priors with density \(b_J^{-1}w(\cdot/b_J)\), where \(w\) is the smooth zero-boundary density used in the proof of Theorem 4.  Conditional on \((\theta,\phi)\), draw pairs independently with probabilities
\[
 \frac{1-r-2b_J-\phi}{2},\quad
 \frac{1-r-2b_J+\phi}{2},\quad
 r,\quad
 b_J+\frac\theta2,\quad
 b_J-\frac\theta2
 \tag{12}
\]
for \(H_0,H_1,D,E_+,E_-\), respectively.  These probabilities are strictly positive for all sufficiently large \(J\).  As in (2),
\[
 \mathbb E(\tau_J\mid\theta,\phi)=\theta,
 \qquad
 \mathbb E\{(\tau_J-\theta)^2\mid\theta,\phi\}
 =\frac{2b_J-\theta^2}{J}\le\frac{2b_J}{J}.
 \tag{13}
\]
The number of discordant pairs is again \(\operatorname{Binomial}(J,r)\) and is independent of \((\theta,\phi)\).

For parameter order \((\theta,\phi)\), summing \((\nabla p)(\nabla p)^{\!\top}/p\) over the finite local observation alphabet gives the exact information matrices for a mixed, all-treated, and all-control pair:
\[
 I_{\mathrm m,J}(\theta,\phi)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \dfrac{r+2b_J}{(r+2b_J)^2-\theta^2}&0\\[5pt]
 0&\dfrac{1-r-2b_J}{(1-r-2b_J)^2-\phi^2}
 \end{pmatrix},
 \tag{14}
\]
\[
 I_{11,J}(\theta,\phi)=
 \frac{1-r}{(1-r)^2-(\theta+\phi)^2}
 \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 I_{00,J}(\theta,\phi)=
 \frac{1-r}{(1-r)^2-(\phi-\theta)^2}
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.
 \tag{15}
\]
For example, on a mixed pair the four observed categories have probabilities
\[
 \frac{1-r-2b_J-\phi}{2},\quad
 \frac{1-r-2b_J+\phi}{2},\quad
 \frac{r+2b_J+\theta}{2},\quad
 \frac{r+2b_J-\theta}{2},
\]
which proves (14); combining \(H_0\) with \(E_-\) and \(H_1\) with \(E_+\) proves the first formula in (15), and the analogous control-arm combination proves the second.

Conditional on orbit \(n\), set
\[
 m_n=\frac{n_{\mathrm m}}J,
 \qquad
 d_n=\frac{n_{11}-n_{00}}J.
\]
The prior-averaged likelihood information is the corresponding sum of (14)--(15).  Since \(|\theta|,|\phi|\le b_J\), its value divided by \(J\), including the prior information \(I_w b_J^{-2}I_2/J=o(1)I_2\), converges uniformly over all orbits to
\[
 M_r(m,d)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \dfrac m r+\dfrac{1-m}{1-r}&\dfrac d{1-r}\\[6pt]
 \dfrac d{1-r}&\dfrac1{1-r}
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \tag{16}
\]

The tightened matrix van Trees lemma applies conditionally on every orbit.  Indeed, the parameter domain \((-b_J,b_J)^2\) is a bounded rectangle with piecewise continuously differentiable boundary; the product prior is continuously differentiable, has zero trace on every boundary face, and has finite information.  The experiment has a finite alphabet with strictly positive affine cell probabilities on the prior support.  Hence coordinatewise differentiation under the finite sum yields the zero-mean likelihood-score identity, all likelihood and prior scores are square-integrable by (14)--(15), and the zero boundary trace makes the integration-by-parts flux vanish.  The clipped estimator is square-integrable.  The total information matrix is positive definite because the prior-information matrix is positive definite, so the ordinary-inverse conclusion of the cited lemma applies to \(\psi(\theta,\phi)=\theta\), with \(a=e_1=(1,0)^{\!\top}\).

To pass uniformly through possibly singular limits, for \(\varepsilon>0\) define the continuous bounded function
\[
 g_{r,\varepsilon}(m,d)
 =e_1^{\!\top}\{M_r(m,d)+\varepsilon I_2\}^{-1}e_1.
\]
Uniform convergence in (16) implies that, for all sufficiently large \(J\), the total information divided by \(J\) is bounded above in Loewner order by \(M_r(m_n,d_n)+\varepsilon I_2\).  Since inversion reverses Loewner order on positive definite matrices, van Trees gives
\[
 J\,\mathbb E\{(\eta_J-\theta)^2\mid n\}
 \ge g_{r,\varepsilon}(m_n,d_n).
\]
The \(L^2\) triangle inequality and (13) replace \(\theta\) by \(\tau_J\), at a uniform error tending to zero because \(g_{r,\varepsilon}\le\varepsilon^{-1}\).  Conditioning on the event \(C_J=\{N_D\le s_J\}\) changes this bound by \(o(1)\) after multiplication by \(J\): the exponential estimate (6) applies because \(r<\rho\), the event is parameter-independent, and squared loss is at most four.  Thus, uniformly in \(n\),
\[
 J\,\mathbb E\{(\eta_J-\tau_J)^2\mid n,C_J\}
 \ge g_{r,\varepsilon}(m_n,d_n)-o(1).
 \tag{17}
\]
The conditioned prior is supported on \(\Theta_{J,s_J}\).  Averaging (17) over \(q_J\), its Bayes risk is bounded above by the worst fixed-schedule risk \(v_J\), and therefore
\[
 Jv_J\ge\int g_{r,\varepsilon}(m,d)\,d\mu_{q_J}-o(1).
 \tag{18}
\]
Weak convergence, bounded continuity of \(g_{r,\varepsilon}\), and (11) give
\[
 \int g_{r,\varepsilon}(m,d)\,d\mu\le\frac12.
\]
As \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), these functions increase pointwise to
\[
 g_r(m,d)=
 \left\{\frac m r+\frac{1-m-d^2}{1-r}\right\}^{-1},
 \tag{19}
\]
with value \(+\infty\) when the denominator is zero.  To see (19), take the Schur complement of the lower-right entry of (16).  Feasibility in \(\Delta_2\) gives \(|d|\le1-m\), hence \(1-m-d^2\ge0\); the denominator vanishes only at \((m,d)=(0,1)\) or \((0,-1)\).  Monotone convergence in (18) therefore proves
\[
 \int g_r(m,d)\,d\mu\le\frac12
 \qquad(0<r<\rho).
 \tag{20}
\]
This regularization also explains the singular case of the tightened van Trees lemma: at either singular endpoint \(e_1\) is not in the range of \(M_r\), and the regularized bound diverges, so no unjustified ordinary inverse is taken.

If \(\rho>1/2\), choose \(r=1/2\) in (20).  If \(\rho=1/2\), let \(r\uparrow1/2\) in (20) and use Fatou's lemma.  In either case,
\[
 \int\frac1{2(1-d^2)}\,d\mu\le\frac12.
 \tag{21}
\]
The integrand is at least \(1/2\), with equality exactly when \(d=0\); therefore
\[
 \mu\{d=0\}=1.
 \tag{22}
\]
On this line put
\[
 G_\mu(r)=\int
 \left\{\frac m r+\frac{1-m}{1-r}\right\}^{-1}d\mu.
\]
For \(r\) in a fixed neighborhood of \(1/2\), the integrand and its derivative are uniformly bounded on \(0\le m\le1\), so differentiation under the integral is justified.  Direct differentiation gives
\[
 G_\mu(1/2)=\frac12,
 \qquad
 G_\mu'(1/2)=\int(2m-1)\,d\mu
 =2\int m\,d\mu-1.
 \tag{23}
\]
At \(\rho=1/2\), (20) holds for \(r<1/2\), while \(G_\mu(1/2)=1/2\); its left derivative at \(1/2\) is therefore nonnegative, and (23) gives \(\int m\,d\mu\ge1/2\).  If \(\rho>1/2\), (20) holds on both sides of \(1/2\), so \(G_\mu\) has a local maximum there; (23) then gives \(\int m\,d\mu=1/2\).  Together with (22), these are precisely the conditions defining \(\mathcal F_\rho\).  This proves necessity.

For recovery, fix \(\mu\in\mathcal F_\rho\).  The balanced finite-orbit grid is
\[
 E_J=\left\{
 \left(\frac kJ,\frac kJ,1-\frac{2k}J\right):
 0\le k\le\lfloor J/2\rfloor
 \right\}.
 \tag{24}
\]
It has mixed-coordinate mesh at most \(2/J\) on the line \(d=0\).  Randomly rounding each point on that line to its adjacent grid points constructs a probability law \(\widetilde q_J\) on \(E_J\) coupled to \(\mu\) with mixed-coordinate error at most \(2/J\), and hence with Euclidean error at most \(\sqrt6/J\); in particular \(\mu_{\widetilde q_J}\Rightarrow\mu\), and its mean mixed fraction differs from \(\int m\,d\mu\) by at most \(2/J\).  If \(\int m\,d\mu=1/2\), mix \(\widetilde q_J\), as needed, with the grid endpoint having mixed fraction \(1\) or with the endpoint having mixed fraction \(0\) for even \(J\) and \(1/J\) for odd \(J\), choosing the mixing weight so that the resulting mean is exactly \(1/2\).  The required weight is \(O(J^{-1})\), so weak convergence is preserved.  If \(\rho=1/2\) and \(\int m\,d\mu>1/2\), no correction is needed for all sufficiently large \(J\).  We have therefore constructed laws \(q_J\) supported on (24), converging weakly to \(\mu\), such that
\[
 \bar m_J:=\sum_nq_{J,n}\frac{n_{\mathrm m}}J
 =\frac12\quad\text{if }\rho>1/2,
 \qquad
 \bar m_J\ge\frac12\quad\text{if }\rho=1/2.
 \tag{25}
\]

Let the assignment law be uniform within each orbit and have orbit masses \(q_J\), and use
\[
 \widehat\tau_J
 =\frac1{2J}\sum_{j,r}(2A_{jr}-1)(2Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{jr}-1).
 \tag{26}
\]
The law is ex ante and outcome-independent.  The estimator is clipped and invariant, hence it induces an array \(\eta_J\) indexed by the assignment and observation orbits.  Write \(S_{jr}=2A_{jr}-1\).  Every orbit in (24) has exactly \(J\) treated units, and invariance gives
\[
 \mathbb E S_{jr}=0,
 \qquad
 c_{\mathrm w}:=\mathbb E(S_{j1}S_{j2})=1-2\bar m_J.
 \tag{27}
\]
For units in distinct pairs, invariance makes \(c_{\mathrm b}:=\mathbb E(S_{jr}S_{k\ell})\) constant.  Squaring the deterministic identity \(\sum_{j,r}S_{jr}=0\), taking expectations, and counting ordered within-pair and between-pair terms gives
\[
 0=2J+2Jc_{\mathrm w}+4J(J-1)c_{\mathrm b},
 \qquad
 c_{\mathrm b}=-\frac{1-\bar m_J}{J-1}.
 \tag{28}
\]

Set \(v_{jr}=Y_{jr}(1)+Y_{jr}(0)-1\) and \(w_j=v_{j1}+v_{j2}\).  The same pointwise substitution as in the Bernoulli calculation gives
\(
 \widehat\tau_J-\tau=(2J)^{-1}\sum_{j,r}S_{jr}v_{jr}
\).  Equations (27)--(28) then yield the exact expansion
\[
\begin{split}
 4J^2\mathbb E(\widehat\tau_J-\tau)^2
 ={}&\sum_j\{v_{j1}^2+v_{j2}^2
       +2c_{\mathrm w}v_{j1}v_{j2}\}\\
 &+c_{\mathrm b}\left\{
       \left(\sum_jw_j\right)^2-\sum_jw_j^2
       \right\}.
\end{split}
 \tag{29}
\]
By (25), \(-1\le c_{\mathrm w}\le0\), and (28) gives \(c_{\mathrm b}\le0\).  Since \(|w_j|\le2\),
\[
 c_{\mathrm b}\left\{
 (\sum_jw_j)^2-\sum_jw_j^2\right\}
 \le -c_{\mathrm b}\sum_jw_j^2
 \le\frac{4J(1-\bar m_J)}{J-1}=O(1)
 \tag{30}
\]
uniformly over schedules.  Let
\(
 e_j=\sum_{a=0}^1\mathbf 1\{Y_{j1}(a)\ne Y_{j2}(a)\}
\).  The four binary unit types give, for every \(c\in[-1,0]\),
\[
 v_{j1}^2+v_{j2}^2+2cv_{j1}v_{j2}
 \le2(1+c)-2ce_j.
 \tag{31}
\]
For completeness, if both \(v\)'s are nonzero, the unit types are \((0,0)\) or \((1,1)\), and (31) is equality for equal as well as opposite signs; if exactly one is nonzero, then \(e_j=1\) and the two sides are \(1\) and \(2\); if both vanish, the right side is nonnegative whether the two effect types agree or disagree.  Thus (31) is exhaustive.

Summing (31), using the two armwise bounds \(D_0(Y)+D_1(Y)\le2s_J\), and then applying (29)--(30), gives
\[
 \sup_{h\in\mathcal H_{J,s_J}}
 \mathcal R_h(q_J,\eta_J)
 \le
 \frac{1+c_{\mathrm w}(1-2s_J/J)}{2J}
 +O(J^{-2}).
 \tag{32}
\]
If \(\rho>1/2\), (25) makes \(c_{\mathrm w}=0\).  If \(\rho=1/2\), then \(-1\le c_{\mathrm w}\le0\) and \(1-2s_J/J=o(1)\).  In both cases the right side of (32) is \(1/(2J)+o(J^{-1})\).  Theorem 4 gives \(R^*(J,s_J)=1/(2J)+o(J^{-1})\), and the minimax value is no larger than the worst risk of this feasible procedure.  Hence
\[
 \max_{h\in\mathcal H_{J,s_J}}\mathcal R_h(q_J,\eta_J)
 =R^*(J,s_J)+o(J^{-1}),
\]
while construction gives \(\mu_{q_J}\Rightarrow\mu\).  This proves recovery and completes the characterization.
