Write \(c_3=f_{R,\{3\}}(\varnothing)\) and \(c_2'=f_{R',\{2\}}(\varnothing)\) for the constant root mechanisms.  By the structural equations and \(\mathrm{ass:block-noise-independence}\),
\[
 X_3=c_3+\varepsilon_{R,\{3\}},\qquad
 X_2=c_2'+\varepsilon_{R',\{2\}},
\]
and each displayed root variable is independent of the nonroot innovation in its own representation.  Define
\[
 r(y)=p_{\varepsilon_{R,\{3\}}}(y-c_3),
 \qquad
 s(x)=p_{\varepsilon_{R',\{2\}}}(x-c_2').
\tag{1}
\]
By \(\mathrm{ass:innovation-positive-density}\) and \(\mathrm{ass:innovation-c3-density}\), \(r\) and \(s\) are strictly positive densities and \(\log r,\log s\in C^3(\mathbb R)\).  In particular, they are the marginal densities of \(X_3\) and \(X_2\), respectively.  Applying \(\mathrm{lem:triangular-observed-density}\) to the two representations of the same law gives, pointwise on \(\mathbb R^3\),
\[
 r(x_3)p_\varepsilon\bigl(x_1-f_1(x_3),x_2-f_2(x_3)\bigr)
 =s(x_2)p_\eta\bigl(x_1-g_1(x_2),x_3-g_3(x_2)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]
This proves the corrected density factorization.  Notice that no zero normalization of a root mechanism has been assumed.

Abbreviate \(x=x_2\), \(y=x_3\), and set
\[
 v=x-f_2(y),\qquad z=y-g_3(x),
 \qquad \delta=f_1(y)-g_1(x).
\tag{3}
\]
Substitution of \(x_1=f_1(y)+t\) into (2) gives
\[
 r(y)p_\varepsilon(t,v)=s(x)p_\eta(t+\delta,z)
 \quad\text{for every }(t,x,y)\in\mathbb R^3.
\tag{4}
\]
Apply \(\mathrm{lem:tonelli-nonnegative-product-integral}\) to each nonnegative bivariate innovation density.  Since each has total integral one,
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}m_\varepsilon(v)\,dv=1,
 \qquad
 \int_{\mathbb R}m_\eta(z)\,dz=1.
\tag{5}
\]
Strict positivity of the joint densities implies that each slice integral is positive, with \(+\infty\) allowed.  Equation (5) implies that the sets on which the slice integrals are finite are conull.  Thus \(E_\varepsilon\) and \(E_\eta\) are conull, as asserted.  For \((x,y)\in D\), integration of (4) in \(t\), using Tonelli and translation invariance of Lebesgue measure, gives
\[
 r(y)m_\varepsilon(v)=s(x)m_\eta(z).
\tag{6}
\]
Every quantity in (6) is finite and strictly positive.  Dividing (4) by (6) proves
\[
 q_\varepsilon(t\mid v)=q_\eta(t+\delta\mid z),
 \qquad t\in\mathbb R,quad (x,y)\in D.
\tag{7}
\]

Fix \(t_0\in\mathbb R\).  Because all factors in (4) are finite and strictly positive, division of (4) by its value at \(t_0\), followed by logarithms, is legitimate for every \((x,y)\), not only on \(D\).  It yields the global identity
\[
 \nu_\varepsilon(t,v)-\nu_\varepsilon(t_0,v)
 =\nu_\eta(t+\delta,z)-\nu_\eta(t_0+\delta,z).
\tag{8}
\]
Consequently the continuity extension requested in the statement is immediate; in what follows only (8), and never a slice normalizer or conditional density, is differentiated.

Define
\[
 S(t,v)=\partial_t\nu_\varepsilon(t,v),
 \qquad T(u,z)=\partial_u\nu_\eta(u,z),
 \qquad J(x,y)=1-f_2'(y)g_3'(x).
\tag{9}
\]
The innovation log-densities and mechanisms are \(C^3\) by \(\mathrm{ass:innovation-c3-density}\) and \(\mathrm{ass:mechanisms-c3}\), so the following differentiations are valid.  Differentiate (8) first in \(t\), and then differentiate the resulting equality in \(x\) and in \(y\).  The chain rule gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 S_v(t,v)&=-g_1'(x)T_u(t+\delta,z)-g_3'(x)T_z(t+\delta,z),\\
 -f_2'(y)S_v(t,v)&=f_1'(y)T_u(t+\delta,z)+T_z(t+\delta,z),\\
 S_t(t,v)&=T_u(t+\delta,z).
 \end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]
On \(\Omega=\{J\ne0\}\), elementary elimination in (10) gives
\[
 S_v(t,v)=a(x,y)S_t(t,v),
 \qquad
 a(x,y)=\frac{g_3'(x)f_1'(y)-g_1'(x)}{J(x,y)},
\tag{11}
\]
and
\[
 T_z(u,z)=b(x,y)T_u(u,z),
 \qquad
 b(x,y)=\frac{f_2'(y)g_1'(x)-f_1'(y)}{J(x,y)}.
\tag{12}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 D\Phi(x,y)=
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-f_2'(y)\\-g_3'(x)&1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det D\Phi(x,y)=J(x,y).
\tag{13}
\]
The set \(\Omega\) is open by continuity of \(J\).  At each point of \(\Omega\), \(\mathrm{lem:inverse-function-theorem-c1}\) applied to \(\Phi\) supplies an inverse chart with a \(C^1\) inverse \(\psi\).  Differentiating \(\Phi(\psi(w))=w\) and using (13) gives
\[
 D\psi(w)=\bigl[D\Phi(\psi(w))\bigr]^{-1}.
\]
Because \(D\Phi\in C^2\), the right side is first \(C^1\), which makes \(\psi\in C^2\); substituting this improved regularity into the same formula makes the right side \(C^2\), which makes \(\psi\in C^3\).  Thus \(\Phi\) is a \(C^3\) diffeomorphism on every sufficiently small inverse chart.

We also verify the asserted conullness on charts.  A \(C^1\) map is Lipschitz on every compact ball on which its derivative is bounded.  A Lipschitz map from \(\mathbb R^2\) to \(\mathbb R^2\) sends Lebesgue-null sets to Lebesgue-null sets: cover a null set by squares having arbitrarily small total area and cover each image square by a square whose side is at most a fixed Lipschitz multiple of the original diameter.  Exhausting the open image of an inverse chart by countably many such compact balls shows that its \(C^1\) inverse sends null sets to null sets.  Since \(E_\varepsilon\times E_\eta\) is conull, it follows that
\[
 D_U=U\cap\Phi^{-1}(E_\varepsilon\times E_\eta)
\]
is conull in every inverse chart \(U\).  Every nonempty open subset of \(U\) has positive Lebesgue measure, so \(D_U\) is dense in \(U\).

We next solve (11)--(12) locally.  Work first on the inverse image of an open rectangle \(I\times K\) contained in a chart image; such rectangular subcharts cover every inverse chart.  Express \(a\) in the \((v,z)\) coordinates.  If \(v\in E_\varepsilon\), then \(S_t(\mathord\cdot,v)\) cannot vanish identically.  Otherwise \(\nu_\varepsilon(\mathord\cdot,v)\) would be affine, and the exponential of an affine function has infinite integral on \(\mathbb R\), contrary to \(m_\varepsilon(v)<\infty\).  Choose \(t\) for which \(S_t(t,v)\ne0\).  Equation (11) then shows that \(a(v,z)=S_v(t,v)/S_t(t,v)\) is independent of \(z\in K\).  The regular values \(E_\varepsilon\cap I\) are dense in \(I\), and \(a\) is continuous; hence \(a(v,z)=A(v)\) throughout \(I\times K\), with \(A\in C^2(I)\).  Choose \(h\in C^3(I)\) satisfying \(h'=-A\).  Along the characteristics of (11),
\[
 \frac{d}{dv}S(w+h(v),v)=h'(v)S_t(w+h(v),v)+S_v(w+h(v),v)=0.
\tag{14}
\]
It follows that, for suitable \(C^3\) functions \(\lambda\) and \(c\),
\[
 \nu_\varepsilon(t,v)=\lambda(t-h(v))+c(v).
\tag{15}
\]
Choose a regular base value \(v^0\in E_\varepsilon\cap I\) and add a constant to \(h\) so that \(h(v^0)=0\).  The finiteness of the base slice shows that \(0<\int_{\mathbb R}e^{\lambda(u)}\,du<\infty\).  After absorbing this normalizing constant into \(c\), (15) becomes
\[
 p_\varepsilon(t,v)=c_\varepsilon(v)\rho(t-h(v)),
 \qquad (t,v)\in\mathbb R\times I,
\tag{16}
\]
where \(c_\varepsilon\) is positive and \(C^3\), and \(\rho\) is a strictly positive unit-integral density with \(\log\rho\in C^3(\mathbb R)\).  In particular, every slice with \(v\in I\), including an initially exceptional slice, is now finite and regular.  Applying the same argument to (12) gives
\[
 p_\eta(u,z)=c_\eta(z)\widetilde\rho(u-k(z)),
 \qquad (u,z)\in\mathbb R\times K,
\tag{17}
\]
with \(k\in C^3(K)\), a positive \(C^3\) coefficient, and a strictly positive unit-integral profile \(\widetilde\rho\) whose logarithm is \(C^3\).

We shall repeatedly use the following elementary uniqueness fact.  A strictly positive integrable density has no nonzero period.  Indeed, if \(\rho(t+c)=\rho(t)\) for \(c\ne0\), some interval of length smaller than \(|c|\) has positive \(\rho\)-mass, whereas its translates by integer multiples of \(c\) are disjoint and have the same mass, contradicting \(\int\rho=1\).

Equations (16)--(17) show that all slices over \(I\times K\) are regular, so (7) applies at every point of this rectangular chart.  At one point it shows that \(\widetilde\rho\) is a translate of \(\rho\); absorb that fixed translation into \(k\).  At an arbitrary point, (7) then gives
\[
 \rho(t-h(v))=\rho(t+\delta-k(z))\quad\text{for every }t.
\]
The no-period fact implies
\[
 \delta=k(z)-h(v).
\tag{18}
\]
This proves all local assertions on a rectangular subchart.  On overlaps, two expressions of the same normalized conditional slice force the two \(h\)'s to differ by a constant.  Equation (18) forces the two \(k\)'s to differ by the same constant.  The sum of transition constants around a closed overlap chain would be a period of the initial unit profile and is therefore zero.  Hence transport is path independent.  Covering a connected inverse chart by rectangular subcharts and applying this argument proves the asserted chart conclusion on the whole chart.

It remains to show that nonsingular charts collectively see every residual coordinate value.  Define
\[
 \Sigma_v=\left\{v_0:J(v_0+f_2(y),y)=0\text{ for every }y\in\mathbb R\right\}.
\tag{19}
\]
Suppose \(v_0\in\Sigma_v\).  Then
\[
 f_2'(y)g_3'(v_0+f_2(y))=1\quad\text{for every }y.
\tag{20}
\]
Thus \(f_2'\) never vanishes and has a constant sign.  Differentiating \(g_3(v_0+f_2(y))-y\) and using (20) shows that this function is constant, so for some \(z_0\),
\[
 g_3(v_0+f_2(y))=y-z_0.
\tag{21}
\]
The range of the strictly monotone function \(f_2\) is an interval.  A finite endpoint of this interval would be approached as \(y\) tends to one of \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\); continuity of \(g_3\) at the corresponding finite point would then contradict the divergence of the right side of (21).  Hence \(f_2(\mathbb R)=\mathbb R\), and
\[
 g_3(x)=f_2^{-1}(x-v_0)-z_0.
\tag{22}
\]

Let \(Y=X_3\), \(E=\varepsilon_2\), \(X=X_2=f_2(Y)+E\), and \(Z=\eta_3=Y-g_3(X)\).  By \(\mathrm{ass:block-noise-independence}\), \(Y\perp\!\!\!\perp E\) and \(X\perp\!\!\!\perp Z\).  Put \(u=f_2^{-1}(X-v_0)\), and let \(\sigma\in\{-1,1\}\) be the sign of \(f_2'\).  Equations (22) and the two structural equations imply
\[
 E-v_0=f_2(u)-f_2(Y),
 \qquad Z-z_0=Y-u,
 \qquad \operatorname{sgn}(E-v_0)=-\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(Z-z_0).
\tag{23}
\]
The joint density \(p_\varepsilon\) is strictly positive.  Integration over rectangles on either side of \(E=v_0\) gives
\[
 0<\mathbb P(E>v_0)<1,
 \qquad \mathbb P(E=v_0)=0.
\tag{24}
\]
By (23), \(A=\{E>v_0\}\) agrees almost surely with one of \(\{Z<z_0\}\) and \(\{Z>z_0\}\).  Since \(X\perp\!\!\!\perp Z\), the event \(A\) is independent of \(X\).

This is impossible.  Put \(W=f_2(Y)\), so \(W\perp\!\!\!\perp E\) and \(X=W+E\).  Let \(E_+\) and \(E_-\) have the conditional laws of \(E\) given \(A\) and \(A^c\), respectively, and couple them with one common independent copy of \(W\).  Independence of \(A\) and \(X\) would imply
\[
 \mathcal L(W+E_+)=\mathcal L(X\mid A)
 =\mathcal L(X\mid A^c)=\mathcal L(W+E_-).
\tag{25}
\]
But \(E_+>v_0>E_-\) almost surely, and hence \(W+E_+>W+E_-\) almost surely.  The bounded strictly increasing function \(q\mapsto\arctan q\) has strictly larger expectation under the first variable, contradicting (25).  Therefore \(\Sigma_v=\varnothing\).

The corresponding singular set in the other coordinate is
\[
 \Sigma_z=\left\{z_0:J(x,z_0+g_3(x))=0\text{ for every }x\in\mathbb R\right\}.
\tag{26}
\]
Suppose \(z_0\in\Sigma_z\).  The same differentiation argument, now with the roles reversed, shows that \(g_3'\) never vanishes, \(g_3(\mathbb R)=\mathbb R\), and for some \(v_0\),
\[
 f_2(z_0+g_3(x))=x-v_0.
\tag{27}
\]
Let \(X=X_2\), \(Z=\eta_3\), \(Y=X_3=g_3(X)+Z\), and \(E=\varepsilon_2=X-f_2(Y)\).  Put \(u=g_3^{-1}(Y-z_0)\), and let \(\tau\) be the sign of \(g_3'\).  Equation (27) gives
\[
 Z-z_0=g_3(u)-g_3(X),
 \qquad E-v_0=X-u,
 \qquad \operatorname{sgn}(Z-z_0)=-\tau\operatorname{sgn}(E-v_0).
\tag{28}
\]
Strict positivity of \(p_\eta\) gives \(0<\mathbb P(Z>z_0)<1\) and \(\mathbb P(Z=z_0)=0\).  The event \(A' =\{Z>z_0\}\) is, by (28), a threshold event of \(E\).  Since \(E\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\), it is independent of \(Y\).  On the other hand, \(W'=g_3(X)\) is independent of \(Z\) and \(Y=W'+Z\).  Coupling the conditional laws of \(Z\) above and below \(z_0\) with a common independent copy of \(W'\), exactly as in (25), produces two strictly ordered variables that would have the same law.  This contradiction proves \(\Sigma_z=\varnothing\).

For every \(v\in\mathbb R\), the equality \(\Sigma_v=\varnothing\) supplies a point \((v+f_2(y),y)\in\Omega\).  Rectangular inverse charts about such points therefore have projected \(v\)-intervals forming an open cover of \(\mathbb R\).  Each chart gives (16).  If two projected intervals overlap, their formulas describe the same density \(p_\varepsilon(t,v)\).  Normalizing at one common \(v\) shows that their profiles differ by a translate; after aligning the profiles, the no-period fact shows that their shift functions differ by a single constant throughout the overlap.  Declare two projected intervals equivalent if they are connected by a finite overlap chain.  Every equivalence class and its complement are open in \(\mathbb R\); connectedness of \(\mathbb R\) leaves one class.  The closed-chain no-period argument makes the accumulated constants path independent.  Thus the local expressions glue to
\[
 p_\varepsilon(t,v)=c_\varepsilon(v)\rho(t-h(v))
 \quad\text{for every }(t,v)\in\mathbb R^2,
\tag{29}
\]
where \(h,c_\varepsilon\in C^3(\mathbb R)\), \(c_\varepsilon>0\), and \(\rho\) is a fixed strictly positive unit-integral density with \(\log\rho\in C^3\).  In particular, integration in \(t\) gives \(m_\varepsilon=c_\varepsilon\), so every slice is regular.  The equality \(\Sigma_z=\varnothing\) and the identical gluing argument give
\[
 p_\eta(u,z)=c_\eta(z)\widetilde\rho(u-k(z))
 \quad\text{for every }(u,z)\in\mathbb R^2,
\tag{30}
\]
with the analogous properties; again every \(\eta\)-slice is regular.

The equality \(\Sigma_v=\varnothing\) also implies \(\Omega\ne\varnothing\).  Choose one point of \(\Omega\).  Substitution of (29)--(30) into (7) at that point shows that \(\widetilde\rho\) is a translate of \(\rho\); absorb the translation into \(k\).  Since every slice is now regular, (7) holds for every \((x,y)\).  Therefore
\[
 \rho(t-h(v))=\rho(t+\delta-k(z))
 \quad\text{for all }(t,x,y),
\]
and the no-period fact gives the global identity
\[
 \delta=k(z)-h(v).
\tag{31}
\]
This also proves the stated critical-set continuation.  Along any nonsingular-chart sequence approaching a point of \(\Omega^c\), the shifts converge to the unique values of the global \(C^3\) functions \(h\) and \(k\), independently of the overlap chain.  Since \(\log\rho\in C^3\), translation is continuous, locally uniformly in \(t\), together with its first two \(t\)-derivatives.  Hence the translated log-density ratios have precisely the asserted unique limits.  This conclusion was obtained from (8) and the unconditional density factorizations, without assigning any regularity to \(m_\varepsilon,m_\eta,q_\varepsilon\), or \(q_\eta\).

Define
\[
 \zeta=\varepsilon_1-h(\varepsilon_2).
\tag{32}
\]
For Borel sets \(A,C\subseteq\mathbb R\), (29), Tonelli, and the translation substitution \(u=t-h(v)\) give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathbb P(\zeta\in A,\varepsilon_2\in C)
 &=\int_C\int_{\mathbb R}{\mathbf 1}_A(t-h(v))
 c_\varepsilon(v)\rho(t-h(v))\,dt\,dv\\
 &=\left(\int_A\rho(u)\,du\right)
   \left(\int_Cc_\varepsilon(v)\,dv\right).
 \end{aligned}
\tag{33}
\]
Thus \(\zeta\perp\!\!\!\perp\varepsilon_2\), and \(\zeta\) has density \(\rho\).  The density is strictly positive, integrates to one, and has a \(C^3\) log-density, so \(\mathcal L(\zeta)\in\operatorname{SmoothPos}(1)\) by \(\mathrm{def:smooth-positive-laws}\).  The same calculation using (30) gives
\[
 \eta_1=k(\eta_3)+\zeta',
 \qquad \zeta'\perp\!\!\!\perp\eta_3,
 \qquad \mathcal L(\zeta')\in\operatorname{SmoothPos}(1).
\tag{34}
\]
Equations (32)--(34) contradict \(\mathrm{ass:innovation-atomicity}\) for the nontrivial coordinate splits of the two bivariate innovations.

For completeness, consider separately the identically singular regime named in the statement.  If \(\Omega=\varnothing\), then
\[
 f_2'(y)g_3'(x)=1\quad\text{for all }(x,y).
\tag{35}
\]
Fixing first \(x\) and then \(y\) shows that both derivatives are nonzero constants and reciprocal.  Hence, for some \(a\ne0,c,d_0\),
\[
 f_2(y)=ay+c,
 \qquad g_3(x)=a^{-1}x+d_0.
\tag{36}
\]
The structural equations then imply
\[
 X_2=aX_3+c+\varepsilon_2,
 \qquad
 \eta_3=-a^{-1}\varepsilon_2-a^{-1}c-d_0.
\tag{37}
\]
Let \(E=\varepsilon_2\) and \(W=aX_3+c\).  Block-noise independence in the first representation gives \(E\perp\!\!\!\perp W\), while block-noise independence in the second representation and (37) give \(E\perp\!\!\!\perp E+W\).  If \(\varphi_E\) and \(\varphi_W\) are the characteristic functions, these two independence relations imply
\[
 \varphi_E(q+t)\varphi_W(t)
 =\varphi_E(q)\varphi_E(t)\varphi_W(t)
 \quad\text{for all }q,t\in\mathbb R.
\tag{38}
\]
Continuity and \(\varphi_W(0)=1\) permit cancellation for all sufficiently small \(|t|\).  Taking \(q=-t\) yields \(|\varphi_E(t)|=1\) in a neighborhood of zero.  For arbitrary \(u\), choose an integer \(n\) such that \(|u/n|\) lies in that neighborhood and iterate (38), with increment \(u/n\), to obtain \(|\varphi_E(u)|=1\).

Let \(E'\) be an independent copy of \(E\).  Then the characteristic function of \(E-E'\) is identically one.  In particular, equality in the triangle inequality gives \(e^{iq(E-E')}=1\) almost surely for every rational \(q\).  Intersecting these countably many probability-one events, and then taking \(q=1/n\) for every positive integer \(n\), forces \(E-E'\in\bigcap_{n\ge1}2\pi n\mathbb Z=\{0\}\) almost surely.  Thus \(E=E'\) almost surely.  Independence of the copies then implies that \(E\) is almost surely constant: for every real \(a\), independence and \(E=E'\) give \(\mathbb P(E\le a)\mathbb P(E>a)=0\), so the distribution function takes only the values zero and one and hence has a single jump from zero to one.  This contradicts the fact that the strictly positive bivariate density \(p_\varepsilon\) assigns positive probability to rectangles whose second coordinates lie in each of two disjoint open intervals.  Thus \(\Omega=\varnothing\) is impossible.

In the nonsingular regime, (32)--(34) violate innovation atomicity; the identically singular regime contradicts block-noise independence and density positivity already.  Therefore the two crossed representations cannot both lie in \(\mathfrak A(P)\), proving every assertion of the corrected theorem.
