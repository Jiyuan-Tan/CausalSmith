Write the slide deck for a 20-minute seminar talk on the econometrics working paper given at the end of this prompt.

GOAL. An econometrician outside this subfield, who has not read the paper and sees only the slides, leaves understanding the paper: what question it answers, what its main result says and under what conditions, and the mechanism — how the method works and why it succeeds where the standard approach does not. Twenty minutes buys more than the gist: the listener should be able to state the result and walk a colleague through the mechanism, short of the proof. An expert in the subfield can also find the exact objects — the model, the estimator or identifying formula, the main theorem. Clarity is the measure, and clarity comes from explaining, not from omitting: a result described only in words is vague, and a formula nobody explains is opaque. Show the mathematics and say in plain words what it means.

TERMS. The inputs at the end of this prompt include the formal-statement catalog: the paper's definitions, assumptions, and theorems. Each entry has an identifier (`obj_id`) and a statement body that has been checked against the machine-verified proof; "audited" below means exactly that text. An entry marked `slide-size` is short enough to read on a slide.

A talk is not a summary. The paper is written for a referee and the talk for a listener: do not compress the paper's sentences or walk through its sections. Re-explain the idea from the beginning, and leave out whatever the story below does not need.

THE STORY. Before writing any slide, settle these five points (they are your plan; do not print them):
1. The problem: what a practitioner wants to do, and what goes wrong.
2. The standard approach: what one would normally do, and exactly where it falls short.
3. The idea: the one observation that gets around that failure.
4. The result: what the idea delivers, and under what conditions.
5. The simplest concrete instance in which a listener can see the idea work.
The talk unfolds this story. Material in the paper that serves none of the five points stays out of the talk, apart from the optional "Further results" slide.

DECK STRUCTURE. The deck has two parts: the TALK (as many slides as the story needs — typically 12–20, never more than 25) and, after it, BACKUP slides. The talk follows the arc below. The arc is a storyline, not a form: merge or reorder beats when the paper's logic reads better, and never name the arc in prose or titles.
1. Title slide: the talk title (plainer than the paper's when that is clearer) and a one-sentence pitch in everyday words, with no label in front of it.
2. The question (1–2 slides): points 1–2, concretely. Name the closest prior work here. End on the question the talk answers.
3. The answer (1 slide): points 3–4 — the audience knows the destination before any setup.
4. The example (1 slide, before the general setup): point 5. When the paper contains an explicit special case, worked example, or closed-form instance, use it as a running example: show the idea working there, with the example's own formula. Later slides return to it — read at least one assumption and the main result in its terms. When the paper contains no such instance, omit this slide; never invent one.
5. Setup (1–2 slides): only the objects the main result needs. Show the model or the estimand as its formula; state each load-bearing assumption as what it rules out or guarantees.
6. Main result (1–2 slides): the theorem in talk form — the assumptions it needs, by the names the setup gave them; the result's headline; the formula that carries it (the identifying formula, the estimator, the bound, the limit — whichever the result is); and what it means for a practitioner, in contrast to the standard approach.
7. Mechanism (as many slides as its main steps need): how the method works and why the result holds, as a sequence of steps a listener could retell at a whiteboard — what the natural first attempt achieves, where it breaks, how each piece of the construction repairs it. Give every main step its reason, with the formula that makes it precise. Routine technical steps are stated without justification, and line-by-line derivations stay out. A step the paper takes from prior work is presented as imported — name the source and say what it delivers — and is not re-derived; attribute only what the paper itself cites, never a step of our own argument.
8. Close (1 slide): at most three points the listener should remember. Open directions the paper states go on their own slide titled "Open questions".
Other results in the paper (variants, comparisons, benchmarks, extensions) get at most one talk slide titled "Further results", one line each, and only those worth a listener's minute. The talk need not mention every result.
BACKUP (after the close; not counted in the talk's slides or its time; at most 4): for each main theorem — a result the abstract leads with — one slide titled `Appendix: <result name>` holding one plain line saying what the statement is, followed by that theorem's exact audited statement. The talk never depends on a backup slide.

WHAT GOES ON A SLIDE. Plain markdown: short bullets and short sentences a speaker could say aloud, plus the four elements below. For each point choose the form that makes it clearest — a sentence, a formula, a statement, or a figure. None is preferred in itself.
- Formula. Write a displayed formula as `\[…\]` on its own lines, with a blank line before and after. It must be a character-for-character copy of a formula in the formal-statement catalog, the paper sections below, or the appendix and proof files they list — you may copy a single display out of a long statement, but never compose, simplify, or recombine displayed math. `$$…$$` and top-level align/equation environments are not accepted. Wherever the paper has such a display, the talk shows: the model or estimand; the formula for what is identified or estimated; and the main result's formula. When the paper has no display for something that needs a formula, write it inline as `\(…\)` or say it in words — never as a composed display. Beside every displayed formula, say in plain words what it computes and what each symbol the listener has not yet met stands for. Inline math `\(…\)` in the paper's notation is free to use in prose, each symbol introduced in words at first use; a rate or scale written inline is copied exactly from the paper.
- Informal statement. `@informal <obj_id>: <headline>` restates a catalog result in one plain sentence of at most 280 characters; it renders labeled "informal" and linked to the audited statement. It is the usual way a result appears in the talk. The headline uses inline math only. It claims no more than the audited body: where the body proves a bound, say "at most" or "at least", never an exact rate, and keep every hypothesis that changes the claim's strength.
- Formal statement. The single line `@formal <obj_id>` injects the audited body exactly. In the talk, only a catalog entry marked `slide-size` may be injected, because a longer body cannot be read on a slide. On a backup slide an entry of any length may be injected. At most one per slide, and always with a plain line on the same slide saying what the statement delivers.
- Figure. `@figure <kebab-name>: <caption saying exactly what the diagram shows>` — at most 2, never required. The renderer draws only labeled boxes connected by arrows, so a figure suits structure such as a causal graph, a design layout, or a map of regimes; it cannot draw axes, curves, or data, and it shows no quantities the paper does not state. Include a figure only when it shows something the slide's sentences and formulas do not already say. The caption uses inline math only. Prose refers to a figure only on the slide that carries it.
Each `@informal`, `@formal`, and `@figure` line stands alone on its own line, never inside a bullet.

FORMAT. Output the deck only: no code fences, no preamble, no commentary. `---` on its own line separates slides. The first slide opens with `# <talk title>` in Title Case; every later slide opens with `## <slide title>` in sentence case.

LIMITS CHECKED AUTOMATICALLY (a deck that breaks one is rejected), in addition to the element rules above:
- The talk has no fewer than 8 and no more than 25 slides; backup slides come after all talk slides, are titled `Appendix: …`, and number at most 4.
- At most 6 top-level bullets per slide.
- Every `obj_id` exists in the catalog. Some theorem appears in the deck, and some theorem's exact statement appears via `@formal` (in the talk if `slide-size`, otherwise on a backup slide).
- The phrase "the paper" does not appear anywhere, titles included: write as the authors (we, our).

WRITING.
- Vocabulary. Assume a first-year econometrics PhD course and nothing more. Any other term — above all a name the paper coins — is either replaced by ordinary words or explained in one plain line before the deck relies on it; a term needed only once or twice is replaced. Expand every acronym at first use unless a general econometrics audience knows it (OLS, IV, ATE).
- Altitude. Drop indexing conventions, measurability and clipping caveats, constant bookkeeping, and generality that the slide's point does not need. Never use the vocabulary of the verification layer (representation or adapter layers, compilation specifications, Borel bookkeeping): state the econometric content only.
- Refer only to what is on the slides. Phrases such as "the displayed condition" or "the stated assumptions" must point at something the listener has actually been shown; otherwise state the condition itself.
- Standard ingredients (sample splitting, clipping, residualizing) get one plain sentence each. The explanation of why the design works is spent on the new idea: what the standard approach achieves, where and why it breaks, how the new piece repairs it.
- One point per slide. A slide that lists facts without making a point is rewritten around the point; a slide making two points is split.
- Where natural, end a slide on the question the next slide answers.
- Titles are short headings, never sentences. Default to the conventional seminar heading for the slide's role (Motivation, Research question, Key idea, Example, Model, Assumptions, Main result, Intuition, Proof sketch, Further results, Takeaways, Conclusion, Open questions); otherwise a noun phrase of at most about five words in sentence case.
- No self-praise ("novel", "punchline") and no narration of the talk itself ("we now turn to…").
- Prior work is cited as "A (Year)", "A and B (Year)", or "A et al. (Year)" for three or more authors, using only the verified bibliography below; work it does not cover is described without a name. Cite the two or three works the story needs.
- State what we establish and under which conditions; do not describe our contribution by listing what it leaves out. Limitations belong on an "Open questions" slide.
- Use only what the inputs contain: no invented statements, numbers, examples, or claims.
