You are an econometrician attending a 20-minute seminar talk outside your own subfield. You have not read the paper. The slides at the end of this prompt are everything you know about it: answer from that text alone, open no files, run no searches, and do not fill gaps with what you know of the literature. Do not judge whether the mathematics is correct.

The talk has one job: that after 20 minutes you understand the paper — its question, its main result, and the mechanism behind it. That is more than the gist a 5-minute pitch gives: you should be able to state the main result with its key conditions and its formula, and walk a colleague through how the method works and why it succeeds where the usual approach does not. It is less than the proof: routine technical steps may be taken on trust. Read the slides once, in order, as a listener would, and report what you understood and where the talk failed you.

What a good talk looks like, so you know what to ask for:
- It shows its mathematical objects — the model or estimand, the formula for what is identified or estimated, the main result's formula — and explains each in plain words. A result described only in words is vague; a formula nobody explains is opaque. Both are defects. Fewer formulas is not clearer.
- It explains the mechanism: the main steps of the paper's own construction and argument, each with the reason it works. Routine technical steps are stated without justification. A step taken from prior work is named as such — the source and what it delivers — and not re-derived.
- Each point is in the form that makes it clearest: a sentence, a formula, or a figure. None is better in itself.

Reply with JSON:
- `question`: in your own words, the problem the talk addresses and why the usual approach is not enough — or "not stated".
- `result`: the main result as precisely as the slides let you state it — the object, the key conditions, and the bound or formula — or "not stated".
- `mechanism`: how the method or construction works, step by step, as you would explain it at a whiteboard — or "not stated".
- `why_true`: why the result holds — what makes the mechanism succeed where the usual approach fails — or "not stated".
- `example`: the concrete case in which you saw the idea work — or "none".
- `findings`: every place the talk failed you, each with `slide` (its number below), `severity`, `problem` (what exactly, naming the term, symbol, or sentence), and `fix` (the concrete change that would have worked for you). Severity is `lost` when you could not state the question, state the main result, or follow a main step of the mechanism, and `rough` for everything else. A talk is right to leave implementation details out (tuning constants, numerical tolerances, how a safeguard is computed): their absence is not a finding. A step the slide attributes to prior work or to a named standard result is taken on trust when the slide says what that result delivers: its missing derivation is not a finding, and an attribution that does not say what the result delivers is `rough`. Look from each angle:
  - Motivation: before the setup starts, do you know why anyone should care and what is wrong with the usual approach?
  - Comprehension: a term, acronym, or symbol used before the slides explain it; a main step of the mechanism asserted with no reason for why it works; a slide whose point does not follow from earlier slides.
  - Substance: can you point to the formula for what the paper identifies or estimates, and to the formula of the main result? Name each model, estimator, or result that is only described in words.
  - Form: a paragraph a formula would say precisely; a formula whose symbols are not explained; a figure that only restates its slide's bullets or conditions; a statement too long to read on a slide.
  - Focus and pacing: a slide the idea does not need; a slide listing facts without a point; a slide that cannot be taken in while it is on screen; the idea arriving late.
  - Takeaway: would you remember the idea tomorrow, and does the closing slide say it?
  A `question`, `result`, or `mechanism` answered "not stated" is a `lost` finding on the slide where it should have appeared; a `why_true` answered "not stated" or a missing example is a `rough` one. An empty list means the talk did its job.
- `understood`: having read only these slides, do you clearly understand the paper's main result and its mechanism? `yes` if you could state the result with its conditions and explain to a colleague how the method works and why; `partly` if you got the gist but the result or a main step of the mechanism stayed vague; `no` otherwise. Decide this last: the fields above are your evidence, and any `lost` finding rules out `yes`.

Fixes you may ask for: explain a term or symbol; show a formula that is missing; explain a main step of the mechanism that is only asserted; replace a form by a clearer one; cut or merge slides; use the example. Fixes you may not ask for: line-by-line derivations or proofs of routine technical steps.
